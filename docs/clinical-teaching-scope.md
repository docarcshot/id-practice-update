# Clinical teaching scope and recommendation review

Updated November 4, 2026. Applies to the public lectures. This is an editorial standard, not legal advice or evidence of institutional approval.

## Purpose and audience

These modules teach clinical reasoning to healthcare professionals. They are not institutional policies, standing orders, prescribing instructions, or substitutes for patient-specific assessment. Retain precise, useful treatment information where supported, but identify the population, source, limitations, and decision factors.

## Three levels of treatment content

1. General principles and evidence: openly teach microbiology, diagnosis, source control, stewardship, comparative evidence, and uncertainty.
2. Regimen examples: preserve clinically useful agents, doses, routes, and durations with a stated syndrome, organism, patient population, source-control assumptions, renal/hepatic adjustment considerations, contraindications, and primary source. Distinguish guideline recommendations from local preferences, expert opinion, extrapolation, and trial-specific regimens. Do not present examples as default orders.
3. High-risk or weak-evidence decisions: explicitly identify specialist review and patient-specific assessment for CNS infection, endocarditis, PJI with retained hardware, resistant organisms, immunocompromise, pregnancy, severe organ dysfunction, complex drug interactions, investigational or off-label therapy, and uncommon infections.

## Review checks before declaring a lecture clinically verified

For each treatment statement, independently check current guideline status, source publication date, population, organism and resistance phenotype, exact dose and interval, route, renal/hepatic adjustment, treatment duration and its start point, oral transition criteria, source-control requirements, monitoring, interactions, contraindications, and follow-up. Distinguish clinical outcomes from pharmacokinetic modeling or observational associations. Verify primary sources and FDA labels where relevant; do not rely on secondary summaries alone.

Remove or qualify unconditional imperatives such as "always treat," "use X," or "stop after Y days" when patient selection and exclusions are necessary. Do not soften genuine contraindications or urgent safety instructions. Keep visible clinical review dates separate from editorial or navigation review dates. Flag uncertain statements for clinical review rather than silently replacing them.

## Current implementation and remaining work

The November 4, 2026 [catalog review](clinical-review-2026-11-04/README.md) records a whole-content reading of all 19 modules and a verified correction batch. ASB, inpatient fever, OPAT, penicillin allergy and hepatitis B/C passed verification of their published teaching scope. Fourteen modules retain partial source-audit status. The detailed records identify actual primary-source access, independent verification of changed claims, cross-module decisions and unfinished clinical questions.

Use this sentence on screen and in print: “This lecture is intended as a supplementary resource for clinicians and trainees to review evidence and discuss clinical cases.” On the catalog page use the plural: “These lectures are intended as supplementary resources for clinicians and trainees to review evidence and discuss clinical cases.” Keep editorial review status in the review record rather than on catalog cards, lecture headers, or teaching sheets. Maintain source citations, clinical limitations and required asset credits. Editing dates remain separate from clinical review dates; the notice does not signify institutional or faculty approval.

## Publication safeguards

Preserve all existing section IDs and cross-links. When editing a module, follow docs/lecture-link-protocol.md, update data/lecture-link-reviews.json with the new exact content hash after reviewing links, and run all README checks before publication. Avoid advancing the clinical review date for disclaimer or wording changes alone.
