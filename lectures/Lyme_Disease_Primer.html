Resident core

Lyme disease from exposure to follow-up

Distinguish a high-risk bite from established infection, choose tests by disease stage, and treat objective manifestations with a defined regimen. Separate persistent symptoms after treatment from reinfection or an untreated manifestation.

Companion lecture: Acute tick-borne infections. Use it for prominent febrile cytopenias, hemolysis, eschars, tularemia, or failure to improve on doxycycline.

Resident core

Where Lyme exposure is most likely

Lyme disease is most common in the Northeast, mid-Atlantic and upper Midwest, with smaller areas of transmission along the Pacific Coast. Residence alone is insufficient; obtain the location of outdoor exposure and travel.

Resident core

Bite-site lesions and systemic rashes

Lyme is caused primarily by B. burgdorferi in the U.S.; B. mayonii is uncommon. Relevant exposure is concentrated in the Northeast, mid-Atlantic, upper Midwest, and focal Pacific Coast areas. Ask where exposure occurred rather than relying on residence alone.

U.S. regional map and international exposure map. Northeast/mid-Atlantic, upper Midwest and focal Pacific Coast exposures have different background risks; location alone cannot diagnose or exclude Lyme.

A photograph cannot identify the pathogen. Erythema migrans expands over days and need not have central clearing. An immediate small itchy reaction that fades over 1-2 days favors local bite hypersensitivity. Size alone is not definitive: early erythema migrans may initially be small. Document the diameter and evolution.

For additional erythema migrans appearances across skin tones and bite-reaction mimics, use the CDC clinical photograph gallery. Individual third-party photographs there are linked rather than reproduced.

Sources [4], [5], [7].

STARI can resemble erythema migrans after a lone star tick bite. There is no validated diagnostic test and antibiotic benefit is uncertain. When Lyme cannot be distinguished in an overlap region, treat for Lyme. A necrotic eschar or prominent regional nodes should prompt the eschar/tularemia differential.

Sources [1], [7].

Resident core

Test according to the stage

Standard two-tier testing uses an enzyme immunoassay (EIA), followed by an immunoblot if the first assay is positive or equivocal. Modified two-tier testing uses two different EIAs in an FDA-cleared sequence. Interpret the completed algorithm, not a screening result or individual bands. A negative first tier generally stops that specimen’s algorithm; repeat a new sample later if early infection remains plausible. Early antibiotic treatment can prevent seroconversion.

Atypical lesion or suspected extracutaneous diseaseUse an FDA-cleared standard two-tier or modified two-tier algorithm. If tested early and negative, repeat when indicated; for an atypical rash, paired testing at least 2-3 weeks apart can help.
Symptoms lasting more than 30 daysDo not use isolated IgM to diagnose late Lyme. A negative validated IgG after months to years of untreated symptoms strongly argues against Lyme, with rare exceptions such as humoral immune deficiency.
Positive antibody after previous treatmentMay persist for years. It does not prove current infection or treatment failure; do not use antibody titers as a test of cure.
Potential cross-reactionRelapsing-fever Borrelia, syphilis, EBV, and rheumatoid arthritis can produce misleading results. Interpret the completed algorithm with exposure and clinical findings.

Resident core

Lumbar puncture and CSF interpretation

Consider LP for suspected meningitis, encephalitis, myelitis, or raised intracranial pressure. Isolated facial palsy or peripheral radiculoneuropathy does not automatically require LP. Fatigue or cognitive complaints alone do not justify CSF Lyme testing.

Start with serum two-tier antibodies. If LP is performed, send routine CSF studies and simultaneous CSF/serum for a validated antibody index. Active CNS disease usually causes lymphocytic pleocytosis. An elevated index may persist after cure; a normal index does not exclude early disease.

CSF Lyme PCR sensitivity was 17% in a meta-analysis of acute neuroborreliosis and 5% in one US meningitis study. These are study estimates, not universal assay characteristics. Routine CSF PCR is not recommended; a negative result cannot exclude infection.

LP also helps identify alternative infections. See the acute CNS evaluation and neurologic treatment regimens.

Source [7].

Adult erythema migrans regimenDuration
Doxycycline 100 mg PO twice daily10 days

Select according to allergy, pregnancy, tolerability, and possible coinfection. Amoxicillin or cefuroxime are usual options for uncomplicated erythema migrans in pregnancy; individualize doxycycline after discussing risks and benefits. Amoxicillin and cefuroxime do not treat Anaplasma or Ehrlichia. Azithromycin is second-line when the preferred agents cannot be used. Multiple erythema migrans lesions do not automatically require IV therapy; assess for neurologic or cardiac involvement.

Resident core

Lyme complications change the treatment plan

Obtain an ECG when cardiac symptoms or signs suggest carditis, such as syncope, palpitations, dyspnea, chest pain, or lightheadedness; routine ECG screening in asymptomatic Lyme disease is not recommended. Nonspecific ECG changes alone do not establish Lyme carditis.

Untreated infection can disseminate to skin, nerves, heart, or joints. Delayed treatment can leave neurologic injury or joint damage; conduction disease can become life-threatening. Persistent injury after eradication does not by itself establish persistent infection.

Manifestation / decisionTreatment and follow-up
Facial palsy or meningitis/radiculoneuritis without brain/spinal-cord parenchymal diseaseDoxycycline 100 mg PO twice daily OR ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily for 14-21 days; route depends on severity, absorption, and competing meningitis diagnoses. Isolated facial palsy usually receives oral treatment; protect the cornea when eye closure is impaired. Evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against adjunctive corticosteroids in confirmed Lyme facial palsy. For patients aged at least 16 years with acute facial palsy but no other objective clinical or serologic evidence of Lyme disease, do not delay corticosteroids beyond the usual 72-hour window while awaiting Lyme testing; follow facial-palsy guidance. AAN/ACR/IDSA recommendation XV [7].
Encephalitis or myelitis attributable to LymeUse IV therapy, usually ceftriaxone 2 g daily. Courses of 2-4 weeks have been used; optimal duration has not been studied. Distinguish parenchymal inflammation from nonspecific cognitive symptoms and individualize with specialist input.
Stable mild carditis, first-degree block with PR <300 msDoxycycline 100 mg PO twice daily, amoxicillin 500 mg PO three times daily, or cefuroxime axetil 500 mg PO twice daily for 14-21 days. Arrange close follow-up.
Symptomatic carditis, PR >=300 ms, second/third-degree block, or other severe cardiac involvementHospitalize with telemetry; ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily initially. Change to oral therapy after improvement to complete 14-21 total days. Temporary pacing may be needed. Do not await serology.
Initial Lyme arthritisObjective swelling of one/few large joints, commonly knee. Doxycycline 100 mg PO twice daily, amoxicillin 500 mg PO three times daily, or cefuroxime axetil 500 mg PO twice daily for 28 days. Consider aspiration for competing septic/crystal arthritis.
Mild residual swelling after first arthritis courseAssess adherence, alternate diagnoses, and trend. Observation or a second oral course may be reasonable; comparative evidence is insufficient. Resolution can lag behind treatment.
Minimal/no improvement with persistent substantial swelling after first oral courseCeftriaxone 2 g IV daily for 14-28 days, after reassessment.
Persistent synovitis after adequate oral and IV coursesConsider post-antibiotic Lyme arthritis; refer to rheumatology for anti-inflammatory/DMARD management. Do not continue serial antibiotic courses.

CSF pleocytosis alone does not require IV ceftriaxone. For established Lyme meningitis without parenchymal disease, oral doxycycline is an accepted option. Initial IV treatment may be needed for severe illness, unreliable oral intake, or empiric coverage of other CNS infections. Amoxicillin and oral cefuroxime are not recommended neurologic regimens. CNS treatment for other tick-borne infections.

Resident core

Fever or cytopenias that do not fit uncomplicated Lyme

Prominent leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, or hemolytic anemia should prompt consideration of another tick-borne infection. Persistent fever on doxycycline requires reassessment rather than simply extending Lyme therapy.

Doxycycline covers anaplasmosis but not babesiosis. Hemolysis should prompt a manual blood smear and Babesia PCR as indicated. Use the companion lecture for initial specimens, Babesia regimens, and persistent-fever reassessment.

Sources [2], [3], [7], [9].

For persistent febrile cytopenias or encephalitis, see Heartland, Bourbon, Powassan and Colorado tick fever testing. Memory aids, including Babesia treatment.

Babesia geography differs by species. Missouri-acquired divergens-like/MO-1 infection is documented, and a B. microti-only assay can miss non-microti disease. See the species-specific map and Missouri exception.

Resident core

Persistent symptoms and the label "chronic Lyme disease"

The label does not specify whether the patient has untreated infection, reinfection, post-infectious symptoms, residual organ injury, or another diagnosis. Document which of these is supported. Fatigue, diffuse pain, poor sleep, and cognitive difficulty can be disabling after treated Lyme disease; their mechanism is not established, and their presence does not prove viable Borrelia persists.

Clinical situationWhat is indicated
Documented untreated Lyme manifestationTreat with the recommended manifestation-specific regimen. Long symptom duration is not a reason to withhold a first appropriate course.
New erythema migrans after a new exposureEvaluate and treat reinfection. Previous Lyme does not confer reliable protection; serology may remain positive from the prior episode.
Objective recurrent arthritis, meningitis, neuropathy, or cardiac findingsReassess prior diagnosis, regimen, adherence, reinfection, and alternative causes. Additional antibiotics may be appropriate for a documented active manifestation; not simply for a positive antibody result.
Persistent fatigue, pain, or cognitive symptoms after appropriate treatment without objective evidence of active infectionNo routine additional antibiotics. Evaluate sleep, anemia, endocrine disease, medication effects, mood, neurologic disease, and other infections as indicated. Treat specific symptoms and follow function over time.
Positive Lyme antibody but no compatible syndromeNo antibiotics solely for the test result. Prior exposure, false positivity, and cross-reactivity are possible.
Nonspecific chronic symptoms with repeatedly negative appropriate late serology and no credible exposureLyme is unlikely. Pursue other explanations rather than using an empiric prolonged-antibiotic trial as a diagnostic test.

Post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome (PTLDS) is commonly defined for research by documented Lyme disease, recommended treatment with resolution or stabilization of objective findings, and functionally important fatigue, widespread pain, or cognitive complaints beginning within six months of diagnosis and persisting for at least six months after completing treatment, without a better explanation. These criteria do not prove a mechanism and are not a reason to defer symptom care until six months.

The evidence supports taking persistent symptoms seriously while declining prolonged antibiotics without an objective indication. A transient improvement on antibiotics does not establish ongoing infection. Do not use serial antibody titers, isolated immunoblot bands, or unvalidated urine assays or CD57 lymphocyte counts to decide whether infection persists.

Sources [7], [13], [14].

Resident core

After a bite and after treatment

Lyme postexposure prophylaxis applies to an identified high-risk Ixodes bite in a highly endemic area, attached at least 36 hours, with treatment possible within 72 hours of removal. Give doxycycline 200 mg PO once in adults (children 4.4 mg/kg once, maximum 200 mg). Confirm doxycycline is appropriate, including allergy assessment; pregnancy requires an individualized risk-benefit discussion. If criteria are uncertain or doxycycline cannot be used, observe with symptom instructions rather than substitute an unproven single dose of another antibiotic. This is not treatment for established erythema migrans and does not reliably prevent other tick-borne diseases. Routine prophylaxis for RMSF/ehrlichiosis/anaplasmosis is not recommended.

Remove ticks promptly with fine-tipped tweezers near the skin; do not burn or smother them. Use EPA-registered repellent and permethrin-treated clothing, inspect skin after exposure, and give return precautions for fever, expanding rash, eschar, dyspnea, syncope, or neurologic symptoms. Do not order Lyme serology or pathogen testing of the tick to decide treatment in an asymptomatic person.

After treatment, assess clinical recovery and new objective manifestations rather than repeating serology. Reinfection remains possible. Provide return precautions for syncope, palpitations, dyspnea, facial weakness, severe radicular pain, or joint swelling.

Sources [7], [10], [11], [12].

Resident core

Cases for discussion

Treated Lyme, fatigue and poor concentration nine months later, no synovitis or neurologic deficit, persistent IgG.

Reassess for other causes and provide symptom-focused care. IgG persistence is not treatment failure. Additional antibiotics are not indicated solely for these symptoms.

New swollen knee months after untreated Lyme-compatible illness, strong IgG response. Same decision?

No. Objective Lyme arthritis warrants a defined treatment course. Evaluate competing septic/crystal arthritis and use the arthritis pathway, not the PTLDS pathway.

Diagnostic performance and difficult interpretations

Test / questionEvidence and interpretation
Lyme two-tier sensitivity and specificityHistorical standard two-tier studies summarized by Moore et al. report approximately 30-40% sensitivity early, 70-100% with disseminated disease, and >95% specificity. These are stage- and assay-dependent estimates, not a single performance number for every modern modified two-tier assay.
Late Lyme arthritisSerum IgG is generally strongly positive; guideline-reviewed conventional two-tier sensitivity is about 95-100%. IgM alone or limited IgG reactivity should prompt reconsideration.
Suspected CNS LymeBegin with serum antibody testing. If CSF testing is indicated, obtain simultaneous serum/CSF for a validated antibody index rather than CSF antibody alone. Intrathecal antibody production may persist after cure; interpret with CSF inflammation and clinical findings. Routine blood/CSF PCR has poor sensitivity.
Synovial-fluid PCRCan support Lyme arthritis in a seropositive patient when uncertainty affects management. Positive DNA after therapy does not prove viable bacteria or justify indefinite retreatment.

What the prolonged-antibiotic trials actually show

Trial / populationIntervention and resultInterpretation
Klempner et al., NEJM 2001
Two trials, 129 previously treated patients, seropositive and seronegative groups		30 days IV ceftriaxone then 60 days oral doxycycline versus matching placebo. No advantage in the primary health-related quality-of-life outcomeApplies to persistent symptoms after documented treated Lyme, not untreated arthritis or meningitis.
Krupp et al., STOP-LD, Neurology 2003
55 patients with persistent severe fatigue		28 days IV ceftriaxone versus placebo. A fatigue outcome improved; cognition did not. Four treatment-associated events required hospitalization, three in placebo recipientsThe fatigue benefit was real within this trial, but the authors did not recommend additional IV ceftriaxone given the narrow benefit and treatment risks.
Fallon et al., Neurology 2008
37 patients with objective memory impairment and prior IV treatment		10 weeks IV ceftriaxone versus placebo. Cognitive improvement at week 12 was not sustained at week 24. Some secondary pain/function signals favored treatment; drug/line events occurred in 6/23 treated versus 1/14 placebo patientsSmall selected population; short-term or secondary signals do not establish a durable overall benefit or persistent infection.
Berende et al., PLEASE, NEJM 2016
281 randomized patients with persistent symptoms attributed to Lyme		All received two weeks ceftriaxone, then 12 weeks doxycycline, clarithromycin/hydroxychloroquine, or placebo. Longer oral therapy did not improve health-related quality of lifeTests the value of additional oral treatment after ceftriaxone; it cannot prove initial ceftriaxone was necessary or beneficial.

Across these trials, prolonged therapy has not shown a consistent durable benefit sufficient to outweigh harms. Potential harms of prolonged treatment include IV line infection/thrombosis, drug reactions, biliary complications, and antibiotic-associated colitis; these were not all measured uniformly in every trial. The trials do not prove that persistent symptoms are imaginary, identify one universal mechanism, or exclude every possible biologic subgroup. They do not support routine prolonged, rotating, or combination antibiotic treatment for nonspecific post-treatment symptoms.

Sources [7], [16], [17], [18], [19].
