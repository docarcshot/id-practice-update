Resident core

Acute tick-borne illness

Fever with cytopenias, an inoculation ulcer or eschar, and fever with hemolysis suggest different diagnoses and specimen choices. The resident core covers the initial decisions. The reference sections compare organisms, tests, and treatment evidence.

Companion lecture: Lyme disease. Use it for an expanding erythema migrans-like rash, bite prophylaxis, carditis, arthritis, or persistent symptoms after treatment.

Resident core

Start with the syndrome and the exposure

Admit or urgently escalate care for shock, hypoxemia, altered mental status, major organ dysfunction, or inability to take effective therapy. Clinical clues shift probability; no single absent rash, eschar, recalled bite, or early negative test rules out these infections. Surveillance case definitions are for reporting and should not delay bedside treatment.

Fever with a plausible exposure and severe headache, cytopenias, hepatitis, rash, or organ dysfunction can fit a rickettsial illness. CDC recommends prompt doxycycline when RMSF, ehrlichiosis, or anaplasmosis is suspected. An absent rash, an unrecognized bite, or negative acute testing should not delay effective treatment. Other indicated sepsis or meningitis coverage remains necessary during evaluation.

Ask where and when exposure occurred, including travel, yard work, pets, hunting, animal handling, and overnight stays in rustic cabins. Record illness day, prior antibiotics, immune suppression, splenic function, pregnancy, and transfusion history. Examine all skin and lymph nodes. Initial studies usually include CBC with differential, metabolic panel and hepatic tests; add hemolysis studies, smear/PCR, ECG, lumbar puncture, or tissue sampling according to the syndrome.

Resident core

When to perform LP and what to send

Perform LP when safe for suspected meningitis or encephalitis, including fever with meningismus, persistent unexplained altered mental status, seizures, or focal findings suggesting CNS inflammation. A tick bite or headache alone is not an indication. Assess bleeding risk, clinical stability, and whether neuroimaging is needed before LP. Do not delay empiric treatment while awaiting imaging or CSF.

Obtain opening pressure when appropriate, cell count with differential, protein, glucose with paired blood glucose, Gram stain and bacterial culture. Add HSV/VZV testing and other studies according to the syndrome, host, season, and travel; retain CSF for additional testing. Evaluate common CNS pathogens alongside tick-borne causes. Empiric doxycycline does not replace bacterial meningitis coverage or acyclovir when HSV encephalitis is suspected.

A negative standard 14-target BioFire meningitis/encephalitis panel does not exclude the tick-borne pathogens discussed here; they are not on its menu. Check the actual assay targets.

Resident core

Bite-site lesions and systemic rashes

Distinguish a localized inoculation lesion from a systemic rash. An eschar is a necrotic crust over an ulcer; not every scab is an infectious eschar. Examine the scalp, axillae, groin, and skin folds. For expanding erythema and bite-reaction mimics, see Lyme rash assessment and photographs.

Initial treatment and the exceptions

CDC recommends prompt doxycycline for suspected RMSF, ehrlichiosis, or anaplasmosis, including when an acute test is negative. IV therapy is appropriate in critical illness or when enteral delivery is unreliable.

General antibiotic selection, coverage and monitoring

Resident core

Tularemia with or without an ulcer

A visible ulcer with regional lymphadenopathy is suggestive, but glandular tularemia may have no ulcer. Ask about animal handling, deer flies, aerosols, and water as well as ticks. Pneumonic and typhoidal disease need not have a skin lesion.

Warn the laboratory before obtaining cultures. Select specimens by the involved site: ulcer material, lymph node aspirate or biopsy, blood, or respiratory material. Discuss PCR availability and transport requirements with microbiology. Early negative serology does not exclude infection; obtain a convalescent specimen.

CDC favors consideration of an initial aminoglycoside in severe disease. Evidence for adding a second active class in severe nonpregnant patients is limited. When effective treatment has been delayed more than two weeks, a bactericidal agent or combination therapy is favored over doxycycline alone. In pregnancy, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, or gentamicin are first-line options. For severe disease in pregnancy, CDC prefers gentamicin plus ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin. CDC 2025 recommendations [25]. Treatment options and duration.

Persistent fever or enlarging/fluctuant nodes warrants reassessment for suppuration, inadequate therapy, and another diagnosis. Consider aspiration or drainage when clinically indicated. A slowly resolving node or persistent antibody alone does not establish relapse or justify indefinite antibiotics.

Sources [3], [14], [21], [25].

Has the drug been delivered and absorbed?Missed doses, vomiting, poor enteral access, and interacting supplements or antacids can reduce exposure. IV therapy can address unreliable enteral delivery.

Follow-up, complications, and prevention

For a well patient after an Ixodes bite, use the separate Lyme prophylaxis criteria. The 36-hour threshold is not a safe exclusion rule for all tick-borne infections.

ConditionComplications and follow-up priorities
RMSF / ehrlichiosis / anaplasmosisUntreated disease may cause shock, respiratory failure, kidney injury, encephalitis, or coagulopathy. Severe RMSF can cause ischemic necrosis and permanent deficits. Ehrlichiosis may trigger HLH. Follow organ function and counts; persistent antibodies do not require retreatment.
TularemiaSuppurative nodes, pneumonia, systemic infection, and relapse after inadequate treatment. Persistent enlarging/fluctuant nodes need reassessment for aspiration/drainage and alternative diagnoses, not automatic indefinite antibiotics.
BabesiosisSevere hemolysis, renal/pulmonary compromise, splenic rupture, relapse in immunocompromised hosts. High parasitemia (>10%), severe hemolytic anemia, or severe pulmonary, renal, or hepatic compromise can warrant early discussion of red cell exchange with ID and transfusion medicine. The clinical course informs the decision. See the fellow evidence note.

Remove ticks promptly with fine-tipped tweezers near the skin; do not burn or smother them. Use EPA-registered repellent and permethrin-treated clothing, inspect skin after exposure, and give return precautions for fever, expanding rash, eschar, dyspnea, syncope, or neurologic symptoms. Do not order Lyme serology or pathogen testing of the tick to decide treatment in an asymptomatic person.

Resident core

Cases for discussion

Missouri, day 4 of fever, platelets 65,000, leukopenia, AST elevation, no rash, negative Ehrlichia antibody. Wait?

The day-4 antibody result does not exclude ehrlichiosis. Doxycycline is time-sensitive in this presentation. Whole-blood PCR is useful before treatment when collection can occur without delay, and convalescent serology can help confirm the diagnosis.

Ozark exposure, inoculation ulcer, bulky nodes, three weeks of illness. Five days of doxycycline for a presumed tick infection did not resolve it.

The ulcer and bulky nodes favor tularemia. The laboratory needs advance warning before culture. Lesion or node sampling and serology can support diagnosis. After this delay, CDC favors a bactericidal agent or combination therapy over doxycycline alone. Suppuration may require drainage.

Nantucket exposure, treated erythema migrans, fever persists on doxycycline with falling hemoglobin and rising bilirubin.

The hemolysis suggests babesiosis. Smear and PCR help establish infection, while parasitemia and organ injury determine urgency. In severe compatible illness, assessment for Babesia treatment is urgent while testing is pending.

Fellow detail / Reference

When doxycycline is sufficient, and when it is not

Route depends on illness severity and the reliability of enteral delivery. Several alternatives require renal adjustment. Doxycycline generally does not, but iron, calcium supplements, and antacids can impair absorption. Water and upright posture reduce pill esophagitis. Fluoroquinolone tendon, neurologic, QT, and aortic risks and gentamicin renal and ototoxicity risks affect drug selection.

SyndromeAgents and treatment durationSelection and exceptions
RMSF or other spotted feverDoxycycline. For RMSF, CDC describes a minimum course of 5-7 days and at least 3 days after fever resolves, with clinical improvement.Doxycycline is preferred, including in children. Beta-lactams do not substitute. Severe allergy requires specialist management, sometimes desensitization. Chloramphenicol has worse observed outcomes and does not cover ehrlichiosis/anaplasmosis; it is not a routine substitute.
EhrlichiosisDoxycycline for at least 5-7 days and until afebrile for 72 hours with clinical improvement.No equally reliable routine substitute. Complicated disease may require a longer course.
AnaplasmosisDoxycycline for 10-14 days.CDC uses this duration to account for possible concurrent early Lyme disease. Disseminated Lyme manifestations have separate treatment requirements. Rifampin is a limited-evidence option for selected mild anaplasmosis, including in pregnancy, only when the clinical assessment excludes RMSF and possible Lyme coinfection has been addressed. A negative early test alone does not exclude RMSF. CDC describes 7-10 days of rifampin in small pediatric series.

These preferred-regimen data primarily concern B. microti. Suspected or confirmed B. duncani or B. divergens/divergens-like infection calls for species confirmation and specialist input because B. microti treatment evidence may not apply. Published experience commonly uses IV clindamycin plus oral quinine; comparative evidence is sparse. Severe disease calls for prompt assessment of exchange-transfusion needs. MO-1 is not automatically equivalent to European B. divergens. IDSA species-specific treatment evidence [4].

In the 2026 STOP-BABESIOSIS study of 629 hospitalized adults with severe babesiosis, red cell exchange was associated with a lower combined rate of in-hospital death or 30-day readmission (3.6% vs 9.8%, adjusted OR 0.22, 95% CI 0.09-0.51). Residual confounding is possible. The study supports early multidisciplinary consideration in severe illness. It did not establish a parasitemia cutoff that predicts benefit. STOP-BABESIOSIS study.

For tularemia in pregnancy, CDC recommends ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, or gentamicin as first-line treatment, using the usual adult durations. For severe disease, CDC prefers gentamicin plus ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin. Selection depends on severity, organ function, and pregnancy-specific risks. CDC 2025 recommendations [25].

IV, intravenous.

CDC recommends prompt doxycycline for children with suspected RMSF, ehrlichiosis, or anaplasmosis. Short courses have not been shown to stain permanent teeth. Suspected RMSF during pregnancy also warrants prompt doxycycline while maternal and fetal risks are discussed. Rifampin does not treat RMSF or Lyme coinfection. Sulfonamides can worsen RMSF and should not be used to treat it.

Treatment with CNS involvement

The pathogen, neurologic manifestation, severity, and reliability of absorption determine the treatment route. Abnormal CSF alone does not distinguish these pathways.

Lyme meningitis / radiculoneuritisOral doxycycline or an IV option such as ceftriaxone are supported for 14-21 days. Tick-borne viruses and send-out testing

Doxycycline does not treat these viruses, but suspected viral disease does not justify delaying treatment for plausible RMSF/ehrlichiosis or other urgent bacterial disease. Supportive care includes monitoring for shock, respiratory failure, encephalitis, and other organ injury. There is no established pathogen-specific antiviral therapy for these four U.S. infections.

Heartland and Bourbon testing eligibility

CDC guidance uses acute febrile illness within the past three months, plus at least one exposure criterion and one clinical criterion. Exposure criteria are tick exposure in the three weeks before onset during spring through fall, or residence/travel in an area with previous virus evidence. Clinical criteria are unexplained WBC <4,500/microliter or platelets <150,000/microliter, or lack of response to appropriate treatment for suspected tick-borne disease. Treatment failure is not required when exposure and cytopenias already fit.

Before day 14, send serum for RT-PCR and antibody testing. From day 14 onward, prioritize antibody testing; later RT-PCR may still be useful in immunocompromised patients. Arrange a convalescent sample when required. CDC guidance does not accept specimens collected more than three months after symptom onset for this pathway.

Sources [16].

Where to send specimens

Contact hospital microbiology and the state/local health department before shipping. They should confirm current assay availability, specimen type/volume, storage, paired-sample timing, and the receiving laboratory. For CDC referrals, submit through the health department with the CDC 50.34 form, onset and collection dates, immune status, exposure location, and travel history. CDC pages currently caution that some assays may be unavailable; a published test menu is not a guarantee of acceptance.

In Missouri, contact DHSS communicable disease staff at 573-751-6113 during business hours. After hours and on weekends, the DHSS reporting number is 800-392-0272. The state manual listed both numbers on November 9, 2026. Local collection and send-out workflows vary.

Mayo Clinic Laboratories HRTVS is one example of a reference-laboratory Heartland serum PCR. The catalog entry checked November 9, 2026 specifies 0.5 mL serum, centrifuged and aliquoted into a sterile container within two hours. Mayo cautions that molecular testing may be negative after more than one week of symptoms. The test targets Heartland virus RNA. Paired serology can still be needed, and Bourbon testing requires a separate public health pathway. Local microbiology can confirm assay availability, handling, and routing.

Diagnostic performance and difficult interpretations

Test / questionEvidence and interpretation
Rickettsial PCR / IFA performanceNo universal sensitivity applies across organism, specimen, illness day, and antibiotic exposure. Tissue or eschar PCR may outperform blood for spotted fevers; whole blood is preferred for Ehrlichia/Anaplasma. An analytically specific PCR still has limited clinical sensitivity if the organism is absent from that specimen. A fourfold rise in paired spotted-fever IgG supports recent infection but usually does not identify the species.
Broad tick panelsRead targets and specimen requirements. A negative panel does not exclude pathogens not included, early antibody-negative disease, or infection suppressed by antibiotics. Multiple positive serologies may represent cross-reactivity rather than multiple active infections.

Order add-on testing through microbiology rather than assuming all PCR panels are interchangeable. The send-out testing section has a reference-laboratory Heartland serum RNA assay example and specimen guidance. For unusual viruses, coordinate testing and current availability with the state health department.
