Recurrent urinary tract infection

Clinical features, persistent sources, preventive options, and the limits of the evidence.

Resident core · Optional fellow section · Edited November 9, 2026

LecturesDiagnosisSource evaluationPreventionAntibiotic preventionFellow sectionReferences

This lecture is intended as a supplementary resource for clinicians and trainees to review evidence and discuss clinical cases.

Resident core

1. Three clinical questions

Does the patient have symptomatic infection? Is there an anatomic or functional reason for recurrence? Which preventive approach has a reasonable chance of helping without causing greater harm?

Recurrent uncomplicated cystitis generally means at least two separate symptomatic, culture-supported episodes within six months, or at least three in 12 months, with resolution between episodes. Persistent symptoms without resolution are not simply another recurrence.

The 2025 AUA guidance primarily addresses otherwise healthy women with recurrent localized cystitis. The IDSA 2025 complicated-UTI guideline addresses acute treatment, not prevention of recurrent cystitis. Patients with devices, neurogenic bladder, stones, obstruction or recurrent febrile infections warrant individualized evaluation; prophylaxis trials in otherwise healthy women may not apply.

IDSA 2025 classifies uncomplicated UTI as infection apparently confined to the bladder, in either sex. Fever, rigors, flank pain, CVA tenderness, bacteremia or other extension beyond the bladder suggest cUTI. Symptomatic infections with long-term catheters, stents or nephrostomy tubes are also included as cUTI. Male sex, diabetes, immunocompromise, BPH or cystocele alone do not automatically make an infection complicated. Risk factors affect treatment even when they do not determine the classification. Prostatic involvement is a separate concern in men.

Trautner et al., IDSA cUTI guideline 2025, classification.

4. Look for a persistent reservoir when the pattern warrants it

The dates, species, susceptibilities, symptom-free intervals, and antibiotic exposure help distinguish recurrence patterns. Prompt recurrence with the same organism shortly after apparently appropriate therapy raises concern for relapse, inadequate drug exposure, retained stone, obstruction, prostatic infection, or device biofilm. Repeated different isolates separated by wellness usually represent reinfection.

6. Suppressive antibiotics when needed

Drug-specific laboratory monitoring

Antibiotic prevention is a potential option for selected nonpregnant adult women with recurrent uncomplicated cystitis. Its role differs from acute treatment and from source control for an infected device or stone. Prior cultures, renal function, contraindications, and adverse effects affect agent selection. Several uses are off-label; nitrofurantoin macrocrystal labeling includes long-term suppression. A review after three to six months can assess benefit, toxicity, and the continuing indication; indefinite treatment is not established by the prevention trials.

Intermittent fosfomycin is another prophylaxis option discussed in EAU guidance. This use is off-label in the United States. A prophylactic strategy does not provide treatment for pyelonephritis or prostatitis. Fluoroquinolones should generally not be used for chronic uncomplicated-cystitis suppression because of serious adverse effects and resistance selection. Nitrofurantoin and single-dose oral fosfomycin are bladder regimens, not initial treatment for upper-tract or prostatic infection. IDSA 2026 AMR guidance allows selected multidose oral fosfomycin transition for ESBL-producing E. coli cUTI after initial therapy when other oral options are unavailable. Prolonged off-label fosfomycin for chronic bacterial prostatitis is a separate specialist decision supported mainly by observational evidence.

Renal function matters, but numerical cutoffs differ by drug and regulatory label. Methenamine has no universally validated US-label CrCl cutoff. A history of stones is a reason to evaluate composition and persistent infection, not an automatic contraindication to every prophylactic agent.

Questions before antibiotic prevention

The indication rests on the number of symptomatic episodes, culture support, and the burden to the patient. Renal function, interactions, pregnancy status, and prior adverse effects influence suitability. A defined goal, reassessment point, and plan for breakthrough symptoms help evaluate benefit. Surveillance cultures while asymptomatic generally do not guide uncomplicated recurrent-cystitis prevention.

Resident core

7. Acute treatment and follow-up

ESBL infection treatment by site · Penicillin allergy assessment

Acute treatment depends on the syndrome, prior cultures, current susceptibility, renal function, allergies, and recent antibiotic exposure. In nonpregnant adult women with uncomplicated cystitis, familiar options include five-day nitrofurantoin, three-day TMP-SMX when susceptible and appropriate, or single-dose fosfomycin for susceptible E. coli. These duration concepts come from cystitis guidance and do not extend to pregnancy, febrile UTI, or prostatic infection. In the 2018 randomized comparison, single-dose fosfomycin produced lower clinical resolution than five-day nitrofurantoin; the nitrofurantoin formulation and schedule differed from common US monohydrate/macrocrystal use. A positive culture alone does not establish a reason to escalate treatment.

Failure to begin improving within 48 hours, early recurrence, or worsening symptoms warrants reassessment. New fever, flank pain, or systemic illness requires prompt assessment for upper-tract infection or obstruction. Persistent symptoms after treatment can reflect ongoing infection or an alternative diagnosis; repeat culture helps distinguish them. Routine post-treatment cultures are generally unnecessary when symptoms resolve. An infected obstructed collecting system requires urgent drainage. NICE reassessment guidance and EAU follow-up and source-control guidance.

8. Cases for discussion

A 71-year-old with chronic frequency, no new urinary symptoms, and urine culture with ESBL E. coli. She has requested another antibiotic.

Discussion
Without a new compatible syndrome, the findings support ASB rather than recurrent infection. Atrophy, incontinence, retention, medications, and other bladder symptoms may explain the baseline complaints. Resistance does not create a treatment indication; pregnancy and mucosa-breaching urologic procedures are separate exceptions.

A 39-year-old has four culture-confirmed cystitis episodes in a year, each within 48 hours after intercourse. Normal exam, no hematuria, no febrile episodes.

Discussion
This otherwise uncomplicated pattern does not justify routine CT or cystoscopy. The temporal association makes postcoital prevention a reasonable discussion, alongside spermicide exposure, nonantibiotic options, and a planned patient-initiated treatment strategy.

A 63-year-old has recurrent Proteus bacteriuria and febrile episodes after antibiotics. She has a history of stones.

Discussion
The combination raises concern for stone or obstruction, particularly struvite/infected calculi. Imaging and urologic source control are central; repeated suppression without source evaluation may fail.

9. Acute complicated UTI and the 2025 IDSA guideline

IV-to-oral transition · Follow-up cultures in urinary-source bacteremia

These recommendations apply to acute cUTI, including pyelonephritis, not asymptomatic bacteriuria or routine recurrent-cystitis prevention.

DecisionEvidence and limitations
Empiric therapySeverity, recent cultures, antibiotic exposure, adverse-effect risks, and a relevant local antibiogram inform empiric selection. Prior resistance predicts mismatch. The guideline conditionally discourages fluoroquinolones after exposure to that class in the prior 12 months. Nonseptic cUTI does not automatically require a carbapenem or a novel agent.
Culture resultsSusceptibility results support a targeted effective regimen. Effectiveness requires adequate exposure in urine and relevant tissue, plus bloodstream exposure when bacteremia is present.
IV to oralClinical improvement, oral tolerance, and an effective oral option support transition after initial parenteral therapy. Bacteremic study participants were generally afebrile, stable, and had achieved source control. Catheterized patients, sepsis, immunocompromise, severe renal insufficiency, and structural abnormalities were commonly excluded.
DurationFor cUTI improving on effective therapy, the guideline conditionally supports 5-7 days of a fluoroquinolone or 7 days of a non-fluoroquinolone, counted from the first effective day. It also supports 7 rather than 14 days for improving urinary-source Gram-negative bacteremia.
Exceptions and uncertaintyThe evidence is less applicable with inadequate source control, urinary abscess, complete obstruction, severe sepsis, devices, CKD, or substantial immunocompromise. Febrile men with suspected acute bacterial prostatitis may need 10-14 days, with limited evidence; this does not define treatment of established chronic prostatitis. Evidence for short-course oral beta-lactams is limited and depends on adequate exposure.

Failure to improve promptly should trigger reassessment for obstruction, retained stone, abscess, inadequate drug exposure or another diagnosis rather than automatic antibiotic extension. The cUTI guideline does not provide a treatment algorithm for chronic urinary symptoms.

Trautner et al., IDSA 2025 cUTI guideline: empiric and definitive therapy, IV-to-oral transition, and duration.

Optional fellow extension

Advanced recurrent UTI questions

Relapse, devices and special populations

Repeated same-species growth does not prove a genetically identical strain. A short symptom-free interval after apparently adequate therapy changes the probability of a reservoir and raises questions about culture collection, adherence, drug penetration, upper tract involvement, and device/stone status. Prostatic infection in men and occult obstruction are possible explanations for febrile relapses.

Routine antibiotic prophylaxis is not supported in indwelling-catheter users or in intermittent-catheter users without recurrent symptomatic UTI. In selected adults using clean intermittent catheterization with recurrent UTI, AnTIC found fewer antibiotic-treated symptomatic episodes but increased resistance. AUA conditionally permits oral prophylaxis in neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction with intermittent catheterization and recurrent UTI after shared discussion of these tradeoffs. Bladder instillations in that population are an expert-opinion option with limited evidence.

Surveillance cultures in stable asymptomatic catheter users generally do not improve management. Obstruction, catheter necessity and technique, bladder pressures/emptying, and stones may explain symptomatic recurrence. Evidence from uncomplicated women is not directly applicable. In neurogenic bladder, a new change from baseline is particularly important to the diagnosis. Pregnancy has different screening, treatment and prophylaxis decisions.

Stones, urinary pH and preventive products

Struvite stones arise with urease-producing organisms; clearance of infected stone burden is part of source control. Other stones can still predispose to recurrent infection through obstruction or bacterial persistence. Stone analysis and metabolic evaluation can clarify the cause in recurrent stone formers. High-dose ascorbic acid may increase urinary oxalate. Cranberry products are not proven to prevent stones. Small metabolic studies report conflicting changes in urinary stone-risk factors, and some concentrated products increase urinary oxalate; their effect on clinical stone events remains uncertain.

Methenamine hydrolyzes to formaldehyde in acidic urine, with poor activity when urine is alkaline. Proteus may alkalinize urine, undermining efficacy. Methenamine is not a replacement for drainage of infected obstruction. US labeling contraindicates renal insufficiency without specifying a CrCl threshold and advises against combining methenamine with sulfonamides such as TMP-SMX because insoluble precipitate can form in urine. ALTAR excluded eGFR <10 mL/min/1.73 m2. That trial eligibility threshold does not override US labeling or establish safety across CKD stages.

Evidence limits and shared decisions

ALTAR was open-label and used symptomatic episodes treated with antibiotics as its principal outcome, without requiring culture confirmation. Its noninferiority margin allowed as many as one additional episode per person-year with methenamine. The absolute event rates clarify what the noninferiority finding means to a patient. The trial excluded correctable contributory urinary tract abnormalities and neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction; its protocol also excluded consistently recurrent Proteus infection. Long-term antibiotic prevention works during exposure but may select resistant flora.

Frequent symptom reports with repeatedly normal cultures raise a different question from culture-supported recurrence. A symptom-triggered testing plan may clarify the pattern; a positive result still requires interpretation in context. Repeated disagreement between symptoms and microbiology is a reason to reconsider the diagnosis.
