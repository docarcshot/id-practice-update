Recurrent urinary tract infection

Recognize infection, investigate the reason for recurrence, and choose prevention that the patient can tolerate.

Resident core

1. Three questions before prescribing anything

Does the patient have symptomatic infection? Is there an anatomic or functional reason for recurrence? Which preventive approach has a reasonable chance of helping without causing greater harm?

Recurrent uncomplicated cystitis generally means at least two separate symptomatic, culture-supported episodes within six months, or at least three in 12 months, with resolution between episodes. Persistent symptoms without resolution are not simply another recurrence.

The 2025 AUA guidance primarily addresses otherwise healthy women with recurrent localized cystitis. The IDSA 2025 complicated-UTI guideline addresses acute treatment, not prevention of recurrent cystitis. Patients with devices, neurogenic bladder, stones, obstruction or recurrent febrile infections warrant individualized evaluation; prophylaxis trials in otherwise healthy women may not apply.

IDSA 2025 classifies uncomplicated UTI as infection apparently confined to the bladder, in either sex. Fever, rigors, flank pain, CVA tenderness, bacteremia or other extension beyond the bladder suggest cUTI. Symptomatic infections with long-term catheters, stents or nephrostomy tubes are also included as cUTI. Male sex, diabetes, immunocompromise, BPH or cystocele alone do not automatically make an infection complicated. Evaluate risk factors separately and exclude prostatitis when relevant in men.

Trautner et al., IDSA cUTI guideline 2025, classification.

2. Symptoms matter more than a positive culture

When asymptomatic bacteriuria should be treated

PresentationInterpretation and action
New dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic discomfort, sometimes visible hematuriaCompatible with acute cystitis, especially when new relative to baseline. Obtain urinalysis and urine culture for each suspected recurrence before antibiotics when feasible. Interpret contamination and prior culture history.
Fever, rigors, flank pain, CVA tenderness, vomiting, systemic illnessEvaluate for pyelonephritis, obstruction, bacteremia, and other sources. Assess severity, renal function, and need for imaging or admission.
Positive culture but no attributable urinary symptomsAsymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB), usually do not treat regardless of pyuria, odor, cloudiness, organism, or colony count. Main exceptions are pregnancy and before endoscopic urologic procedures with anticipated mucosal trauma.
Chronic urgency, nocturia, incontinence, pelvic pain without a convincing new changeDo not equate chronic baseline symptoms or pyuria with active UTI. Evaluate noninfectious causes.
Delirium or fall alone in an older adult with bacteriuria, no focal urinary or systemic signsSearch for other causes and observe. Positive culture alone does not establish infection. In hemodynamic instability, evaluate and treat suspected sepsis without assuming a urinary source.
Catheter or spinal cord injuryChronic bacteriuria is expected. New fever, malaise, autonomic dysreflexia, spasticity, pelvic/flank discomfort, catheter obstruction, or other new compatible findings may prompt evaluation. Symptoms can be atypical; do not screen or treat stable bacteriuria.

Pyuria supports inflammation but is not proof of UTI. Absence of pyuria in a patient with typical baseline immune function should prompt reconsideration. A positive culture without a compatible syndrome may be colonization; a negative culture during symptoms warrants reassessment, particularly if collected before antibiotics.

4. Look for a persistent reservoir when the pattern warrants it

Compare dates, species, susceptibilities, symptom-free intervals, and antibiotic exposure. Prompt recurrence with the same organism shortly after apparently appropriate therapy raises concern for relapse, inadequate drug exposure, retained stone, obstruction, prostatic infection, or device biofilm. Repeated different isolates separated by wellness usually represent reinfection.

ClueTargeted workupPossible intervention
Proteus, other urease-producing organisms, recurrent same isolate, stone history, hematuriaRenal/bladder ultrasound or noncontrast CT when stone suspicion is high; assess stone burden and obstructionUrology for infected stone or staghorn calculus management. Routine cystoscopy and upper-tract imaging are not recommended in the otherwise uncomplicated index patient. Order them when atypical features, poor response, suspected relapse, hematuria, stones, obstruction, or renal involvement change the pretest probability.

6. Suppressive antibiotics when needed

Drug-specific laboratory monitoring

These oral prophylaxis examples apply to nonpregnant adult women with recurrent uncomplicated cystitis and adequate renal function. They do not treat an acute episode, pyelonephritis, prostatitis, or an infected device or stone. Confirm recent susceptible isolates, drug-specific contraindications, renal dosing, and local availability. Several regimens are off-label; nitrofurantoin macrocrystal labeling includes long-term suppression. Reassess efficacy, toxicities and indication after about three to six months rather than prescribing indefinitely by default.

DrugContinuous dosePostcoital doseMajor limitation
Nitrofurantoin macrocrystals50–100 mg PO nightly50–100 mg PO once after intercourseFor bladder only. The linked US label contraindicates CrCl <60 mL/min; EAU uses eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m² for cystitis treatment. These are different standards and renal estimates; short-course guidance does not establish safety of prolonged suppression in CKD. Avoid after prior nitrofurantoin-related hepatic injury. Pulmonary injury, hepatitis, neuropathy, and G6PD-associated hemolysis require symptom review and periodic renal/liver monitoring; stop and assess suspected toxicity.

Cephalexin125–250 mg PO nightly250 mg PO onceGI effects, candidiasis, C. difficile and selection of resistant flora. Adjust dose or interval and monitor renal function in CKD. Review cephalosporin hypersensitivity and metformin exposure; probenecid coadministration is not recommended by the US label.

Fosfomycin 3 g orally every 10 days is an option listed in AUA guidance for continuous prophylaxis. It is off-label in the United States; select it according to prior cultures, tolerability, and stewardship considerations. This prophylactic schedule is not treatment for pyelonephritis or prostatitis. Fluoroquinolones should generally not be used for chronic uncomplicated-cystitis suppression because of serious adverse effects and resistance selection. Nitrofurantoin and single-dose oral fosfomycin are bladder regimens, not initial treatment for upper-tract or prostatic infection. IDSA 2026 AMR guidance allows selected multidose oral fosfomycin transition for ESBL-producing E. coli cUTI after initial therapy when other oral options are unavailable. Prolonged off-label fosfomycin for chronic bacterial prostatitis is a separate specialist decision supported mainly by observational evidence.

Renal function matters, but numerical cutoffs differ by drug and regulatory label. Methenamine has no universally validated US-label CrCl cutoff. A history of stones is a reason to evaluate composition and persistent infection, not an automatic contraindication to every prophylactic agent.

Before starting suppression

Document true symptomatic episode count, culture results, baseline renal function, medication interactions, pregnancy status where relevant, and prior adverse effects. Agree on the target (e.g., fewer symptomatic culture-confirmed episodes), duration, breakthrough-culture plan, and stopping point. Do not perform surveillance cultures while asymptomatic.

7. Acute treatment and follow-up

ESBL infection treatment by site · Penicillin allergy assessment

Treat each symptomatic recurrence according to syndrome, prior cultures, current susceptibility, renal function, allergies, and recent drug exposure. For nonpregnant adult women with uncomplicated cystitis and adequate renal function, guideline examples are nitrofurantoin monohydrate/macrocrystals 100 mg PO twice daily for five days, TMP-SMX 160/800 mg PO twice daily for three days when susceptible and appropriate, or fosfomycin trometamol 3 g PO once for susceptible E. coli. In the 2018 randomized comparison, single-dose fosfomycin produced lower clinical resolution than five-day nitrofurantoin. That trial used nitrofurantoin macrocrystals 100 mg three times daily, a different regimen from the guideline example above. These short bladder regimens should not be extrapolated to pregnancy, febrile UTI, or suspected prostatic infection. A positive urine culture alone is not an indication to escalate. Avoid routine post-treatment cultures if symptoms resolve.

Reassess if symptoms fail to begin improving within 48 hours, recur soon after therapy, or worsen at any time. New fever, flank pain or systemic illness warrants prompt assessment for upper-tract infection or obstruction. For uncomplicated cystitis, AUA expects symptom resolution within 3–7 days; persistent symptoms after treatment warrant repeat culture before another antibiotic and consideration of alternative diagnoses. Treat infected obstruction as urgent.

8. Cases for discussion

A 71-year-old with chronic frequency, no new urinary symptoms, and urine culture with ESBL E. coli. She has requested another antibiotic.

Discussion
Do not treat ASB. Determine what, if anything, changed from baseline. Evaluate atrophy, incontinence, retention, medications, and bladder symptoms. A resistant isolate does not convert colonization into infection.

A 39-year-old has four culture-confirmed cystitis episodes in a year, each within 48 hours after intercourse. Normal exam, no hematuria, no febrile episodes.

Discussion
No routine CT or cystoscopy. Avoid spermicide, discuss postcoital prophylaxis based on susceptibility versus patient-initiated treatment, and review whether nonantibiotic measures are preferred.

A 63-year-old has recurrent Proteus bacteriuria and febrile episodes after antibiotics. She has a history of stones.

Discussion
Image for stone and obstruction, particularly struvite/infected calculi. Urology source control is central; repeated suppressive antibiotics without source evaluation may fail.

9. Acute complicated UTI and the 2025 IDSA guideline

IV-to-oral transition · Follow-up cultures in urinary-source bacteremia

These recommendations apply to acute cUTI, including pyelonephritis, not asymptomatic bacteriuria or routine recurrent-cystitis prevention.

DecisionRecommended approach and limitation
Empiric therapyAssess severity and sepsis first, then recent urine cultures and resistance, prior antibiotic exposure, patient-specific adverse effects, and the local antibiogram when septic. Avoid agents with recent documented resistance. The guideline suggests avoiding fluoroquinolones if exposed to that class in the prior 12 months. Nonseptic cUTI does not automatically warrant a carbapenem or a novel agent.
Culture resultsDe-escalate to the narrowest effective regimen once susceptibilities are available. An effective cUTI antibiotic must reach the urine and relevant tissue; bloodstream exposure also matters with bacteremia.
IV to oralSwitch if clinically improving, able to take oral medication, and an active oral agent is available. In selected Gram-negative bacteremia, this is reasonable after stability and source control. Trials underrepresented severe sepsis, catheterized patients, immunocompromise, severe renal insufficiency and structural abnormalities.
DurationFor improving cUTI, use 5–7 days of an effective fluoroquinolone or 7 days of an effective non-fluoroquinolone, counted from the first day of effective therapy. In improving cUTI with Gram-negative bacteremia, 7 days is suggested over 14 days.
Exceptions and uncertaintyIndividualize with inadequate source control, urinary abscess, complete obstruction, severe sepsis, indwelling devices, CKD or substantial immunocompromise; many trials excluded these patients. Suspected acute bacterial prostatitis in febrile men may warrant 10–14 days. Evidence for short-course oral beta-lactams in cUTI is more limited and adequate dosing is important.

Failure to improve promptly should trigger reassessment for obstruction, retained stone, abscess, inadequate drug exposure or another diagnosis rather than automatic antibiotic extension. The cUTI guideline does not provide a treatment algorithm for chronic urinary symptoms.

Trautner et al., IDSA 2025 cUTI guideline: empiric and definitive therapy, IV-to-oral transition, and duration.

Optional fellow extension

Advanced recurrent UTI questions

Relapse, devices and special populations

Repeated same-species growth does not prove a genetically identical strain. A short symptom-free interval after apparently adequate therapy changes the probability of a reservoir and should prompt review of culture collection, adherence, drug penetration, upper tract involvement, and device/stone status. Consider prostatic infection in men and occult obstruction with febrile relapses.

Avoid routine prophylaxis in indwelling-catheter users and in intermittent-catheter users without recurrent symptomatic UTI. In selected adults using clean intermittent catheterization with recurrent UTI, AnTIC found fewer antibiotic-treated symptomatic episodes but increased resistance. AUA conditionally permits oral prophylaxis in neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction with intermittent catheterization and recurrent UTI after shared discussion of these tradeoffs. Bladder instillations in that population are an expert-opinion option with limited evidence.

Do not perform surveillance cultures in otherwise stable asymptomatic catheter users. Assess obstruction, catheter necessity and technique, bladder pressures/emptying, and stone burden. Evidence from uncomplicated women is not directly applicable. In neurogenic bladder, distinguish baseline symptoms from true new changes and involve the relevant specialists. Pregnancy has different screening, treatment and prophylaxis decisions.

Stones, urinary pH and preventive products

Struvite stones arise with urease-producing organisms; remove infected stone burden when feasible. Other stones can still predispose to recurrent infection through obstruction or bacterial persistence. Consider stone analysis and metabolic evaluation for recurrent stone formers. High-dose ascorbic acid may increase urinary oxalate. Cranberry products are not proven to prevent stones. Small metabolic studies report conflicting changes in urinary stone-risk factors, and some concentrated products increase urinary oxalate; their effect on clinical stone events remains uncertain.

Methenamine hydrolyzes to formaldehyde in acidic urine, with poor activity when urine is alkaline. Proteus may alkalinize urine, undermining efficacy. Methenamine is not a replacement for drainage of infected obstruction. US labeling contraindicates renal insufficiency without specifying a CrCl threshold; do not present a fixed cutoff as a product-label fact. ALTAR excluded eGFR <10 mL/min/1.73 m². That trial eligibility threshold does not override US labeling or establish safety across CKD stages.

Novel acute antibiotics do not solve recurrent bacteriuria, and they should not be prescribed as prophylaxis without evidence. Review current FDA labeling when considering a recently approved agent.

Evidence limits and shared decisions

ALTAR was open-label and used symptomatic episodes treated with antibiotics as its principal outcome, without requiring culture confirmation. Its noninferiority margin allowed as many as one additional episode per person-year with methenamine. Patients need the absolute event rates, not just the word “noninferior.” The trial excluded correctable contributory urinary tract abnormalities and neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction; its protocol also excluded consistently recurrent Proteus infection. Long-term antibiotic prevention works during exposure but may select resistant flora.

For the patient with frequent symptom reports and intermittent normal cultures, offer symptom-triggered urine testing with a documented threshold for treatment. A standing culture order can be reasonable; automatic antibiotics after a positive result are not. Agree to revisit the diagnosis if symptoms and microbiology repeatedly diverge.
