Resident core · 3 min

1. Indications for testing and treatment

Test only when a positive result would lead to treatment. Once active H. pylori infection is established, eradication is recommended, even when symptoms are absent. Do not use serology to diagnose active infection or document cure.

Test and treatContext
Current or previous peptic ulcer diseaseIncluding patients using NSAIDs or aspirin. Eradication reduces ulcer recurrence; some patients still require gastroprotection.
Gastric MALT lymphoma, gastric atrophy or intestinal metaplasia, early gastric cancer resectionEradication is indicated but does not remove the need for appropriate GI surveillance or oncologic care.
Uninvestigated dyspepsiaNoninvasive test-and-treat is appropriate in selected adults younger than 60 without features that justify endoscopy; apply individualized cancer risk assessment.
Unexplained iron deficiency anemia or ITPConsider testing after appropriate evaluation for other causes.
Higher gastric cancer risk or adult household contact of an infected personACG 2024 expands testing to selected high-risk groups, including first-degree family history of gastric cancer and adult household contacts.

Routine universal screening of low-risk asymptomatic adults is not established. Do not postpone an indicated diagnostic endoscopy in favor of an isolated stool or breath test when alarm findings or cancer risk warrants biopsy.

Deferral is reasonable during an acute competing illness, pregnancy when treatment can safely wait, or when severe interactions, intolerance, or inability to adhere make immediate eradication impractical. Document why and arrange follow-up. Permanent observation of confirmed infection is generally not recommended.
Deferring treatment or discontinuing further eradication attempts

Confirmed active infection: The ACG 2024 guideline recommends eradication for all patients with confirmed H. pylori infection. Lack of symptoms alone is not an indication for observation.

Active infection not established: An isolated positive H. pylori IgG, particularly after previous therapy, does not establish current infection. Confirm with urea breath testing, stool antigen testing, or appropriate biopsy-based testing before prescribing antibiotics.

Temporary deferral: Eradication may be postponed when an acute competing illness, pregnancy without an urgent indication, significant drug interactions, or inability to complete treatment makes immediate therapy inappropriate. Reassess after the limiting condition resolves.

Repeated treatment failure: The AGA 2021 Clinical Practice Update (Best Practice Advice 9) recommends individualized discussion of additional eradication attempts, balancing benefits against adverse effects and repeated antibiotic exposure. Frailty, limited life expectancy, major intolerance, and absence of peptic ulcer disease or identifiable gastric cancer risk factors may favor withholding another empiric regimen. This is an expert-practice recommendation for refractory infection, not a routine alternative to initial treatment. Consider susceptibility-guided treatment before abandoning further attempts when feasible.

References: ACG guideline 2024; AGA Clinical Practice Update 2021, Best Practice Advice 9 and 10.

Risks of untreated infection

Infection commonly persists for decades and causes chronic active gastritis. Lifetime peptic ulcer risk among infected patients is often estimated near 10–20%, varying by population; only a minority develop gastric cancer (often cited around 1–3%, strongly dependent on regional and individual risk). H. pylori is a class I gastric carcinogen and a cause of gastric MALT lymphoma. Eradication reduces, but does not abolish, subsequent gastric cancer risk, especially once premalignant mucosal changes are present. Avoid applying population averages to an individual patient as a precise prediction.

2. Diagnostic testing and limitations

Use urea breath testing (UBT) or a validated monoclonal stool antigen test for most patients who do not need endoscopy. Gastric biopsies are appropriate when endoscopy is clinically indicated. Stool PCR may also provide clarithromycin resistance prediction.

Before UBT, stool antigen, or stool PCR: stop PPIs and vonoprazan for 14 days; avoid systemic antibiotics and bismuth for at least four weeks. Check the performing laboratory's additional instructions. H2 blockers or antacids can bridge acid symptoms, although some UBT laboratories request a short H2-blocker hold.

A positive appropriately performed test for active infection generally establishes the diagnosis. The major medication-related problem is false negatives, not false positives. Rare UBT false positives can reflect urease-producing non-H. pylori flora or assay-specific circumstances. Discordant tests require review of timing, medications, assay, and sampling before labeling reinfection.

3. Initial treatment

Assessment of a reported penicillin allergy before regimen selection

For treatment-naive adults with unknown susceptibility, the 2024 ACG guideline recommends optimized bismuth quadruple therapy for 14 days. Omeprazole-amoxicillin-rifabutin (Talicia) and vonoprazan-amoxicillin (Voquezna Dual Pak) are alternatives for patients without penicillin allergy.

Rifabutin formulation: Talicia delivers rifabutin 50 mg with each four-capsule dose. Conventional U.S. rifabutin capsules contain 150 mg; one conventional capsule per dose would not reproduce the Talicia regimen. If Talicia is unavailable, the ACG regimen table lists a 14-day separate-drug option of omeprazole 40 mg TID, amoxicillin 1 g TID, and rifabutin 150 mg BID. This is a different rifabutin schedule, not a dose-equivalent substitution. Verify the complete prescription, organ function, and interactions with pharmacy.

Pylera contains bismuth subcitrate potassium, metronidazole, and tetracycline, with a separately prescribed PPI. Its U.S. label describes a 10-day course; that is not identical in dose or duration to guideline-optimized 14-day bismuth quadruple therapy. See the commercial formulations reference for capsule counts.

Clarithromycin combinations, including Voquezna Triple Pak, should only be used when clarithromycin susceptibility is demonstrated. Levofloxacin combinations are susceptibility-directed salvage options. Both appear in the fellow section rather than the empiric initial-treatment table.

Assess adherence, drug interactions, renal function, and the feasibility of multiple daily doses before choosing a regimen.

Prior macrolide or fluoroquinolone exposure increases concern for resistance even when prescribed for an unrelated indication. Lack of exposure does not establish susceptibility.
Resident core · 3 min

4. Test of cure

Perform UBT or laboratory-based stool antigen testing at least four weeks after the last antibiotic dose; hold PPIs and vonoprazan two weeks, and antibiotics/bismuth four weeks before testing. Biopsy-based testing can be used if EGD is otherwise necessary. Symptom improvement is not proof of eradication, and serology cannot confirm cure.

Positive test of cure after an adequately timed, properly performed test generally indicates persistent infection. When the last pre-treatment eradication was never documented, call this persistent infection or recrudescence rather than proven reinfection.

With a negative appropriately timed test, persistent symptoms warrant evaluation for alternative causes rather than empiric repeat eradication therapy.

5. Management after treatment failure

Before prescribing salvage therapy, reconstruct each prior regimen (actual antibiotic, dose, frequency, days completed), dates, missed doses, PPI/PCAB use, previous macrolide or fluoroquinolone exposure, and exact timing of each diagnostic test.

Abbreviations. BID, twice daily; BQT, bismuth quadruple therapy; EGD, esophagogastroduodenoscopy; PPI, proton pump inhibitor; QID, four times daily; TID, three times daily.

Clarithromycin resistance commonly follows 23S rRNA mutations and predicts poor results with clarithromycin combinations. Fluoroquinolone resistance commonly follows gyrA alterations. Metronidazole resistance is more complex; higher-dose metronidazole with bismuth and adequate acid suppression can sometimes overcome in vitro resistance. Amoxicillin, tetracycline, and rifabutin resistance are generally less prevalent, but susceptibility patterns vary geographically.

6. Molecular resistance testing and culture

General limitations of resistance-gene and susceptibility results

Two different molecular options are available. Mayo Clinic Laboratories HPFRP (stool) and HPRP (tissue) detect H. pylori and selected 23S rRNA clarithromycin resistance mutations. They do not constitute broad antibiotic susceptibility panels. American Molecular Laboratories (AML) offers a broader next-generation sequencing panel, PyloriDx/PyloriAR (also described as AmHPR), for H. pylori detection and prediction of resistance to six agents. Molecular assays predict resistance from detected variants; they do not measure minimum inhibitory concentrations or prove susceptibility to agents without a detected resistance marker.

Prediction is better characterized for clarithromycin and levofloxacin than for metronidazole, for which genotype-to-phenotype concordance remains problematic. Evidence for prediction of amoxicillin, tetracycline, and rifabutin resistance is limited by the low prevalence of resistant isolates. Interpret each reported marker in the context of prior treatment and local laboratory validation.

AML PyloriDx/PyloriAR send-out and requisition

AML H. pylori detection and six-drug resistance NGS panel accepts formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue, fresh gastric tissue, or stool according to the laboratory's published service description. This may permit testing archived gastric pathology tissue without repeat endoscopy when an adequate block is available. Stool offers a noninvasive option; check the specific assay's treatment washout and handling instructions with the receiving lab.

AML forms and H. pylori test requisition. Request “PyloriDx/PyloriAR H. pylori detection and antibiotic resistance NGS panel” (AmHPR; CPT 0008U as listed in published billing references). Coordinate pathology release of an FFPE block or unstained sections with the institution's send-out laboratory. Verify current specimen quantities, collection containers, shipping, payer coverage, and whether the order requires microbiology/pathology approval.

Suggested request: “Persistent H. pylori after eradication therapy. Please send existing H. pylori-positive gastric biopsy FFPE tissue, if adequate, for AML PyloriDx/PyloriAR molecular resistance panel (23S rRNA, gyrA, rdxA, pbp1, 16S rRNA, rpoB). If tissue is unavailable, coordinate a stool specimen with the reference laboratory.”

For broad phenotypic testing, coordinate EGD and a dedicated fresh biopsy in the receiving laboratory's transport conditions. A routine formalin-fixed specimen for histopathology will not produce a bacterial culture. A negative culture following therapy is common enough that an unsuccessful recovery must not be mistaken for susceptibility.

Mayo clarithromycin PCR and culture send-outs

HELIS, H. pylori culture with antimicrobial susceptibilities: fresh gastric/duodenal biopsy or acceptable gastric sampling in sterile container, tissue moistened with sterile saline; refrigerate, deliver to testing lab within 48 hours; contact the reference laboratory before procedure. Culture panel includes amoxicillin, clarithromycin, levofloxacin, metronidazole, rifampin, tetracycline. Rifampin testing is not identical to a directly measured rifabutin MIC.

HPFRP, stool PCR with clarithromycin resistance prediction, and HPRP, tissue PCR with clarithromycin resistance prediction, are useful if corresponding specimens can be obtained. Verify current catalog specimen, storage, and shipping requirements with the local send-out lab before ordering. See Mayo diagnostic algorithm (December 2025).

Suggested GI order note: “Persistent H. pylori despite prior eradication regimens. Please obtain dedicated fresh gastric biopsy for H. pylori culture with antimicrobial susceptibility testing in addition to routine pathology. Coordinate specimen transport with microbiology/send-out laboratory before EGD; do not place the culture specimen in formalin.”

Adverse effects and medication counseling

Bismuth can darken stool or tongue and may be unsuitable with salicylate hypersensitivity (subsalicylate formulation). Tetracycline interacts with polyvalent cations and is avoided in pregnancy; separate from calcium, iron and antacids as directed. Metronidazole causes nausea, metallic taste and neuropathy with prolonged exposure; assess warfarin interaction. Rifabutin can cause cytopenias, uveitis, discoloration of secretions, CYP interactions and resistance concerns when used for other mycobacterial indications. Amoxicillin requires allergy and renal assessment. Clarithromycin has QT, CYP3A, and drug-interaction concerns. Levofloxacin carries tendon, neuropathy, CNS, dysglycemia, aortic aneurysm risk and other FDA warnings; renal adjustment is required. Vonoprazan has drug interactions and renal/hepatic labeling considerations.

Clinical cases

Case 1. A patient took 14 days of BQT. A stool antigen assay is negative 8 days after finishing treatment while the patient is still taking pantoprazole. Answer: uninterpretable for cure; repeat after appropriate antibiotic/bismuth and acid suppression washout.

Case 2. A patient failed PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin, then reports taking bismuth + tetracycline + metronidazole + PPI (“quadruple therapy”) but only received 7 days and missed many afternoon doses. Answer: first verify whether truly optimized BQT was completed; do not assume multidrug resistance from regimen names alone.

Case 3. Two fully documented regimens failed and stool PCR predicts clarithromycin resistance. Answer: do not use clarithromycin; consider susceptibility-directed management, including dedicated EGD biopsy culture if needed, after reviewing adherence and prior drug exposure.
