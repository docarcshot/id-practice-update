What this primer is for

Most antibiotic errors on the wards are not failures to remember a mechanism. They come from treating a diagnosis that is not established, choosing a drug without accounting for the site or likely resistance, missing source control, or continuing the initial regimen after better information arrives.

This module is meant to help with those decisions. It is not a formulary and it is not a substitute for pharmacy, the local antibiogram, current labeling, or an organism-specific guideline.

By the end, you should be able to

  1. Build an empiric regimen from the syndrome, severity, likely pathogens, prior microbiology, and local resistance data.
  2. Compare the practical coverage gaps of commonly used inpatient antibiotics and recognize the main niches of newer agents used for resistant gram-negative infections.
  3. Recognize when dose, route, tissue penetration, toxicity, monitoring needs, or source control makes an apparently active drug a poor choice.
  4. Narrow, stop, or transition therapy when the clinical and microbiologic data permit.
  5. Explain what the major antibiotic trials did and did not establish.
Before this is used as a Missouri Baptist prescribing reference, Sena Sayood and Adam Schafer should review it for stewardship alignment with Dorsata, formulary, renal adjustment, infusion practices, the BJC vancomycin trough workflow, antibiogram data, and OPAT monitoring. Interpret susceptibility results with the infection site, resistance mechanism, dose, and clinical evidence.

Before choosing the drug

When someone asks, “What antibiotic should we use?” I usually need several other answers first. Sometimes the correct response is immediate broad therapy. Sometimes it is a better culture, drainage, or no antibiotic at all.

Is there an infection?What findings support infection rather than colonization, inflammation, aspiration pneumonitis, or a contaminated specimen?
How sick is the patient?Instability lowers the tolerance for missing a pathogen. A stable patient allows more diagnostic precision.
Where is it?The syndrome determines likely organisms, penetration, source control, and duration.
What has grown before?Review prior cultures, recent antibiotics, healthcare exposure, devices, and the local antibiogram.
What will fix the source?Drainage, debridement, device removal, or relief of obstruction may matter more than adding another drug.
When will we reassess?Write what you expect to know in 24–72 hours and what would allow narrowing or stopping.

For students and residents

Be able to state the syndrome, likely organisms, why the chosen drug covers them, what it misses, and what information will change the plan.

For fellows

Separate guideline recommendations from trial evidence and local practice. When several drugs are active, weigh resistance mechanism, inoculum, exposure, toxicity, source control, and the consequences of failure.

A practical word about dosing

Do not memorize one dose for each drug. The same antibiotic may have different doses for cystitis, bacteremia, meningitis, obesity, augmented renal clearance, intermittent hemodialysis, or continuous renal replacement therapy. Loading doses are often based on volume of distribution and may still be needed in acute kidney injury; maintenance dosing is where renal clearance usually matters. For severe infection, verify the dose before the first administration rather than waiting for the next day’s rounds.

Local practice: Missouri Baptist currently uses the regimens in Dorsata and does not routinely use extended-infusion beta-lactam dosing. Other institutions may use prolonged infusion. On this service, follow the current local order set and pharmacy guidance.

Fellow detail: ESBL and AmpC are not interchangeable labels

ESBL enzymes are most often encountered in E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and K. oxytoca and inactivate most penicillins, cephalosporins, and aztreonam. Carbapenems generally remain active. For organisms at moderate risk of clinically significant inducible AmpC, the 2026 IDSA guidance identifies E. cloacae complex, K. aerogenes, C. freundii, and H. alvei. Cefepime is a preferred option when the MIC is susceptible or susceptible-dose-dependent and an ESBL gene has not been identified. Susceptible-dose-dependent results require an exposure-optimized regimen confirmed with pharmacy; do not assume a routine dose is adequate. Ceftriaxone is generally avoided for invasive AmpC-risk infection. The 2026 guidance allows selected patients with non-severe infection to complete empirically started ceftriaxone when they are improving and source control is adequate. The old SPACE or SPICE mnemonics overgeneralize species risk.1

Approach to a reported penicillin allergy

Ask what happened, how soon after the dose, how long ago, and whether the patient has since tolerated another beta-lactam. Nausea, headache, a family history, or an unknown childhood label is not the same as immediate anaphylaxis or a severe delayed reaction such as Stevens-Johnson syndrome. Cross-reactivity is driven mainly by side-chain similarity, not by a fixed “10% cephalosporin risk.” The 2022 practice parameter supports direct oral challenge for selected low-risk histories and emphasizes structurally dissimilar beta-lactams when appropriate. Severe delayed reactions require a different approach and should not be challenged casually.4

PEN-FAST can help organize the history, but it does not make the decision for you. The score gives 2 points when the reaction occurred within 5 years or the timing is unknown, 2 points for anaphylaxis or angioedema or a severe cutaneous adverse reaction, and 1 point when treatment was required or is unknown. In the original study, a score below 3 identified a lower-risk group, but the negative predictive value was 96.3%, not 100%.13 Use the PEN-FAST calculator as a triage aid within the local allergy pathway. Allergy delabeling, test doses, and oral challenges should follow the supervised local pathway. Do not use a low score to dismiss suspected SJS/TEN, DRESS, AGEP, interstitial nephritis, drug-induced liver injury, serum sickness, or another serious delayed reaction. Several of these phenotypes were excluded from the derivation and validation cohorts.

Newer agents for resistant gram-negative infections

These agents have specific resistance niches, and activity can vary within the same species. Before using one, confirm that the isolate represents infection, review the full susceptibility panel and resistance mechanism if available, address source control, and involve ID pharmacy or an experienced ID consultant.

Coverage gaps with newer gram-negative agents

A beta-lactamase inhibitor usually protects or extends the gram-negative activity of its partner beta-lactam. It does not automatically add anaerobic, MRSA, enterococcal, or atypical coverage. Sulbactam's intrinsic activity against Acinetobacter is an important exception to the simple version of that rule.

Newer oral options for ESBL urinary infection

For ESBL complicated UTI today, TMP-SMX, ciprofloxacin, or levofloxacin remain preferred oral options when susceptibility is demonstrated and the patient is otherwise appropriate for oral therapy.1 The newer oral drugs below do not create a general oral pathway for ESBL bacteremia or infection outside the urinary tract.

Currently approved oral agents

Tebipenem pivoxil (Utebzi) was FDA-approved in June 2026 as the first oral carbapenem in the United States. Its indication is complicated UTI, including pyelonephritis, caused by listed susceptible organisms in adults with limited or no alternative oral options. Approval does not establish it for nonurinary ESBL infection, endovascular infection, or an uncontrolled urinary source. Availability was anticipated by the end of 2026.18

Sulopenem etzadroxil-probenecid (Orlynvah) is approved for uncomplicated UTI in selected adult women with limited or no oral alternatives. FDA specifically states that it is not indicated for primary or step-down treatment of complicated UTI. It should not be used as an oral pyelonephritis or bacteremia shortcut.19

Investigational oral agent

Ceftibuten-ledaborbactam etzadroxil pairs an oral cephalosporin with an oral prodrug of a boronic-acid beta-lactamase inhibitor. In vitro studies show activity against many ESBL-, AmpC-, KPC-, and OXA-48-producing Enterobacterales. As of September 2026 it is described as phase 3-ready for complicated UTI, including pyelonephritis, but it is not FDA-approved and there are no phase 3 clinical outcomes establishing its role.20

Before discussing a pipeline agent as a treatment option, recheck its approval status, labeling, availability, clinical outcome data, and the local susceptibility-testing method. In vitro activity and a developer announcement are not clinical outcome evidence.

Other commonly used inpatient agents

Vancomycin

Useful empirically for serious MRSA infection and selected resistant gram-positive infections. BJC currently uses pharmacy-directed trough monitoring. Follow serum creatinine, confirm when the trough should be drawn, and use the indication-specific target in the local pharmacy and Dorsata workflow. Do not apply one trough goal to every syndrome.

Fellow context: The 2020 national consensus recommends AUC-guided monitoring for serious MRSA infection. That differs from the current local workflow and should not be substituted at the bedside without a local practice change.3

Daptomycin

Useful for bacteremia and other serious gram-positive infections, including selected VRE infections. It is inactivated by pulmonary surfactant and should not be used for pneumonia. Follow CPK and assess statin use and new muscle symptoms. Dose depends heavily on organism and syndrome.

Linezolid

IV and oral exposure are similar. It is useful for MRSA pneumonia and selected VRE infections. Follow the CBC with prolonged therapy and review serotonergic drugs, neuropathy risk, and drug interactions.

TMP-SMX

Useful for susceptible urinary pathogens, MRSA skin infection, and several organism-specific indications. Dose by the trimethoprim component. Check potassium (K+), renal function, blood counts, and interacting drugs.

Metronidazole

Treats susceptible anaerobes at appropriate infection sites, including intra-abdominal, oral, and CNS infections. It does not cover aerobic or facultative organisms, so mixed infections may need a companion drug. Adding it to piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem is usually redundant.28

Nitrofurantoin

Nitrofurantoin is a lower-tract drug. It should not be used for pyelonephritis or bacteremia.

Oral fosfomycin

Its established U.S. role is limited. The oral product is FDA-labeled as a single dose for uncomplicated cystitis in women due to susceptible E. coli or E. faecalis. The label states that it is not indicated for pyelonephritis or perinephric abscess.40

Fluoroquinolones

Ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin can be appropriate definitive therapy for complicated UTI or pyelonephritis when the organism is susceptible, the source is controlled, absorption is reliable, and the patient has no major contraindication. The 2025 cUTI guideline supports 5–7 days of a fluoroquinolone in improving patients and supports IV-to-oral transition when an effective oral option is available.2

Oral treatment of susceptible Pseudomonas. Ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin are the only established oral antibiotic class with reliable antipseudomonal activity. Moxifloxacin should not be used for a urinary infection and is not a dependable antipseudomonal drug. Confirm susceptibility before relying on oral fluoroquinolone therapy, because resistance is common and can emerge during treatment.1, 6

Use them with the adverse-effect discussion they deserve. FDA warnings include disabling tendon, muscle, joint, neurologic, and CNS effects; dysglycemia; and risk of aortic aneurysm or dissection in susceptible patients. Review QT risk, renal dosing, drug interactions, and separation from cations or tube feeds.5

Laboratory monitoring

There is no universal “antibiotic lab schedule.” Start with the CBC, creatinine and estimated renal function, hepatic profile when relevant, major drug interactions, and the baseline abnormality most likely to confuse later interpretation. Follow-up depends on illness severity, organ-function stability, treatment length, concomitant drugs, and whether the patient remains hospitalized or is leaving on OPAT. Case: Klebsiella complicated UTI

The culture is back. Does the patient still need IV therapy?

A patient was admitted with fever, hypotension, pyelonephritis, and an obstructing ureteral stone. Blood and urine cultures grow K. pneumoniae. Urology has decompressed the collecting system. The patient is improving on cefepime. The isolate is susceptible to ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, and levofloxacin and has no identified ESBL phenotype.

Before opening the discussion

  • What mattered most in the first 24 hours?
  • Would you narrow cefepime?
  • Could the patient finish orally?
  • How would you choose duration?
How I would discuss this on rounds

The initial priorities were active therapy and decompression. Once the obstruction is relieved, the patient is stable, and susceptibility results are available, continued antipseudomonal coverage is unnecessary unless there is another reason for it.

Ceftriaxone is a reasonable narrower IV option for this susceptible isolate. Ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin is also a reasonable oral option if absorption is reliable, interactions and safety concerns are acceptable, and there is no other deep focus. The presence of bacteremia alone does not require a PICC.

For this improving patient with bacteremic cUTI, the 2025 IDSA guideline suggests 7 total days of effective therapy, counted from the first day of active treatment. The general 5–7-day fluoroquinolone recommendation should not be read as a five-day recommendation for the bacteremia in this case. Many supporting studies excluded patients with complete obstruction, abscess, severe sepsis, significant immunocompromise, chronic kidney disease, or urologic procedures, so duration may need individualization. Source control and clinical response matter more than mechanically assigning 14 days.2

When to change from IV to oral therapy

Oral therapy is reasonable when the patient is improving, source control is adequate, the organism is susceptible, the chosen drug reaches the relevant site and systemic exposure, the gastrointestinal tract works, and the patient can take the regimen reliably. “Bacteremia” by itself is not an indication for prolonged IV therapy.

Agents with high oral exposure

Fluoroquinolones, linezolid, metronidazole, TMP-SMX, doxycycline, and fluconazole have high oral bioavailability. Oral beta-lactams can also work for selected infections, but dose, organism MIC, syndrome, and evidence are more important than simply calling the drug bioavailable.

When IV therapy should continue

Hemodynamic instability, uncontrolled source, unreliable absorption, no active oral option, endovascular or CNS infection without a validated oral strategy, adherence barriers that cannot be addressed, or a clinical course that does not fit the presumed diagnosis.

Selected trials

These trials changed practice, but each answered a narrower question than its nickname sometimes implies.

MERINO

Randomized trial Harris et al., 2018. In 378 patients with ceftriaxone-resistant E. coli or K. pneumoniae bloodstream infection, 30-day mortality was 12.3% with piperacillin-tazobactam and 3.7% with meropenem. Piperacillin-tazobactam did not meet noninferiority. The practical conclusion is to prefer a carbapenem for invasive ESBL infection rather than trusting an apparently susceptible piperacillin-tazobactam result.7

CAMERA2

Randomized trial Tong et al., 2020. Adding 7 days of an antistaphylococcal beta-lactam to vancomycin or daptomycin for MRSA bacteremia did not improve the primary composite outcome. It reduced persistent bacteremia at day 5 but increased acute kidney injury from 6% to 23%, and the trial stopped early. This does not support routine combination therapy.8

OVIVA

Randomized noninferiority trial Li et al., 2019. Among 1,054 adults with bone, joint, or orthopedic hardware infection, an oral strategy used during the first 6 weeks was noninferior to continued IV therapy. Definite treatment failure occurred in 13.2% of the oral group and 14.6% of the IV group among patients with endpoint data. IV catheter complications were much more frequent with IV therapy. Oral drugs were selected by infection specialists; this was not evidence that any oral agent is adequate for any bone infection.9

POET

Randomized noninferiority trial Iversen et al., 2019. Four hundred stable adults with selected left-sided endocarditis due to streptococci, E. faecalis, S. aureus, or coagulase-negative staphylococci were randomized after at least 10 days of IV therapy. The composite outcome occurred in 9.0% with partial oral therapy and 12.1% with continued IV therapy. The conclusion applies to carefully selected, stable patients using protocolized combination regimens and close follow-up, not to routine early oral treatment of endocarditis.10

BALANCE

Randomized noninferiority trial Daneman et al., published online 2024 and in print 2025. In 3,608 hospitalized patients with bloodstream infection, 7 days was noninferior to 14 days for 90-day mortality. The trial excluded S. aureus, S. lugdunensis, fungemia, severe immunosuppression, prosthetic heart valves or endovascular grafts, and infections requiring prolonged therapy such as endocarditis, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, undrained abscess, or retained infected prosthetic material. Seven days is not a universal bacteremia duration.11

Presenting the antibiotic plan on rounds

The primary team should be able to tell what you think is being treated, why this drug was chosen, what still needs to happen, and when the plan will change.

Syndrome and source [What infection are we treating? Is the source controlled?] Microbiology [Relevant cultures, susceptibilities, and prior organisms] Antibiotic decision today [Drug and route] because [coverage, site, host, and resistance considerations] Safety and dosing [Renal/hepatic adjustment, allergy, interaction, and monitoring plan] Reassessment [What pending result or clinical change permits narrowing, oral transition, stopping, or additional source evaluation?] Duration [First day of effective therapy or other duration anchor, anticipated stop date, and reason]

Common errors

  • Fever is treated as a diagnosis without establishing an infectious syndrome.
  • Vancomycin and an antipseudomonal beta-lactam are continued after cultures and the clinical course allow narrowing.
  • MSSA remains on vancomycin instead of an active antistaphylococcal beta-lactam.
  • Susceptible Klebsiella is treated broadly because its narrower beta-lactam options were never reviewed.
  • Fluoroquinolones are rejected categorically even when they provide the best susceptible oral option for cUTI or Pseudomonas.
  • Renal function changes without a dosing reassessment, particularly with cefepime, vancomycin, aminoglycosides, TMP-SMX, or fluoroquinolones.
  • A PICC is ordered before anyone asks whether a validated oral regimen is available.
  • The drug is changed repeatedly while an abscess, obstruction, infected catheter, or hardware remains untreated.

Questions for the end of the session

1. A ceftriaxone-susceptible K. pneumoniae urine and blood isolate is improving after urinary decompression. Must cefepime be continued?

Answer

No. If there is no ESBL or other resistance concern and the patient is improving, ceftriaxone is a reasonable narrower IV option. Ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin may be reasonable orally when susceptible and otherwise appropriate.

2. Which oral antibiotics can be relied on for susceptible Pseudomonas aeruginosa?

Answer

Ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin. Fluoroquinolones are the only established oral class with reliable antipseudomonal activity. Confirm susceptibility. Moxifloxacin is not an appropriate substitute.

3. How is vancomycin monitored on this service?

Answer

BJC currently uses pharmacy-directed trough monitoring. Confirm the correct draw time, follow serum creatinine, and use the indication-specific target in Dorsata or the local pharmacy workflow. The national 2020 consensus recommends AUC-guided monitoring for serious MRSA infection, but that is not the current local workflow.

4. Does OVIVA show that every osteomyelitis patient can receive any active oral antibiotic from day 1?

Answer

No. Patients entered a specialist-directed oral strategy within 7 days of definitive surgery or planned curative treatment. Drug selection, source control, diagnosis, absorption, adherence, and follow-up still matter.

5. What should happen when cultures narrow the likely pathogen?

Answer

Reassess whether infection is present, narrow to the least broad effective drug, confirm the dose and route, address source control, and establish the duration anchor and stop date. Sometimes the correct decision is to stop antibiotics.

6. An Enterobacterales isolate produces NDM. Is ceftazidime-avibactam alone a reliable choice?

Answer

No. Avibactam does not inhibit NDM, and NDM hydrolyzes ceftazidime. Current IDSA guidance lists aztreonam-avibactam and cefiderocol as preferred options for NDM-producing Enterobacterales. Ceftazidime-avibactam plus aztreonam is an alternative when the fixed aztreonam-avibactam product is unavailable. Final selection still depends on susceptibility testing, infection site, clinical stability, source control, and local ID pharmacy input.

Antibiotic rounds sheet

Before the drug

Infection or mimic? Syndrome? Severity? Cultures? Prior organisms? Local resistance? Source control?

Coverage

Name the likely pathogens. State what the regimen covers and what it misses. Interpret susceptibility with the site, resistance mechanism, and dose.

Klebsiella

Ampicillin does not cover K. pneumoniae. Susceptible isolates may allow cefazolin, ceftriaxone, cefepime, pip-tazo, or carbapenem depending on syndrome and resistance mechanism.

Pseudomonas

Common IV options include cefepime, ceftazidime, pip-tazo, meropenem/imipenem, aztreonam, or newer agents when indicated. Ciprofloxacin/levofloxacin are the oral class if susceptible.

Newer resistant gram-negative agents

Choose by organism, site, AST, and mechanism. KPC: Avycaz, Vabomere, or Recarbrio. NDM-E: Emblaveo or cefiderocol; Avycaz plus aztreonam is an alternative. DTR Pseudomonas: Zerbaxa, Avycaz, Recarbrio, or cefiderocol depending on site and susceptibility. Avycaz, Zerbaxa, cefiderocol, aztreonam, and Emblaveo are not complete polymicrobial regimens; add anaerobic or gram-positive therapy only when the syndrome requires it.

New oral agents

Utebzi is approved for selected cUTI/pyelonephritis with limited oral alternatives. Orlynvah is uUTI only. Oral fosfomycin is labeled for uncomplicated cystitis; selected cUTI transition use follows initial IV therapy and needs ID/pharmacy review. None creates a general oral pathway for ESBL bacteremia.

Vancomycin

BJC: trough-guided monitoring through pharmacy and Dorsata. Verify draw timing, the indication-specific target, and serum creatinine.

IV to oral

Improving patient, controlled source, active oral drug, adequate exposure at the site, functioning GI tract, feasible adherence and follow-up.

Monitoring

Start with CBC, renal function, and drug-specific risks. Vancomycin: trough and creatinine. Daptomycin: CPK and creatinine. Linezolid: weekly CBC. TMP-SMX: potassium (K+), creatinine, and CBC. State who follows results after discharge. PEN-FAST does not override a serious delayed reaction. Aztreonam and ceftazidime share an R1 side chain.

Before signing

Drug, route, dose source, monitoring, source control, reassessment trigger, duration anchor, and stop date.

Local review required: Dorsata alignment, antibiogram, formulary, dosing, renal replacement therapy, vancomycin trough targets and timing, allergy, OPAT, and monitoring protocols.
