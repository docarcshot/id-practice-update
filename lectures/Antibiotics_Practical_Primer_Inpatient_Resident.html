What this primer is for

A positive culture tells us which organism grew. It does not establish that the organism is causing infection, that a drug reaches the infected tissue, or that antibiotics alone will clear the source.

The cases follow an antibiotic decision from the first dose to reassessment. The initial choice reflects the syndrome and severity of illness. As cultures return and the clinical course becomes clearer, the question shifts to whether the patient still needs the same drug, route, or duration.

By the end, you should be able to

  1. Build an empiric regimen from the syndrome, severity, likely pathogens, prior microbiology, and local resistance data.
  2. Compare the practical coverage gaps of commonly used inpatient antibiotics, recognize the major restricted agents, and know when their use requires ID and pharmacy input.
  3. Recognize when dose, route, tissue penetration, toxicity, monitoring needs, or source control makes an apparently active drug a poor choice.
  4. Narrow, stop, or transition therapy when the clinical and microbiologic data permit.
  5. Explain what the major antibiotic trials did and did not establish.
Local information. The local antibiogram describes a population; the susceptibility report describes the patient’s isolate. The formulary, renal dosing resources, and vancomycin monitoring service supply practical details that a lecture cannot.
This is primarily a resident primer. Residents should complete the main text. Fellows should know the same material and continue through the brief fellow section on resistance mechanisms, difficult treatment choices, and pipeline agents.

Before choosing the drug

The first decision is whether the patient has an infection and how urgently it needs treatment. An unstable patient may need broad initial coverage. In a stable patient, a better specimen may clarify whether antibiotics are needed at all. When an infected space needs drainage, broadening coverage does not resolve the underlying problem.

Is there an infection?What findings support infection rather than colonization, inflammation, aspiration pneumonitis, or a contaminated specimen?
How sick is the patient?Instability lowers the tolerance for missing a pathogen. A stable patient allows more diagnostic precision.
Where is it?The syndrome determines likely organisms, penetration, source control, and duration.
What has grown before?Prior cultures, recent antibiotics, devices, healthcare exposures, and local resistance patterns help estimate which organisms may be present.
What will fix the source?Drainage, debridement, device removal, or relief of obstruction may matter more than adding another drug.
When will we reassess?Which results are likely in the next 24–72 hours? What would support narrower therapy or show that antibiotics are unnecessary?

For students and residents

Be able to state the syndrome, likely organisms, why the chosen drug covers them, what it misses, and what information will change the plan.

For fellows

Separate guideline recommendations from trial evidence and local practice. When several drugs are active, weigh resistance mechanism, inoculum, exposure, toxicity, source control, and the consequences of failure.

A practical word about dosing

Dose depends on what exposure is needed at the site of infection and how the patient clears the drug. Cystitis, bacteremia, and meningitis need not share a regimen. Obesity, augmented renal clearance, and intermittent or continuous dialysis add further variability. Loading and maintenance doses are separate questions for the drug-specific reference and pharmacy team. In severe infection, dose verification belongs before the first administration rather than on the next day’s rounds.

Infusion practice. The choice between standard and extended beta-lactam infusions depends on the drug, indication, and local protocol. The current institutional order set and pharmacy guidance describe how each regimen is delivered.

Resident essential: ESBL and AmpC are not interchangeable labels

ESBL enzymes inactivate most penicillins, cephalosporins, and aztreonam. They are frequently encountered in E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and K. oxytoca; carbapenems generally retain activity. Inducible AmpC presents a different problem: an initially susceptible isolate can develop resistance during ceftriaxone exposure. E. cloacae complex, K. aerogenes, and C. freundii carry moderate risk. H. alvei is grouped with them largely on in vitro evidence; clinical experience is limited.1

For these moderate-risk species, cefepime is a preferred option when its MIC falls in the susceptible or susceptible-dose-dependent range, no ESBL gene has been identified, and the regimen provides the exposure that MIC requires. Ceftriaxone is generally unsuitable as planned therapy for invasive infection. A narrower exception is completion of empiric ceftriaxone for selected nonsevere infections after improvement and adequate source control. Y. enterocolitica also has inducible chromosomal ampC, but clinical evidence is insufficient to assign it the same risk; the isolate’s susceptibility and the syndrome matter.1

Approach to a reported penicillin allergy

Penicillin allergy assessment, testing, and side-chain cross-reactivity

A reaction history defines the clinical question; the local allergy pathway determines how testing or a supervised challenge is performed.

An allergy history has a chronology: which drug was given, how soon symptoms appeared, what treatment was needed, how long ago the reaction occurred, and which beta-lactams the patient has tolerated since. Many reported penicillin allergies do not represent current IgE-mediated allergy. Missing details leave the history unresolved.4

What a low PEN-FAST score means. A score below 3 had a negative predictive value of 96.3% in the original Melbourne derivation/internal-validation cohort; external-cohort values ranged from 84.9% to 98.4%. These estimates depend on the tested population. Isolated drug fever, serum sickness, interstitial nephritis and drug-induced liver injury were excluded phenotypes, so a low score does not clear those histories.13 The PEN-FAST calculator can help organize the history, but residents should not independently remove the allergy label or order a test dose or oral challenge. Suspected severe delayed reactions follow a separate pathway.

Testing, challenge, and desensitization

Skin testing

Penicillin skin testing evaluates immediate IgE-mediated allergy. It may be followed by an oral amoxicillin challenge. It does not evaluate SJS/TEN, DRESS, AGEP, interstitial nephritis, serum sickness, hemolytic anemia, or other serious non-IgE reactions.

When to involve Allergy/Immunology

Allergy evaluation is especially useful when the label rules out a clearly preferable drug, when several allergy labels limit treatment, or when transplant, chemotherapy, or another immunocompromising condition makes repeated antibiotic exposure likely.

Graded challenge

A graded challenge assesses whether a low-risk patient tolerates the drug. It does not induce temporary tolerance. The eligibility and supervision for a challenge depend on the clinical setting and the evaluating team.

Desensitization

Desensitization induces temporary tolerance when the preferred drug is required and an appropriate alternative is not available. It requires a supervised protocol and uninterrupted dosing. It is not a routine approach to severe delayed reactions.

Cross-reactivity

R1 side-chain similarity helps explain why a penicillin allergy does not predict a reaction to every cephalosporin. An unverified, nonanaphylactic penicillin allergy without features of a severe delayed reaction generally does not require testing or added precautions before a cephalosporin. Even after penicillin anaphylaxis, a cephalosporin with a dissimilar side chain, such as cefazolin, can usually be given routinely. Cross-reactivity with carbapenems is also low: routine pretesting or added precautions are generally unnecessary for a penicillin or cephalosporin allergy, including anaphylaxis. Severe delayed reactions fall outside this approach.4

Coverage gaps with newer gram-negative agents

Adding an inhibitor to a beta-lactam can restore activity against a resistant gram-negative isolate. That activity does not imply coverage of anaerobes, MRSA, enterococci, or atypical organisms. Sulbactam is a useful exception to the usual role of the inhibitor because it also acts directly against A. baumannii.1

Newer oral options and pipeline evidence

For ESBL complicated UTI today, TMP-SMX, ciprofloxacin, or levofloxacin remain preferred oral options when susceptibility is demonstrated and the patient is otherwise appropriate for oral therapy.1 The newer oral drugs below do not create a general oral pathway for ESBL bacteremia or infection outside the urinary tract.

Access matters: confirm inpatient formulary status and outpatient availability before building a transition plan around a newer oral agent.

Approved oral agents: labeling details

Tebipenem pivoxil (Utebzi) was FDA-approved in June 2026 as the first oral carbapenem in the United States. Its indication is complicated UTI, including pyelonephritis, caused by listed susceptible organisms in adults with limited or no alternative oral options. Approval does not establish it for nonurinary ESBL infection, endovascular infection, or an uncontrolled urinary source. Availability was anticipated by the end of 2026.18

Sulopenem etzadroxil-probenecid (Orlynvah) is FDA-approved for selected adult women with uncomplicated UTI; its label excludes primary or step-down treatment of cUTI.19 After improvement on IV therapy, oral sulopenem has a limited, off-label role as an alternative for selected ESBL-E cUTI. This evidence does not extend to nonurinary bacteremia.1

Investigational oral agent

Ceftibuten-ledaborbactam etzadroxil pairs an oral cephalosporin with an oral prodrug of a boronic-acid beta-lactamase inhibitor. In vitro studies show activity against many ESBL-, AmpC-, KPC-, and OXA-48-producing Enterobacterales. As of September 2026 it is described as phase 3-ready for complicated UTI, including pyelonephritis, but it is not FDA-approved and there are no phase 3 clinical outcomes establishing its role.20

Recheck approval status, labeling, availability, outcome data, and the local susceptibility-testing method before considering a pipeline agent. In vitro activity alone is insufficient.

Other commonly used inpatient agents

Vancomycin

Vancomycin provides empiric coverage for serious suspected MRSA infection and selected resistant gram-positive infections. Its exposure varies with renal function, so the timing of blood samples affects their interpretation.

For serious MRSA infection, the monitoring target is an AUC24 of 400–600 mg·h/L when the MIC is assumed to be 1 mg/L. A trough alone, even one between 15 and 20 mg/L, is not the recommended target for that indication. The treating pharmacy or OPAT service coordinates sampling and interpretation.3

Daptomycin

FDA-labeled for S. aureus bacteremia, including right-sided endocarditis in adults, and selected skin infections. VRE treatment is off-label and requires specialist selection. It should not be used for pneumonia. CPK trends, renal function, statin exposure, and new muscle symptoms inform safety monitoring. Dose selection depends on organism and syndrome.25

Linezolid

Linezolid has similar oral and IV exposure and activity against MRSA in pneumonia and selected VRE infections. Blood counts, serotonergic drug interactions, and neuropathy risk become important during treatment.26

TMP-SMX

TMP-SMX has a role in susceptible urinary infections, MRSA skin infection, and selected organism-specific indications. Doses are expressed by the trimethoprim component. Potassium, renal function, blood counts, and interacting medications affect its suitability.27

Metronidazole

Provides anaerobic coverage below the diaphragm and for selected oral anaerobic infections. Adding it to piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem is usually redundant.

Nitrofurantoin

Nitrofurantoin is a lower-tract drug. It should not be used for pyelonephritis or bacteremia.

Oral fosfomycin

Oral fosfomycin has a single-dose U.S. indication for uncomplicated cystitis in adult women with susceptible E. coli or E. faecalis. Pyelonephritis and perinephric abscess fall outside that indication.40

Two newer oral UTI drugs residents may hear about. Tebipenem pivoxil (Utebzi) is approved for selected adults with complicated UTI, including pyelonephritis, who have limited or no alternative oral options. Sulopenem etzadroxil-probenecid (Orlynvah) is labeled for selected adult women with uncomplicated UTI; its label excludes cUTI. Switching to oral sulopenem after improvement on IV therapy is a selected, off-label alternative for ESBL-E cUTI.1 Hospital and outpatient availability may limit the use of either drug.1819

The cUTI transition evidence above does not establish oral fosfomycin as completion therapy for ESBL bloodstream infection, even after blood cultures clear. Any exceptional treatment plan requires an explicit account of the remaining infection, expected drug exposure, source control, uncertainty and follow-up.1

Fluoroquinolones

For patients with complicated UTI who improve on effective therapy, the suggested 5–7-day fluoroquinolone course is conditional when the organism is susceptible. The separate conditional duration for improving cUTI with gram-negative bacteremia is 7 days. The case below illustrates how response and relief of obstruction affect those decisions.2

Oral treatment of susceptible Pseudomonas. Ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin are oral options when the isolate is susceptible. Moxifloxacin should not be used for a urinary infection and is not a dependable antipseudomonal drug. Activity cannot be inferred from the species name, and resistance can emerge during treatment.16

The convenience of oral treatment must be weighed against fluoroquinolone toxicity. FDA warnings address disabling tendon, muscle, joint, peripheral nerve, and CNS effects, dysglycemia, and aortic aneurysm or dissection in susceptible patients. QT risk, renal function, interacting drugs, and reduced absorption with cations or tube feeds can change the choice.5

Laboratory monitoring

Monitoring asks two questions: is drug clearance changing, and is toxicity developing? Baseline blood counts, renal function, and, when relevant, liver tests give later abnormalities context. Illness severity, treatment length, concomitant drugs, and the ability to obtain follow-up determine how often measurements are useful.

Case: Klebsiella complicated UTI

The culture is back. Does the patient still need IV therapy?

A patient was admitted with fever, hypotension, pyelonephritis, and an obstructing ureteral stone. Blood and urine cultures grow K. pneumoniae. Urology has decompressed the collecting system. The patient is improving on cefepime. The isolate is susceptible to ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, and levofloxacin and has no identified ESBL phenotype.

Before opening the discussion

  • What mattered most in the first 24 hours?
  • Would you narrow cefepime?
  • Could the patient finish orally?
  • How would you choose duration?
Teaching discussion for rounds

At presentation, hypotension and an obstructed collecting system made active therapy and decompression urgent. Both cultures now identify the same organism, and continued antipseudomonal coverage adds little once the patient is stable, unless another infection requires it.

Ceftriaxone is a narrower IV option for this susceptible isolate if there is no additional resistance concern. Once the obstruction is relieved and the patient is stable, oral ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin is an option if absorption is reliable, exposure in blood, urine, and infected tissue is adequate, and interactions and adverse-effect risks are acceptable. The suggestion to switch to oral therapy in this bacteremic population is conditional, with very low certainty of evidence.2

For improving urinary-source gram-negative bacteremia, the suggested course is 7 days from the first day of effective therapy rather than 14 days. This is a conditional recommendation based on low-certainty evidence. Here, the initial hypotension and obstruction make sustained recovery and adequate drainage central to that judgment. An abscess, persistent obstruction, suspected prostatitis, immunocompromise, renal impairment, or a complicated urologic course can change the duration.2

When to change from IV to oral therapy

Oral therapy in bone and joint infection · Assessing the need for outpatient IV therapy

A reliable oral regimen has to reach the infected site at adequate concentrations. For the narrower category of uncomplicated gram-negative bacteremia, clinical improvement, source control, an active oral option, and a functioning gastrointestinal tract are central to the decision. These criteria do not establish oral treatment for S. aureus bacteremia or endocarditis. OVIVA and POET addressed different, selected populations.38910

Agents with high oral exposure

Fluoroquinolones, linezolid, metronidazole, TMP-SMX, doxycycline, and fluconazole have high oral bioavailability. Oral beta-lactams can also work for selected infections. Their suitability depends on the dose, the organism’s MIC, the infection site, and the evidence for that particular use.

When oral transition is poorly supported

Hemodynamic instability, uncontrolled source, unreliable absorption, no active oral option, endovascular or CNS infection without a validated oral strategy, adherence barriers that cannot be addressed, or a clinical course that does not fit the presumed diagnosis.

Selected trials

A trial result is easiest to interpret by identifying who entered the study, what actually differed between the groups, and when the outcome was measured. Those details determine which clinical questions the study can answer.

MERINO

Randomized trial Harris et al., 2018. In 378 patients with ceftriaxone-resistant E. coli or K. pneumoniae bloodstream infection, 30-day mortality was 12.3% with piperacillin-tazobactam and 3.7% with meropenem. Piperacillin-tazobactam did not meet noninferiority. This helps explain the preference for carbapenems in ESBL bloodstream infection even when piperacillin-tazobactam tests susceptible. The trial itself did not study infection confined to other sites; guidance for those sites draws partly on extrapolation.17

CAMERA2

Randomized trial Tong et al., 2020. Adding 7 days of an antistaphylococcal beta-lactam to vancomycin or daptomycin for MRSA bacteremia did not improve the primary composite outcome. It reduced persistent bacteremia at day 5 but increased acute kidney injury from 6% to 23%, and the trial stopped early. This does not support routine combination therapy.8

OVIVA

Randomized noninferiority trial Li et al., 2019. Among 1,054 adults with bone, joint, or orthopedic hardware infection, an oral strategy used during the first 6 weeks was noninferior to continued IV therapy. Definite treatment failure within one year occurred in 67/509 (13.2%) in the oral group and 74/506 (14.6%) in the IV group among patients with endpoint data. The six-week period describes the assigned route strategy, not the failure follow-up window. OVIVA did not compare total treatment durations or establish equivalence for every oral drug. IV catheter complications were much more frequent with IV therapy. Oral drugs were selected by infection specialists. The trial excluded S. aureus bacteremia and bacterial endocarditis at presentation or within the preceding month; these results do not establish an oral strategy for those populations.9

POET

Randomized noninferiority trial Iversen et al., 2019. Four hundred stable adults with selected left-sided endocarditis due to streptococci, E. faecalis, S. aureus, or coagulase-negative staphylococci were randomized after at least 10 days of IV therapy. The composite outcome occurred in 9.0% with partial oral therapy and 12.1% with continued IV therapy. The conclusion applies to carefully selected, stable patients using protocolized combination regimens and close follow-up, not to routine early oral treatment of endocarditis.10

BALANCE

Randomized noninferiority trial Daneman et al., published online 2024 and in print 2025. In 3,608 hospitalized patients with bloodstream infection, 7 days was noninferior to 14 days for 90-day mortality. The trial excluded S. aureus, S. lugdunensis, fungemia, severe immunosuppression, prosthetic heart valves or endovascular grafts, and infections requiring prolonged therapy such as endocarditis, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, undrained abscess, or retained infected prosthetic material. Seven days is not a universal bacteremia duration.11

Presenting the antibiotic plan on rounds

A useful presentation on rounds makes the reasoning audible: what infection explains the findings, what the antibiotic is expected to accomplish, and what new information would change the plan. The prompts below are a way to present that argument.

Syndrome and source [What infection are we treating? Is the source controlled?] Microbiology [Relevant cultures, susceptibilities, and prior organisms] Antibiotic decision today [Drug and route] because [coverage, site, host, and resistance considerations] Safety and dosing [Renal/hepatic adjustment, allergy, interaction, and monitoring plan] Reassessment [What pending result or clinical change permits narrowing, oral transition, stopping, or additional source evaluation?] Duration [First day of effective therapy or other duration anchor, anticipated stop date, and reason]

Potential errors to examine

  • Fever is treated as a diagnosis without establishing an infectious syndrome.
  • Vancomycin and an antipseudomonal beta-lactam are continued after cultures and the clinical course allow narrowing.
  • Vancomycin continues for MSSA without addressing whether an active antistaphylococcal beta-lactam is feasible.
  • Susceptible Klebsiella is treated broadly because its narrower beta-lactam options were never reviewed.
  • Fluoroquinolones are rejected categorically even when they provide the best susceptible oral option for cUTI or Pseudomonas.
  • Renal function changes without a dosing reassessment, particularly with cefepime, vancomycin, aminoglycosides, TMP-SMX, or fluoroquinolones.
  • A PICC is ordered before anyone asks whether a validated oral regimen is available.
  • The drug is changed repeatedly while an abscess, obstruction, infected catheter, or hardware remains untreated.

Questions for the end of the session

1. A patient improves after urinary decompression. Blood and urine grow ceftriaxone-susceptible K. pneumoniae. What still justifies cefepime?

Answer

Neither the organism nor its stated susceptibility requires continued cefepime in this case. In the absence of another resistance concern, ceftriaxone is a narrower IV option. An oral option also requires the stability, obstruction relief, bloodstream exposure, intake, and safety checks described in the case; a susceptible result alone is insufficient.2

2. Which oral antibiotics can be relied on for susceptible Pseudomonas aeruginosa?

Answer

Ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin are the established oral options discussed here, provided the isolate is susceptible and the drug suits the site. Moxifloxacin is not a dependable substitute.16

3. What should be checked when monitoring vancomycin?

Answer

In serious MRSA infection, the useful measurement is exposure over 24 hours. A drug level is interpretable only in light of renal function, actual doses, and sample timing; a universal trough target does not answer that question.3

4. Does OVIVA show that every osteomyelitis patient can receive any active oral antibiotic from day 1?

Answer

No. Patients entered a specialist-directed oral strategy within 7 days of definitive surgery or planned curative treatment. Drug selection, source control, diagnosis, absorption, adherence, and follow-up still matter. OVIVA excluded S. aureus bacteremia or bacterial endocarditis at presentation or during the preceding month.9

5. What should happen when cultures narrow the likely pathogen?

Answer

The organism can narrow the differential, but the culture still needs a clinical explanation. If it fits the syndrome, a narrower effective drug may be appropriate, with route, source control, and duration reconsidered. If it represents colonization or contamination, there may be no infection to treat.

6. Fellow extension: An Enterobacterales isolate produces NDM. Is ceftazidime-avibactam alone a reliable choice?

Answer

No. Avibactam does not inhibit NDM, and NDM hydrolyzes ceftazidime. Aztreonam-avibactam and cefiderocol are preferred options for NDM-producing Enterobacterales. Ceftazidime-avibactam plus aztreonam is an alternative when the fixed aztreonam-avibactam product is unavailable. The isolate’s susceptibility, infection site, clinical stability, and source control distinguish among these specialist options.1

Antibiotic rounds sheet

Before the drug

Infection or mimic? Syndrome? Severity? Cultures? Prior organisms? Local resistance? Source control?

Coverage

Which likely organisms are covered? Which are missed? The susceptibility result is most useful when interpreted with the resistance mechanism, infection site, achievable exposure, and clinical evidence.1

Klebsiella

Ampicillin does not cover K. pneumoniae. Susceptible isolates may allow cefazolin, ceftriaxone, cefepime, pip-tazo, or carbapenem depending on syndrome and resistance mechanism.

Pseudomonas

Common IV options include cefepime, ceftazidime, piperacillin-tazobactam, meropenem, imipenem, aztreonam, or selected newer agents. Ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin are oral options only when the isolate is susceptible and the drug suits the site.16

Restricted agents

The resistance mechanism helps explain the choice: ceftazidime-avibactam for susceptible KPC or OXA-48-like Enterobacterales; ceftolozane-tazobactam for selected resistant Pseudomonas; aztreonam-avibactam or cefiderocol for susceptible NDM-producing Enterobacterales. Ceftazidime-avibactam plus aztreonam is an alternative if the fixed product is unavailable. Invasive CRAB is a separate problem, for which sulbactam-durlobactam is generally paired with imipenem-cilastatin or meropenem. Site and isolate testing still matter.1

Vancomycin

In serious MRSA infection, the usual AUC24 target is 400–600 mg·h/L when MIC is assumed to be 1 mg/L. Sampling time, renal function, and the dosing history determine what a level means.3

IV to oral

Can the oral drug cover this organism at the infected site? Stability, source control, absorption, adherence, and follow-up determine whether the evidence fits. Criteria for uncomplicated gram-negative bacteremia do not establish oral treatment for S. aureus bacteremia or endocarditis.38

Monitoring

Examples include timed levels and creatinine for vancomycin, CPK and renal function for daptomycin, weekly CBC for linezolid, and potassium, creatinine, and CBC for TMP-SMX. At discharge, a named clinician needs to receive and act on results.3252627

A low PEN-FAST score does not clear a serious delayed reaction. Aztreonam and ceftazidime share an R1 side chain.421

Questions to close rounds

Why this drug and route? What reference supports the dose? Who follows toxicity and source control? What starts the treatment clock, and what would prompt a change before the planned stop date?

Local practice: the current antibiogram, formulary, renal replacement dosing resources, infusion protocols, microbiology reporting practices, and vancomycin and OPAT services supply details specific to the treating institution.
