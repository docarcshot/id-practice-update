Resident core / 01

Decide whether the patient needs outpatient IV treatment

A 59-year-old is improving after drainage of an infection. The team requests a PICC for discharge tomorrow. Final susceptibilities are pending, the intended duration is not documented, and no outpatient clinician has accepted the laboratory results.

Discuss before reading on. Which decisions are still missing? Would placing the line solve any of them?

Outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy (OPAT) delivers IV treatment outside inpatient care. First establish the diagnosis, source control, intended duration, and whether an effective oral regimen is appropriate. Clinical improvement alone does not establish oral eligibility; the infection, susceptibilities, absorption, interactions, and ability to take treatment must fit. [1] [2]

Resident core / 02

Match the plan to both the infection and the patient

Discharge planning includes the patient and the receiving team.
QuestionWhat must be established
Is the patient ready?Clinical stability, a source-control plan, and no unresolved need for inpatient care.
Can the regimen be delivered?Home, infusion-center, or facility capacity; caregiver ability when needed; training, transport, supplies, and cost.
Is the prescription appropriate?Drug, dose, interval, infusion method, renal adjustment, and a defined duration based on the infection.
Who will manage problems?An accepting OPAT/ID team, a route for urgent contact, and an agreed response to missed doses or adverse events.

Do not lengthen a dosing interval simply because the infusion center is open once daily. Pharmacy must verify that any alternative regimen or pump method provides appropriate exposure and stability. [2]

Resident core / 03

Choose vascular access after choosing the treatment

A PICC is not automatic. IDSA allows a midline for selected adult courses shorter than 14 days, but the infusate and local vascular-access policy also matter. In advanced chronic kidney disease, discuss vein preservation and alternatives with nephrology before PICC placement. [1]

If a new central catheter is needed after catheter-related bloodstream infection, timing depends on the organism and documented clearance. For S. aureus catheter-related infection, IDSA advises waiting until additional blood cultures show no growth. [3]

Document when and by whom the line will be removed. Do not leave it in place after treatment simply because removal was omitted from the discharge plan. [4]

Resident core / 04

Order monitoring and identify who will act on it

The order must include the test, timing, recipient, and response responsibility.
TreatmentMonitoring considerations
Most IV regimensBaseline CBC with differential, renal function and relevant chemistries; liver tests as appropriate. Weekly CBC and chemistry/liver testing is a common OPAT starting plan, individualized to the drug and patient.
VancomycinRenal function and appropriately timed levels. BJC uses pharmacy-directed trough monitoring in this curriculum; use the indication-specific local target and Dorsata workflow.
DaptomycinBaseline and at least weekly creatine kinase (CK), with more frequent monitoring for renal impairment or increased myopathy risk. Ask about muscle pain or weakness.
Any regimen with changing renal function or new symptomsReassess promptly. Do not wait for the next routine weekly laboratory draw.

IDSA recommends serial laboratory monitoring but does not establish one evidence-based schedule for every drug. The 2026 UK OPAT guidance recommends at least weekly blood testing during IV OPAT. Use earlier testing when clinical risk warrants. [1] [2]

Daptomycin can cause eosinophilic pneumonia. New fever, dyspnea, and hypoxemia require prompt evaluation and drug reassessment. [5]

Resident core / 05

Write a discharge plan that the next team can execute

Document the infection and organism; relevant source-control procedure; drug, dose, route, interval and infusion method; treatment start and planned stop or review date; access and line-care plan; tests and timing; named result reviewer; follow-up appointment; pending studies; urgent contact; and line-removal responsibility. [1] [2]

Use patient teach-back for administration and line care. Explain how to obtain urgent help for rigors during infusion, line-site inflammation, arm swelling, rash, breathing difficulty, or inability to administer treatment. Severe symptoms require emergency evaluation. [2] [4] [5]

For the opening case, clarify the final regimen and treatment endpoint and confirm that a team accepts follow-up before discharge. The line choice follows that decision. If an oral regimen is suitable, the PICC may be unnecessary. [1] [2]

Resident core / Close

Apply this on the next consult

Confirm the need for IV therapy, make delivery feasible, and assign responsibility for results. Every discharge needs a treatment endpoint, follow-up, and an access-removal plan.

Fellow discussion / Optional

The receiving service must be able to deliver the agreed plan

Review the actual infusion schedule with the receiving pharmacy or facility. A hospital prescription does not establish home-drug stability, refrigeration needs, pump compatibility, or nursing availability. Include the patient in choosing a feasible setting. [2]

Injection drug use is not an automatic exclusion from OPAT. IDSA recommends case-by-case decisions; assess the available supports and involve addiction care where appropriate. [1]

Oral therapy and long-acting IV agents can reduce access burdens in selected infections, but each still requires a defined indication, monitoring, and follow-up. They are not substitutes for source control or review of the supporting infection-specific evidence. [2]

Discussion variation. The home nurse reports a substantial creatinine increase during vancomycin treatment. Which clinician reviews the result today, who adjusts or holds dosing, and who contacts the patient? A plan that only says "weekly labs" does not answer those questions.
