Resident core

1. Approach to FUO

This module covers the initial evaluation of prolonged fever, including exposure history, baseline tests, infectious and noninfectious causes, and indications for imaging or biopsy.

The fellow section covers culture-negative endocarditis, PET/CT, tissue sampling, molecular testing, and inflammatory disorders.

Resident core

2. Definition and patient population

New fever in the hospitalized patient · Opportunistic infection evaluation in HIV

Classic FUO is prolonged documented fever, conventionally at least 38.3°C on several occasions over at least three weeks, without an explanation after an appropriate minimum evaluation in an immunocompetent adult. The 2024 Delphi consensus emphasizes the tests completed over the number of inpatient days. The three-week convention helps define a teaching and research population. Urgent evaluation depends on the presentation from the first encounter.

PresentationHow to approach it
New fever over hours to daysThe acute syndrome and its severity guide evaluation. Negative initial cultures alone are insufficient for FUO classification.
New hospital or ICU feverRecent procedures, devices, aspiration, thrombosis, transfusion, drugs, and new infection help distinguish causes of hospital-acquired fever.
Neutropenia, transplant, substantial immunosuppression, or advanced HIVImmune status and likely opportunistic infections determine urgency and testing. These populations need separate pathways from classic FUO.
Inflammation of unknown originPersistent unexplained inflammatory-marker elevation for at least three weeks without qualifying fever. Similar etiologic categories; elevated CRP alone does not prove infection.

Recorded temperatures, measurement method, antipyretic use, and timing help characterize the fever. Examination during an episode may reveal transient findings.

Sources Wright et al. FUO and IUO modified Delphi consensus. OFID 2024. · SCCM/IDSA guideline on new fever in adult ICU patients. 2023.

3. When treatment cannot wait

FindingWhy evaluation and treatment are urgent
Possible septic shock, probable or definite sepsis, or rapidly worsening organ dysfunction with suspected infectionThe Surviving Sepsis Campaign calls for immediate resuscitation and antimicrobials in possible septic shock or probable or definite sepsis. Blood cultures are most useful before antibiotics when they can be collected without delay. An infection that needs drainage or another procedure calls for early source control.
Febrile neutropeniaFebrile neutropenia requires prompt empiric treatment under a host-specific pathway.
Meningismus, encephalopathy, new neurologic deficit, or back pain with neurologic signsA possible CNS infection or spinal emergency needs urgent evaluation and indicated empiric treatment. A delayed or unsafe lumbar puncture must not postpone necessary therapy.
New visual symptoms with a convincing giant cell arteritis syndromeThreatened vision requires urgent specialist assessment and glucocorticoid treatment. Vascular imaging or biopsy can support the diagnosis and must not delay vision-saving treatment.
Cytopenias, hepatitis, coagulopathy, rising ferritin, and deteriorationThis pattern raises concern for HLH/MAS and warrants urgent multidisciplinary assessment. Infection and malignancy remain possible triggers or alternatives.
The 2024 FUO consensus generally favors observation over empiric antibiotic or glucocorticoid trials in stable classic FUO. Such trials can obscure the diagnosis and are reserved for selected patients after other diagnostic approaches have been exhausted or when illness makes observation unsafe. A time-sensitive suspected diagnosis is a separate treatment decision.

Workup by stage

This sequence is for a stable, immunocompetent adult with prolonged unexplained fever. History, examination, and earlier results may move a test to an earlier stage or make it unnecessary. A focal finding should be evaluated when identified.

StageEvaluationWhen to change the sequence
First assessmentThe initial assessment establishes the fever timeline, immune status, medications, exposures, prior tests, and examination findings. Hypotension or organ injury changes the urgency.Shock, febrile neutropenia, suspected meningitis, and threatened vision from giant cell arteritis are indications for urgent evaluation and treatment.
Initial workupThe Delphi minimum includes CBC with differential, a metabolic panel with liver tests and calcium, ESR/CRP, ferritin, TSH, ANA, ANCA, and RF. It also includes three blood-culture sets, urinalysis with microscopy, HIV screening, and an IGRA or TST. The panel preferred CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis when available. Chest radiography plus abdominal ultrasound is an alternative.Completed tests and specimen timing determine whether repetition adds value. Blood cultures have better yield before antibiotics when collection is feasible without delay. Suspected active TB calls for site-specific specimens regardless of IGRA/TST results.
Additional tests based on findingsEchocardiography for suspected endocarditis; MRI for focal spinal symptoms; exposure-specific serology, PCR, or fungal testing; selected viral studies; CSF studies for a neurologic presentation; organ-directed rheumatologic or hematologic evaluation.These tests may belong in the initial evaluation. Examples include TEE with a prosthetic valve and suspected endocarditis, malaria smears after relevant travel, or MRI for back pain with neurologic findings.
If the initial evaluation is unrevealingA repeat history, examination, and medication timeline can uncover new clues. FDG PET/CT may identify a target when inflammatory markers remain elevated and no focus is apparent. A suitable node, skin lesion, or imaging abnormality can provide tissue for diagnosis.An accessible suspicious lesion can be biopsied earlier. Progressive cytopenias or an abnormal smear may warrant marrow evaluation. PET uptake alone does not distinguish infection, inflammation, and malignancy.
If no diagnosis is foundThe completed workup and pending results guide follow-up. Selected molecular tests may help when a specific infectious concern remains. Stable patients may be followed with repeat examination and laboratory testing as clinically indicated.New focal symptoms, falling blood counts, or renal or liver injury require reassessment. In a stable patient, the Delphi consensus reserves nonspecific antibiotic or glucocorticoid trials for selected cases after diagnostic options have been exhausted.

These stages illustrate how the workup can change as clues emerge. Exposure-based tests depend on the patient’s history, timing, and syndrome.

4. History and examination

Ask where the patient has lived and traveled, what they did there, and when. Clarify animal contact and work or recreational exposures. Ask whether others became ill. Take the sexual and substance-use history privately.

History and examination beyond exposures

Ask about new headache, jaw fatigue or visual symptoms; back or focal bone pain; joint swelling or morning stiffness; transient rash; oral ulcers; pleuritic pain; diarrhea or blood in stool; weight loss; and symptom-free intervals. Examine the mouth, skin, nails, nodes, heart, abdomen/spleen, spine, joints, wounds, and device sites. Repeat the examination when new symptoms emerge.

Select tests based on the exposure, timing, immune status, and clinical findings.

6. Initial workup

TB testing and its limitations · Interpreting bacteriuria in the clinical context

The 2024 Delphi consensus describes a minimum evaluation for prolonged unexplained fever in an immunocompetent adult. Clinical clues can justify earlier targeted tests.

ComponentProposed minimum and interpretation
Blood testingCBC with differential; comprehensive metabolic panel including calcium and liver tests; ESR, CRP, ferritin, and TSH. Review trends and obtain a smear when counts suggest hematologic disease.
MicrobiologyThe consensus includes three blood-culture sets, urinalysis with microscopy, HIV Ag/Ab screening, and TB infection testing with an IGRA or TST. Adequate blood volume and collection before antibiotics when feasible are more important than waiting for a fever spike. Urine culture depends on the clinical context. A positive urine culture alone does not establish the fever source. IGRA and TST results cannot establish or exclude active TB.
Inflammatory serologyThe consensus includes ANA, ANCA, and rheumatoid factor. Interpret in clinical context. Follow-up antibodies, complement, and organ-specific testing should be directed by the suspected syndrome.
ImagingThe consensus panel preferred CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis when available, including when a focal clue is absent. Chest radiography plus abdominal ultrasound is an alternative. The question being investigated and patient factors guide contrast use.
Additional testsHepatitis tests, EBV/CMV tests, fungal antigens, zoonotic serology, echocardiography, spine MRI, and lumbar puncture require a relevant question. They are not all routine FUO screening.
Original reports and specimen timing help identify gaps. Cultures obtained after antibiotics or inadequate imaging may need to be repeated if the clinical concern persists.

Sources Wright et al. FUO and IUO modified Delphi consensus. OFID 2024. · DeSimone et al. AHA scientific statement on blood culture-negative endocarditis. 2025.

7. Imaging and biopsy

Vertebral osteomyelitis and biopsy timing

Focal findings can guide imaging before additional broad testing. Echocardiography evaluates suspected endocarditis, and MRI is the preferred test for suspected spinal infection. An accessible lesion may provide a biopsy target.

FDG PET/CT can identify a focus for further testing or biopsy after an unrevealing initial evaluation, particularly when inflammatory markers remain elevated. Uptake occurs with infection, inflammation, and malignancy.

The 2024 EANM review found PET/CT diagnostically helpful in about 50% to 60% of patients. The studies were mostly observational and used different definitions of diagnostic yield. A negative scan may support observation in a stable patient but does not exclude all causes of fever.

Planning with pathology and microbiology before biopsy helps preserve tissue for the intended tests. Culture usually requires fresh sterile tissue. Histology generally uses a separate formalin-fixed specimen, with handling adapted when flow cytometry or other specialized tests are needed.

Glucocorticoids can reduce inflammatory PET signal. In a stable patient, this affects scan timing. Urgent treatment takes precedence over scan yield.

Sources Hess et al. EANM consensus on FDG PET/CT in FUO and IUO. 2024. · DeSimone et al. AHA scientific statement on blood culture-negative endocarditis. 2025.

Resident core

8. Management and follow-up

For a stable immunocompetent patient without a time-sensitive syndrome, hydration, nutrition, and symptom control can continue during evaluation. Antipyretics may improve comfort. A response to acetaminophen, an NSAID, or glucocorticoids is nonspecific.

Safe withdrawal of a suspected drug can help assess drug fever. If it treats a confirmed infection, the plan accounts for continued effective therapy when needed.

A discharge plan includes a follow-up interval matched to the patient’s condition and clear ownership of pending results. Reassessment considers new symptoms, examination findings, blood counts, and renal or liver abnormalities.

Urgent reassessment is needed for hypotension, dyspnea, confusion, new neurologic or visual symptoms, severe focal pain, poor intake, bleeding, or other deterioration.

Sources Wright et al. FUO and IUO modified Delphi consensus. OFID 2024. · Someko et al. Drug fever. Annals of Clinical Epidemiology 2023.

Resident core

9. Cases

For each case, discuss the differential, next test, and whether treatment should begin before results are available.

Case 1. A 68-year-old has five weeks of documented fever, elevated CRP, fatigue, and negative cultures. CT chest/abdomen/pelvis is unrevealing. On repeat questioning, there is new jaw fatigue with chewing and transient monocular dimming. What changes now?

Discussion

Jaw symptoms and transient monocular dimming raise concern for giant cell arteritis with threatened vision. Same-day specialist assessment and glucocorticoids are time-sensitive. Temporal and axillary ultrasound where expertise is available, or temporal artery biopsy, can support the diagnosis without delaying treatment.

Case 2. A stable patient with a prosthetic valve has four weeks of fever and recent amoxicillin exposure. Blood cultures are negative and a limited TTE shows no vegetation. How do the negative cultures and limited TTE affect the likelihood of endocarditis?

Discussion

A prosthetic valve and prior antibiotics raise concern for culture-negative endocarditis despite the limited TTE. Culture quality and antibiotic timing guide additional cultures, with TEE and endocarditis consultation informing the next decision. Persistently negative cultures can warrant Coxiella and Bartonella serology and other exposure-directed tests.

Case 3. A 45-year-old has four weeks of confirmed fever. Baseline evaluation and PET/CT are unrevealing. Counts, renal function, and liver tests are stable, there is no focal syndrome, and the patient is eating and ambulatory. Is a two-week broad-spectrum antibiotic course the next step?

Discussion

This stable patient has no finding that supports a broad-spectrum antibiotic trial. Medication review, symptom control, and follow-up are appropriate while the diagnosis remains uncertain. New focal symptoms or worsening laboratory findings warrant further evaluation.

Present the fever timeline, immune status, relevant exposures, examination findings, and abnormal tests. State the leading diagnoses and the reason for the next test.

Explain how the result would affect treatment or further testing.

Include the follow-up plan if testing remains negative.

Advanced fellow section

Additional evaluation when the initial workup is unrevealing.

FUO definitions and study limitations

The traditional temperature threshold is 38.3°C. The 2024 panel discussed lowering it to 38.0°C without reaching consensus. The panel emphasized a completed baseline evaluation, immune status, and geography. PET/CT is optional after the minimum workup and is outside the FUO definition.

FUO studies differ in entry criteria, immune exclusions, referral populations, and prior testing. These differences limit comparisons of diagnostic yield.

Sources Wright et al. FUO and IUO modified Delphi consensus. OFID 2024.

Culture-negative endocarditis

Prior antibiotics are a common cause of negative cultures in endocarditis. Antibiotic timing and culture quality guide further cultures, cardiac imaging, and discussion with microbiology when suspicion persists.

The 2025 AHA statement describes broadening microbiologic testing when cultures in suspected endocarditis remain negative at 72 hours. Options include Coxiella and Bartonella serology, Brucella testing if epidemiologically indicated, and selected molecular tests. The timing of echocardiography and treatment depends on clinical urgency. When surgery is planned, coordination with pathology and microbiology helps preserve valve material for complementary tests. Malignancy and autoimmune disease can cause sterile vegetations. Negative cultures alone are insufficient to establish nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis.

Sources DeSimone et al. AHA scientific statement on blood culture-negative endocarditis. 2025.

Molecular testing

Evidence for broad molecular testing in unselected FUO is limited. In selected cases with a strong infectious concern, such as unresolved blood culture-negative endocarditis, plasma microbial cell-free DNA or broad sequencing may supplement conventional testing. Contamination or incidental detection can yield a positive result. A negative result cannot exclude localized or low-burden infection.

Material from the involved site, when available, can support complementary molecular testing, conventional cultures, and pathology.

Sources Wright et al. FUO and IUO modified Delphi consensus. OFID 2024. · DeSimone et al. AHA scientific statement on blood culture-negative endocarditis. 2025.

PET/CT

PET/CT may identify an occult focus or biopsy site after an unrevealing initial evaluation. Specific findings may justify MRI, echocardiography, or direct biopsy earlier. FDG uptake occurs with infection, sterile inflammation, malignancy, and normal physiologic activity.

A negative PET/CT can support observation in a stable patient, with follow-up guided by symptoms and laboratory findings. Infection and malignancy remain possible. Glucocorticoids can reduce inflammatory uptake, so their timing affects interpretation. Vision-threatening vasculitis requires urgent treatment even when imaging is pending.

Sources EANM PET/CT consensus, 2024

Interpreting conflicting results

FindingInterpretation and next action
Positive low-level zoonotic antibody without a matching syndromeCheck assay, prior exposure, cross-reactivity, and timing. A single result may require paired titers or confirmation.
Positive Histoplasma and Blastomyces antigensCross-reactivity can produce both. Correlate with geography and tissue/culture; do not assume dual infection.
Negative early tickborne or zoonotic serologyAntibodies may not yet be detectable. Use organism-specific PCR when suitable, and repeat serology at the appropriate interval.

Follow-up after a negative workup

Follow-up includes reviewing the workup, medications, pending tests, and interval changes in symptoms or laboratory results. Progressive cytopenias, organ injury, or new focal symptoms require reassessment.

Improvement with an NSAID or glucocorticoid is nonspecific. If the suspected diagnosis warrants treatment, specimens obtained beforehand when feasible can improve diagnostic yield. The plan includes when to reassess the working diagnosis and treatment.
