Fever of Unknown Origin

Initial evaluation, exposure-directed testing, and infectious and noninfectious causes of prolonged fever.

Resident core

1. Approach to FUO

This module covers the initial evaluation of prolonged fever, including exposure history, baseline tests, infectious and noninfectious causes, and indications for imaging or biopsy.

The fellow section covers culture-negative endocarditis, PET/CT, tissue sampling, molecular testing, and inflammatory disorders.

A patient has four weeks of documented fever, negative blood cultures, and no localizing symptoms. What else would you ask before ordering tests?

The exposure table can also be used during a consult.

Resident core

2. Definition and patient population

New fever in the hospitalized patient · Opportunistic infection evaluation in HIV

Classic FUO is prolonged documented fever, conventionally at least 38.3°C on several occasions over at least three weeks, without an explanation after an appropriate minimum evaluation in an immunocompetent adult. The 2024 consensus emphasizes what evaluation was completed rather than a required number of inpatient days. Definitions vary; the three-week threshold is a classification convention, not a reason to delay necessary care.

PresentationHow to approach it
New fever over hours to daysEvaluate the acute syndrome and severity. Negative initial cultures do not make this classic FUO.
New hospital or ICU feverReview procedures, devices, aspiration, thrombosis, transfusion, drugs, and new infection. Evaluate causes of hospital-acquired fever.
Neutropenia, transplant, substantial immunosuppression, or advanced HIVEvaluate according to immune status and likely opportunistic infections. The urgency and testing differ from classic FUO.
Inflammation of unknown originPersistent unexplained inflammatory-marker elevation for at least three weeks without qualifying fever. Similar etiologic categories; elevated CRP alone does not prove infection.

Review the recorded temperatures, measurement method, antipyretic use, and timing. If possible, examine the patient during a febrile episode.

Sources Wright et al. FUO and IUO modified Delphi consensus. OFID 2024. · SCCM/IDSA guideline on new fever in adult ICU patients. 2023.

3. When treatment cannot wait

FindingImmediate response
Shock, rapidly worsening organ dysfunction, or convincing serious infectionObtain cultures promptly if this does not delay treatment, stabilize, start antimicrobials for the suspected infection, and obtain source control.
Fever with profound neutropeniaBegin treatment for febrile neutropenia promptly.
Meningismus, encephalopathy, new neurologic deficit, or back pain with neurologic signsUrgent CNS/spinal evaluation and empiric treatment when indicated. Do not delay necessary therapy for an unsafe or delayed lumbar puncture.
New visual symptoms with a convincing giant cell arteritis syndromeUrgent ophthalmology/rheumatology assessment and glucocorticoids. Obtain rapid vascular imaging or biopsy without delaying vision-saving treatment.
Cytopenias, hepatitis, coagulopathy, rising ferritin, and deteriorationConsider HLH/MAS while investigating infection and malignancy. Seek urgent hematology/rheumatology/critical care input.
Avoid empiric antibiotics or glucocorticoids in stable classic FUO unless a specific suspected diagnosis requires treatment. Both can reduce diagnostic yield.

Workup by stage

This sequence is for a stable, immunocompetent adult with prolonged unexplained fever. History, examination, and earlier results may move a test to an earlier stage or make it unnecessary. A focal finding should be evaluated when identified.

StageEvaluationWhen to change the sequence
First assessmentConfirm documented fever and its duration. Review immune status, medications, exposures, prior tests, and the full examination. Assess for hypotension, organ injury, or another indication for urgent treatment.Shock, neutropenia, suspected meningitis, or threatened vision from giant cell arteritis requires urgent evaluation and treatment.
Initial workupCBC with differential; metabolic panel with liver tests and calcium; ESR/CRP, ferritin, TSH; ANA, ANCA, and RF as included in the FUO consensus. Three blood-culture sets; urinalysis with reflex culture; HIV screening; IGRA or TST. CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, or chest radiography plus abdominal ultrasound when CT is unavailable.Review completed testing before repeating it. Obtain cultures before antibiotics when possible. Suspected active TB requires specimens from the involved site regardless of IGRA/TST results.
Additional tests based on findingsEchocardiography for suspected endocarditis; MRI for focal spinal symptoms; exposure-specific serology, PCR, or fungal testing; selected viral studies; CSF studies for a neurologic presentation; organ-directed rheumatologic or hematologic evaluation.These tests may belong in the initial evaluation. Examples include TEE with a prosthetic valve and suspected endocarditis, malaria smears after relevant travel, or MRI for back pain with neurologic findings.
If the initial evaluation is unrevealingRepeat the history and examination and review medication timing. Consider FDG PET/CT, particularly with persistent elevated inflammatory markers and no localizing finding. Biopsy an appropriate node, skin lesion, or imaging abnormality.An accessible suspicious lesion can be biopsied earlier. Progressive cytopenias or an abnormal smear may warrant marrow evaluation. These stages summarize the evaluation discussed below. They are not a validated scoring system or a required sequence of orders. Exposure-based tests are ordered for the relevant patient, not as a routine panel.

4. History and examination

Ask where the patient has lived and traveled, what they did there, and when. Clarify animal contact and work or recreational exposures. Ask whether others became ill. Take the sexual and substance-use history privately.

History and examination beyond exposures

Ask about new headache, jaw fatigue or visual symptoms; back or focal bone pain; joint swelling or morning stiffness; transient rash; oral ulcers; pleuritic pain; diarrhea or blood in stool; weight loss; and symptom-free intervals. Examine the mouth, skin, nails, nodes, heart, abdomen/spleen, spine, joints, wounds, and device sites. Repeat the examination when new symptoms emerge.

Select tests based on the exposure, timing, immune status, and clinical findings.

6. Initial workup

TB testing and its limitations · Interpreting bacteriuria in the clinical context

The tests below follow the 2024 FUO consensus. This baseline evaluation applies to prolonged unexplained fever in an immunocompetent adult.

ComponentProposed minimum and interpretation
Blood testingCBC with differential; comprehensive metabolic panel including calcium and liver tests; ESR, CRP, ferritin, and TSH. Review trends and obtain a smear when counts suggest hematologic disease.
MicrobiologyThree adequately filled blood-culture sets from peripheral draws, before antibiotics when feasible; urinalysis with microscopy and reflex urine culture; HIV Ag/Ab screening; TB infection testing with IGRA or TST. Do not wait for a fever spike to obtain cultures. An IGRA/TST neither diagnoses nor excludes active TB. A positive urine culture still requires assessment for a urinary syndrome.
Inflammatory serologyThe consensus includes ANA, ANCA, and rheumatoid factor. Interpret in clinical context. Follow-up antibodies, complement, and organ-specific testing should be directed by the suspected syndrome.
ImagingCT chest, abdomen, and pelvis is preferred by the panel when available; chest radiography plus abdominal ultrasound is an alternative. Contrast choice depends on the suspected lesion and patient factors.
Additional testsHepatitis tests, EBV/CMV tests, fungal antigens, zoonotic serology, echocardiography, spine MRI, and lumbar puncture require a relevant question. They are not all routine FUO screening.
Review the original reports and timing. Cultures obtained after antibiotics and inadequate imaging may need to be repeated.

Sources Wright et al. FUO and IUO modified Delphi consensus. OFID 2024. · DeSimone et al. AHA scientific statement on blood culture-negative endocarditis. 2025.

7. Imaging and biopsy

Vertebral osteomyelitis and biopsy timing

Use targeted imaging for focal findings. Obtain echocardiography for suspected endocarditis and MRI for suspected spinal infection. An accessible lesion may be suitable for biopsy.

Consider FDG PET/CT after an unrevealing initial evaluation, particularly when inflammatory markers remain elevated. It may identify a focus for further testing or biopsy. Uptake occurs with infection, inflammation, and malignancy.

The 2024 EANM review found PET/CT diagnostically helpful in about 50% to 60% of patients. The studies were mostly observational and used different definitions of diagnostic yield. A negative scan may support observation in a stable patient but does not exclude all causes of fever.

Before biopsy, arrange specimens for pathology and any indicated cultures or molecular tests. Send fresh sterile tissue for cultures and a separate specimen in formalin for histology.

Glucocorticoids can reduce inflammatory PET signal. Discuss timing before starting them when the patient is stable, but never delay urgent treatment to improve a scan.

Sources Hess et al. EANM consensus on FDG PET/CT in FUO and IUO. 2024. · DeSimone et al. AHA scientific statement on blood culture-negative endocarditis. 2025.

Resident core

8. Management and follow-up

For a stable immunocompetent patient with no urgent treatable syndrome, provide hydration, nutrition, and symptom control while pursuing diagnosis. Fever suppression is for comfort; response to acetaminophen, an NSAID, or steroids does not establish the cause.

Stop a suspected offending drug if it can be safely discontinued. If the drug is treating a confirmed infection, select an alternative when needed.

Before discharge, arrange follow-up and assign responsibility for pending results. Reassessment should include new symptoms, examination findings, blood counts, and renal or liver abnormalities. Choose the interval according to the patient’s condition.

Urgent reassessment is needed for hypotension, dyspnea, confusion, new neurologic or visual symptoms, severe focal pain, poor intake, bleeding, or other deterioration.

Sources Wright et al. FUO and IUO modified Delphi consensus. OFID 2024. · Someko et al. Drug fever. Annals of Clinical Epidemiology 2023.

Resident core

9. Cases

For each case, discuss the differential, next test, and whether treatment should begin before results are available.

Case 1. A 68-year-old has five weeks of documented fever, elevated CRP, fatigue, and negative cultures. CT chest/abdomen/pelvis is unrevealing. On repeat questioning, there is new jaw fatigue with chewing and transient monocular dimming. What changes now?

Discussion

Suspect giant cell arteritis with threatened vision. Arrange same-day specialist assessment and start glucocorticoids without waiting for PET/CT or biopsy. Obtain temporal and axillary ultrasound where expertise is available, or temporal artery biopsy according to local practice.

Case 2. A stable patient with a prosthetic valve has four weeks of fever and recent amoxicillin exposure. Blood cultures are negative and a limited TTE shows no vegetation. Should empiric treatment be stopped because this is “noninfectious FUO”?

Discussion

A prosthetic valve and prior antibiotics raise concern for culture-negative endocarditis. Review the cultures and antibiotic history, obtain additional cultures when indicated, and arrange TEE and endocarditis consultation. Add Coxiella and Bartonella testing if cultures remain negative; select other tests according to exposures.

Case 3. A 45-year-old has four weeks of confirmed fever. Baseline evaluation and PET/CT are unrevealing. Counts, renal function, and liver tests are stable, there is no focal syndrome, and the patient is eating and ambulatory. Is a two-week broad-spectrum antibiotic course the next step?

Discussion

An empiric antibiotic course is not indicated. Review medications and the completed workup, provide symptomatic care, and arrange follow-up. New focal symptoms or worsening laboratory findings warrant further evaluation.

Advanced fellow section

Additional evaluation when the initial workup is unrevealing.

FUO definitions and study limitations

The 2024 panel retained 38.3°C as the fever threshold and emphasized a completed baseline evaluation, immune status, and geography. PET/CT is not required for the FUO definition.

FUO studies differ in entry criteria, immune exclusions, referral populations, and prior testing. These differences limit comparisons of diagnostic yield.

Sources Wright et al. FUO and IUO modified Delphi consensus. OFID 2024.

Culture-negative endocarditis

Prior antibiotics are a common cause of negative cultures in endocarditis. Review antibiotic timing and the quality of blood cultures. If endocarditis remains suspected, obtain further cultures and cardiac imaging and discuss additional testing with microbiology.

In suspected culture-negative endocarditis, obtain Coxiella and Bartonella serology and add Brucella testing when epidemiologically indicated. Consider targeted PCR or sequencing after discussion with microbiology. If surgery is planned, arrange valve pathology, cultures, and molecular testing in advance. Malignancy and autoimmune disease can cause sterile vegetations, but negative cultures alone do not establish nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis.

Sources DeSimone et al. AHA scientific statement on blood culture-negative endocarditis. 2025.

Molecular testing

Consider plasma microbial cell-free DNA or broad sequencing when infection remains suspected despite negative conventional tests, particularly if tissue cannot be obtained. Interpret a positive result for possible contamination or incidental detection. A negative result does not exclude localized or low-burden infection.

When possible, test material from the involved site and send conventional cultures and pathology along with molecular testing.

Sources Wright et al. FUO and IUO modified Delphi consensus. OFID 2024. · DeSimone et al. AHA scientific statement on blood culture-negative endocarditis. 2025.

PET/CT

PET/CT may identify an occult focus or biopsy site after an unrevealing initial evaluation. Use MRI, echocardiography, or direct biopsy when there is a specific indication. FDG uptake occurs with infection, sterile inflammation, malignancy, and normal physiologic activity.

A negative PET/CT does not exclude all infection or malignancy. Continue follow-up according to symptoms and laboratory findings. Glucocorticoids can reduce inflammatory uptake, so discuss scan timing with nuclear medicine. Treatment of vision-threatening vasculitis should not wait for imaging.

Sources EANM PET/CT consensus, 2024

Interpreting conflicting results

FindingInterpretation and next action
Positive low-level zoonotic antibody without a matching syndromeCheck assay, prior exposure, cross-reactivity, and timing. A single result may require paired titers or confirmation.
Positive Histoplasma and Blastomyces antigensCross-reactivity can produce both. Correlate with geography and tissue/culture; do not assume dual infection.
Negative early tickborne or zoonotic serologyAntibodies may not yet be detectable. Use organism-specific PCR when suitable, and repeat serology at the appropriate interval.
Ferritin elevation with inflammationDoes not distinguish infection, Still’s disease, malignancy, and HLH/MAS by itself. Falling counts and organ injury demand reassessment.
Granulomas with negative organism stainsReview sampling, cultures, molecular testing, host, and exposure. Do not equate nonnecrotizing granulomas with sarcoidosis.

Follow-up after a negative workup

Review the workup, medications, pending tests, and changes in symptoms or laboratory results. Arrange reassessment. Progressive cytopenias, organ injury, or new focal symptoms require further evaluation.

Improvement with an NSAID or glucocorticoid is nonspecific. If treatment is started for a suspected diagnosis, obtain relevant specimens beforehand when safe and document when the diagnosis and treatment will be reassessed.
