1. What should learners be able to do?

RESIDENT

  • Interpret HBsAg, anti-HBs, total anti-HBc, and IgM anti-HBc without calling every positive IgM result acute infection.
  • Complete the initial evaluation of HBsAg-positive patients, including fibrosis staging, coinfection testing, and HCC risk assessment.
  • Recognize clear HBV treatment indications and choose among entecavir, tenofovir alafenamide, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate.
  • Screen before immunosuppression and distinguish patients who need prophylaxis from those who may be monitored.
  • Confirm active HCV with RNA, select a simplified pangenotypic regimen when appropriate, and document cure.

FELLOW

  • Resolve isolated core antibody, discordant serologies, possible occult HBV, and indeterminate chronic HBV.
  • Manage HBV around B-cell depletion, CAR-T therapy, stem-cell transplantation, checkpoint inhibitors, prolonged corticosteroids, and HCV DAA treatment.
  • Know when simplified HCV treatment does not apply and when a protease inhibitor is unsafe.

2. Start HBV evaluation with the triple panel

RESIDENT

Order HBsAg, anti-HBs, and total anti-HBc together. Add IgM anti-HBc when acute infection is clinically plausible. HBV DNA is not part of routine population screening, but it is essential once current infection, occult infection, or reactivation risk is suspected.

Two common traps: HBsAg may be transiently positive shortly after hepatitis B vaccination. IgM anti-HBc can also be positive during a severe flare or reactivation of chronic HBV, so it is not proof of a new acute infection by itself.

Acute versus chronic HBV

Acute infection is supported by
Compatible exposure and hepatitis syndrome, HBsAg positivity, and IgM anti-HBc positivity. During the window period, HBsAg may already be negative while IgM anti-HBc remains positive.
Chronic infection is established by
HBsAg persisting for at least 6 months, or prior evidence of chronic infection. A single HBsAg-positive result without earlier testing does not date the infection.

3. Complete the HBV workup before deciding to treat

RESIDENT

Define viral activity. Quantitative HBV DNA, HBeAg, anti-HBe, ALT, AST, bilirubin, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, and INR.
Stage liver disease. CBC/platelets, FIB-4, and preferably elastography when available. Review imaging for nodularity, splenomegaly, collaterals, or ascites. Biopsy is reserved for cases in which noninvasive assessment and laboratory findings do not settle a treatment decision.
Look for coinfection. HIV Ag/Ab, HCV antibody with reflex RNA, and anti-HDV with reflex HDV RNA if positive. EASL recommends one-time HDV screening for every HBsAg-positive patient; older AASLD guidance uses risk-based testing. Universal one-time testing is a reasonable practical approach because risk histories miss infections.
Identify preventable liver injury. Check HAV immunity and vaccinate if nonimmune. Assess alcohol, metabolic dysfunction/steatotic liver disease, medications and supplements, and iron overload or autoimmune liver disease when the pattern warrants it.
Plan drug safety. Creatinine/eGFR and urinalysis; add phosphorus when considering or using TDF. Review osteoporosis, fragility fracture, pregnancy potential, and prior lamivudine exposure.
Address transmission. Test and vaccinate household and sexual contacts. Counsel on blood exposure, barrier protection until partner immunity is documented, and avoiding shared razors, toothbrushes, or injection equipment.

Who needs HCC surveillance?

Use liver ultrasound plus AFP approximately every 6 months when surveillance is indicated. This includes all patients with HBV cirrhosis and selected noncirrhotic patients at increased risk, including men from endemic countries older than 40, women from endemic countries older than 50, people from Africa at an earlier age, those with a first-degree family history of HCC, and patients with PAGE-B ≥10. The 2025 AASLD guidance adds broader surveillance for HBV/HDV coinfection and age/sex-specific surveillance for HBV/HIV coinfection.

Do not use normal ALT or suppressed HBV DNA to cancel HCC surveillance. Antiviral therapy reduces but does not eliminate HCC risk.

4. Who should receive HBV treatment?

RESIDENT

Acute liver failure or severe/protracted acute hepatitis BStart a high-barrier nucleos(t)ide analogue and urgently involve a transplant center. Routine uncomplicated acute HBV is treated supportively.
Cirrhosis with detectable HBV DNATreat, regardless of ALT. Decompensated cirrhosis requires indefinite nucleos(t)ide therapy and transplant/hepatology management; peginterferon is contraindicated.
HBeAg-positive immune-active CHBTreat when HBV DNA is ≥20,000 IU/mL and ALT is ≥2 times the HBV-specific ULN, or when significant inflammation/fibrosis is present.
HBeAg-negative immune-active CHBTreat when HBV DNA is ≥2,000 IU/mL and ALT is ≥2 times the HBV-specific ULN, or when significant inflammation/fibrosis is present.
Immune-tolerant CHB2025 AASLD suggests treatment when age is over 40 or there is at least F2 fibrosis or grade 2 inflammation. Confirm persistently normal ALT before using this label.
HBeAg-negative indeterminate CHBUse shared decision-making. Treatment is reasonable when risk is higher, including older age, fibrosis, male sex, lower platelets, family history of HCC, or persistent ALT/HBV DNA abnormalities. Evaluate other causes when ALT is elevated but HBV DNA is low.

Stopping therapy is not routine. The 2025 AASLD guideline suggests that HBeAg-negative adults without cirrhosis remain on nucleos(t)ide therapy until HBsAg loss. Never stop in cirrhosis without specialist oversight.

6. Screen before immunosuppression and prevent HBV reactivation

RESIDENT

Before therapy, order HBsAg, total anti-HBc, and anti-HBs. If HBsAg or anti-HBc is positive, obtain baseline ALT and HBV DNA. Do this before treatment starts, but do not delay urgent cancer therapy while the evaluation is completed.

Risk is the combination of serostatus and the planned therapy

HIGH means an estimated reactivation risk of at least 10%. MODERATE means 1–10%. LOW means less than 1%. These estimates generally assume a single agent. Combination chemotherapy, added corticosteroids, detectable baseline HBV DNA, and prolonged treatment can increase risk.

Newer agents, especially JAK and IL-6 inhibitors, have limited data and inconsistent placement across guidelines. Confirm the current recommendation when the planned regimen is new, combined, or prolonged.

7. Hepatitis C: confirm viremia, stage disease, treat nearly everyone

RESIDENT

Diagnosis

  • Screen with HCV antibody and automatic reflex quantitative HCV RNA.
  • A positive antibody with negative RNA means no current viremia. It may represent resolved infection, successful prior treatment, or a false-positive antibody. Antibody remains positive after cure.
  • If exposure was recent, the patient is immunocompromised, or acute HCV is strongly suspected, order HCV RNA even when antibody is negative.
  • Do not wait for spontaneous clearance once acute HCV viremia is confirmed. Treat using the same regimens used for chronic infection.

Pretreatment checklist

Confirm infection. Quantitative HCV RNA. Genotype is unnecessary for most noncirrhotic treatment-naive patients receiving a pangenotypic regimen, but it remains relevant in selected cirrhosis and retreatment pathways.
Stage fibrosis. Calculate FIB-4. Review platelets and imaging; use elastography when needed. Presume cirrhosis when FIB-4 is >3.25 or there is prior elastography, biopsy, imaging, or clinical evidence of cirrhosis.
Obtain baseline laboratories. CBC, hepatic function panel, eGFR, and INR when cirrhosis is possible. Assess Child-Pugh class before any NS3 protease inhibitor.
Check coinfections and vaccines. HIV Ag/Ab and the complete HBV triple panel. Vaccinate against HAV and HBV when nonimmune.
Review every medication. Include OTC products and supplements. Check a current interaction resource. Pay particular attention to acid suppression, anticonvulsants, rifamycins, statins, amiodarone, anticoagulants, and antiretrovirals.

Monitoring and follow-up

  • For uncomplicated simplified therapy, routine on-treatment laboratory monitoring is usually unnecessary. Check in for adherence, new medications, and adverse effects.
  • Monitor glucose more closely in patients taking diabetes medications and INR in those taking warfarin because control may change as HCV clears.
  • Obtain quantitative HCV RNA and a hepatic panel 12 weeks or later after therapy to document sustained virologic response (SVR12).
  • After SVR, noncirrhotic patients need no HCV-specific liver follow-up unless liver tests remain abnormal or another liver disease is present.
  • With ongoing exposure risk, check HCV RNA, not antibody, at least annually and whenever unexplained liver-test abnormalities occur.
  • Cirrhosis remains cirrhosis after cure: continue ultrasound with or without AFP every 6 months and manage portal-hypertension risk.
FELLOW HBV during HCV treatment: test the HBV triple panel before DAAs. If HBsAg is positive and HBV treatment criteria are met, start HBV therapy. The 2025 AGA reactivation guideline classifies HBsAg-positive patients receiving HCV DAAs as high risk and favors prophylaxis. AASLD-IDSA HCV Guidance also permits monthly HBV DNA monitoring during and immediately after DAA therapy when baseline HBV DNA is low or undetectable and ordinary HBV treatment criteria are not met. If prophylaxis is chosen under the HCV pathway, continue it through 12 weeks after DAA completion.

8. Cases for the core lecture

Case 1: The positive core antibody

A patient scheduled for rituximab has HBsAg negative, total anti-HBc positive, and anti-HBs 42 mIU/mL.

Answer: This is resolved HBV, not vaccine immunity. Anti-HBs does not eliminate reactivation risk. Obtain baseline ALT and HBV DNA and begin entecavir, TAF, or TDF prophylaxis before rituximab. Continue for at least 12 months after the final B-cell-depleting dose and monitor after prophylaxis ends.

Case 2: Normal ALT, very high DNA

A 44-year-old man is HBeAg positive, HBV DNA 18 million IU/mL, ALT 31 U/L on repeated measurements, and has no cirrhosis by elastography.

Answer: He meets a laboratory immune-tolerant pattern, but age over 40 is now a reason to offer treatment under the 2025 AASLD guideline. The decision should include fibrosis assessment, HCC risk, transmission issues, and patient preference.

Case 3: HCV antibody positive, RNA negative

The patient has no recent exposure and normal liver tests.

Answer: There is no active HCV infection and no DAA indication. Determine whether this reflects prior cure, spontaneous clearance, or a false-positive antibody. Repeat RNA only if recent exposure, ongoing risk, immunosuppression, or unexplained hepatitis makes current infection plausible.

Case 4: HCV with prior ascites

A treatment-naive patient with HCV RNA positivity had ascites two years ago but now has normal bilirubin and albumin.

Answer: Prior decompensation excludes glecaprevir/pibrentasvir (Mavyret) even if the current Child-Pugh score has improved. Use the decompensated-cirrhosis pathway and specialist management.

A vaccine challenge is not a definitive diagnostic test for isolated anti-HBc. An anamnestic response can support prior immunity, but a response does not erase documented anti-HBc or remove future reactivation risk.

Discordant or indeterminate HBV findings

  • New weak HBsAg positivity after vaccination: repeat after the period of possible transient antigenemia and obtain total anti-HBc/HBV DNA if current infection remains plausible.
  • HBsAg and anti-HBs both positive: confirm results and obtain HBV DNA. Consider assay interference, passive antibody, mixed subtypes, or immune-escape variants.
  • IgM anti-HBc positive in known chronic HBV: assess for flare/reactivation. Do not label acute infection without timing, prior serology, HBV DNA trajectory, and clinical context.
  • ALT high with HBV DNA <2,000: evaluate steatotic liver disease, alcohol, medications, HCV/HDV, autoimmune hepatitis, ischemia, and other causes. Continue serial HBV assessment because phase transition remains possible.
  • HBV DNA high with unexpectedly negative HBsAg: repeat both assays and consider occult infection or an HBsAg escape variant.

Immunosuppression: where mistakes occur

  • Ordering only HBsAg misses resolved infection and therefore misses most patients at risk during rituximab or CAR-T therapy.
  • Using anti-HBs positivity as permission to skip prophylaxis with B-cell depletion is incorrect.
  • “Monitor liver enzymes” is not an adequate preemptive plan. HBV DNA must be included every 1–3 months, and the ordering clinician must own the results.
  • Stopping prophylaxis when chemotherapy ends ignores delayed reactivation, especially after B-cell depletion, CAR-T therapy, or transplantation.
  • New immunotherapies should be checked against current guidance. Checkpoint inhibitors can also cause immune-mediated hepatitis, so ALT elevation requires evaluation for both drug toxicity and HBV reactivation.

Stopping chronic HBV therapy

For HBeAg-negative adults without cirrhosis, 2025 AASLD suggests continuing nucleos(t)ide therapy until HBsAg loss. If a highly selected patient strongly prefers withdrawal, reassess cirrhosis, prior decompensation, HCC, coinfections, extrahepatic disease, duration of suppression, quantitative HBsAg when available, and ability to comply with intensive follow-up. Restart thresholds include clinically significant flare, jaundice or impaired synthetic function, or substantial virologic relapse as defined by the specialist protocol.
