1. What should learners be able to do?

RESIDENT

  • Interpret HBsAg, anti-HBs, total anti-HBc, and IgM anti-HBc without calling every positive IgM result acute infection.
  • Complete the initial evaluation of HBsAg-positive patients, including fibrosis staging, coinfection testing, and HCC risk assessment.
  • Recognize clear HBV treatment indications and choose among entecavir, tenofovir alafenamide, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate.
  • Screen before immunosuppression and distinguish patients who need prophylaxis from those who may be monitored.
  • Confirm active HCV with RNA, select a simplified pangenotypic regimen when appropriate, and document cure.

FELLOW

  • Resolve isolated core antibody, discordant serologies, possible occult HBV, and indeterminate chronic HBV.
  • Manage HBV around B-cell depletion, CAR-T therapy, stem-cell transplantation, checkpoint inhibitors, prolonged corticosteroids, and HCV DAA treatment.
  • Know when simplified HCV treatment does not apply and when a protease inhibitor is unsafe.

2. Start HBV evaluation with the triple panel

RESIDENT

Order HBsAg, anti-HBs, and total anti-HBc together. Add IgM anti-HBc when acute infection is clinically plausible. HBV DNA is not part of routine population screening, but it is essential once current infection, occult infection, or reactivation risk is suspected.

Two common traps: HBsAg may be transiently positive shortly after hepatitis B vaccination. IgM anti-HBc can also be positive during a severe flare or reactivation of chronic HBV, so it is not proof of a new acute infection by itself.

Acute versus chronic HBV

Acute infection is supported by
Compatible exposure and hepatitis syndrome, HBsAg positivity, and IgM anti-HBc positivity. During the window period, HBsAg may already be negative while IgM anti-HBc remains positive.
Chronic infection is established by
HBsAg persisting for at least 6 months, or prior evidence of chronic infection. A single HBsAg-positive result without earlier testing does not date the infection.

3. Complete the HBV workup before deciding to treat

RESIDENT

Define viral activity. Quantitative HBV DNA, HBeAg, anti-HBe, ALT, AST, bilirubin, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, and INR.
Stage liver disease. CBC/platelets, FIB-4, and preferably elastography when available. Review imaging for nodularity, splenomegaly, collaterals, or ascites. Biopsy is reserved for cases in which noninvasive assessment and laboratory findings do not settle a treatment decision.
Look for coinfection. HIV Ag/Ab, HCV antibody with reflex RNA, and anti-HDV with reflex HDV RNA if positive. EASL 2025 recommends at least one anti-HDV test for every HBsAg-positive patient; the AASLD 2018 guidance uses risk-based testing. This module follows the EASL screening approach. Repeat testing when exposure risk continues or otherwise unexplained hepatitis develops.
Identify preventable liver injury. Check HAV immunity and vaccinate if nonimmune. Assess alcohol, metabolic dysfunction/steatotic liver disease, medications and supplements, and iron overload or autoimmune liver disease when the pattern warrants it.
Plan drug safety. Creatinine/estimated creatinine clearance and urine protein/glucose before tenofovir; add serum phosphorus in chronic kidney disease. Review osteoporosis, fragility fracture, pregnancy potential, and prior lamivudine exposure.
Address transmission. Test and vaccinate household and sexual contacts. Counsel on blood exposure, barrier protection until partner immunity is documented, and avoiding shared razors, toothbrushes, or injection equipment.

Who needs HCC surveillance?

Use liver ultrasound plus AFP approximately every 6 months when surveillance is indicated. This includes Child-Pugh A or B HBV cirrhosis and transplant-eligible Child-Pugh C disease and selected noncirrhotic patients at increased risk, including men from endemic countries older than 40, women from endemic countries older than 50, people from Africa at an earlier age, those with a first-degree family history of HCC, and patients with PAGE-B ≥10 (validated chiefly in patients receiving antiviral therapy). The 2025 AASLD/IDSA guidance also includes adults with active HBV/HDV coinfection and, without cirrhosis, HBV/HIV coinfection in men age 18 or older and women age 40 or older. Anti-HDV positivity without detectable HDV RNA follows the HBV-alone surveillance criteria. Surveillance assumes the patient could benefit from HCC treatment; competing illness and transplant candidacy matter.

Do not use normal ALT or suppressed HBV DNA to cancel HCC surveillance. Antiviral therapy reduces but does not eliminate HCC risk.

4. Who should receive HBV treatment?

ART selection with HBV coinfection

RESIDENT

These are adult treatment principles using the AASLD/IDSA 2025 guideline and the AASLD 2018 guidance for questions not replaced by that update. Other societies use different eligibility thresholds.

6. Screen before immunosuppression and prevent HBV reactivation

Latent TB evaluation and preventive treatment

RESIDENT

Before therapy, order HBsAg, total anti-HBc, and anti-HBs. If HBsAg or anti-HBc is positive, obtain baseline ALT and HBV DNA. Do this before treatment starts, but do not delay urgent cancer therapy while the evaluation is completed.

Risk is the combination of serostatus and the planned therapy

HIGH means an estimated reactivation risk greater than 10%. MODERATE means 1–10%. LOW means less than 1%. These estimates generally assume a single agent. Combination chemotherapy, added corticosteroids, detectable baseline HBV DNA, and prolonged treatment can increase risk.

When prophylaxis is used
Start before or at immunosuppression. Continue through therapy and generally for at least 6 months afterward; continue at least 12 months after B-cell-depleting therapy. CAR-T and stem-cell transplantation may warrant longer individualized prophylaxis and monitoring because immune recovery is delayed.
When monitoring is used
ALT alone is inadequate. The 2025 AGA guideline requires HBV DNA at 1–3 month intervals. Start a high-barrier antiviral promptly if reactivation occurs.

The categories above follow the 2025 AGA framework, with ASCO cancer-specific guidance layered on top. The AGA categories for IL-6 and JAK inhibitors have low-certainty baseline estimates. Assess the specific agent, HBV DNA and concomitant immunosuppression; categories can differ between guidelines. Confirm current guidance when the planned regimen is new, combined, or prolonged.

7. Hepatitis C: confirm viremia, stage disease, treat nearly everyone

Antiretroviral interaction and absorption review

RESIDENT

Diagnosis

  • Screen with HCV antibody and automatic reflex quantitative HCV RNA.
  • A positive antibody with negative RNA means no current viremia. It may represent resolved infection, successful prior treatment, or a false-positive antibody. Antibody usually remains positive after cure.
  • If exposure was recent, the patient is immunocompromised, or acute HCV is strongly suspected, order HCV RNA even when antibody is negative.
  • Do not wait for spontaneous clearance once acute HCV viremia is confirmed. AASLD-IDSA recommends the same regimens as for chronic infection. Mavyret gained an FDA indication for acute HCV in 2025; Epclusa use for acute infection remains guideline-supported off-label treatment.

Pretreatment checklist

Confirm infection. Quantitative HCV RNA. Genotype is unnecessary for most noncirrhotic treatment-naive patients receiving a pangenotypic regimen, but it remains relevant in selected cirrhosis and retreatment pathways.
Stage fibrosis. Calculate FIB-4. Review platelets and imaging; use elastography when needed. Presume cirrhosis when FIB-4 is >3.25 or there is prior elastography, biopsy, imaging, or clinical evidence of cirrhosis.
Obtain baseline laboratories. For the noncirrhotic simplified pathway, obtain CBC, hepatic panel, and eGFR within 6 months. For compensated cirrhosis, obtain those tests plus INR within 3 months and liver ultrasound within 6 months to exclude HCC and subclinical ascites. Assess Child-Pugh class and any prior decompensation before an NS3 protease inhibitor.
Check coinfections, pregnancy, and vaccines. HIV Ag/Ab and the complete HBV triple panel. Offer pregnancy testing and counseling about treatment risks when pregnancy is possible. Vaccinate against HAV and HBV when nonimmune.
Review every medication. Include OTC products and supplements. Check a current interaction resource. Pay particular attention to acid suppression, anticonvulsants, rifamycins, statins, amiodarone, anticoagulants, and antiretrovirals.

Monitoring and follow-up

  • For uncomplicated noncirrhotic simplified therapy, routine on-treatment laboratory monitoring is usually unnecessary. In cirrhosis, consider hepatic tests and promptly evaluate jaundice, ascites, encephalopathy, or worsening laboratory results. Check in for adherence, new medications, and adverse effects.
  • Monitor glucose more closely in patients taking diabetes medications and INR in those taking warfarin because control may change as HCV clears.
  • Obtain quantitative HCV RNA and a hepatic panel 12 weeks or later after therapy to document sustained virologic response (SVR12).
  • After SVR, noncirrhotic patients need no HCV-specific liver follow-up unless liver tests remain abnormal or another liver disease is present.
  • With ongoing exposure risk, check HCV RNA, not antibody, at least annually and whenever unexplained liver-test abnormalities occur.
  • After HCV cure, patients with cirrhosis who remain candidates for HCC-directed treatment need continued surveillance. The AASLD HCC guidance recommends ultrasound plus AFP approximately every 6 months; use specialist-selected imaging when ultrasound quality is inadequate. Continue assessment of portal-hypertension risk.
FELLOW HBV during HCV treatment: test the HBV triple panel before DAAs. If HBsAg is positive and HBV treatment criteria are met, start HBV therapy. The 2025 AGA reactivation guideline classifies HBsAg-positive patients receiving HCV DAAs as high risk and favors prophylaxis. AASLD-IDSA HCV Guidance also permits monthly HBV DNA monitoring during and immediately after DAA therapy when baseline HBV DNA is low or undetectable and ordinary HBV treatment criteria are not met. If monitoring is chosen, start HBV treatment for an increase in HBV DNA greater than tenfold above baseline, or a level above 1,000 IU/mL when baseline DNA was undetectable or unquantifiable. If prophylaxis is chosen under the HCV pathway, continue it through 12 weeks after DAA completion. For HBsAg-negative, anti-HBc-positive patients receiving DAAs alone, HCV Guidance does not establish a routine HBV DNA schedule; unexplained ALT elevation during or after therapy should prompt assessment for reactivation, including HBV DNA and HBsAg.

8. Cases for the core lecture

Case 1: The positive core antibody

A patient scheduled for rituximab has HBsAg negative, total anti-HBc positive, and anti-HBs 42 mIU/mL.

Answer: This is resolved HBV, not vaccine immunity. Anti-HBs does not eliminate reactivation risk. Obtain baseline ALT and HBV DNA and begin entecavir, TAF, or TDF prophylaxis before rituximab. Continue for at least 12 months after the final B-cell-depleting dose and monitor after prophylaxis ends.

Case 2: Normal ALT, very high DNA

A 44-year-old man is HBeAg positive, HBV DNA 18 million IU/mL, ALT 31 U/L on repeated measurements over at least 6 months, and has no cirrhosis by elastography.

Answer: He meets a laboratory immune-tolerant pattern, but age over 40 is now a reason to offer treatment under the 2025 AASLD guideline. The decision should include fibrosis assessment, HCC risk, transmission issues, and patient preference.

Case 3: HCV antibody positive, RNA negative

The patient has no recent exposure and normal liver tests.

Answer: There is no active HCV infection and no DAA indication. Determine whether this reflects prior cure, spontaneous clearance, or a false-positive antibody. Repeat RNA only if recent exposure, ongoing risk, immunosuppression, or unexplained hepatitis makes current infection plausible.

Case 4: HCV with prior ascites

A treatment-naive patient with HCV RNA positivity had ascites two years ago but now has normal bilirubin and albumin.

Answer: Prior decompensation excludes glecaprevir/pibrentasvir (Mavyret) even if the current Child-Pugh score has improved. Use the decompensated-cirrhosis pathway and specialist management.

A vaccine challenge is not a definitive diagnostic test for isolated anti-HBc. An anamnestic response can support prior immunity, but a response does not erase documented anti-HBc or remove future reactivation risk.

Discordant or indeterminate HBV findings

  • New weak HBsAg positivity after vaccination: repeat after the period of possible transient antigenemia and obtain total anti-HBc/HBV DNA if current infection remains plausible.
  • HBsAg and anti-HBs both positive: confirm results and obtain HBV DNA. Consider assay interference or passive antibody. Subtype-mismatched antibodies and surface-gene variants are reported explanations for coexistence; serology alone cannot identify the mechanism.
  • IgM anti-HBc positive in known chronic HBV: assess for flare/reactivation. Do not label acute infection without timing, prior serology, HBV DNA trajectory, and clinical context.
  • ALT high with HBV DNA <2,000: evaluate steatotic liver disease, alcohol, medications, HCV/HDV, autoimmune hepatitis, ischemia, and other causes. Continue serial HBV assessment because phase transition remains possible.
  • HBV DNA high with unexpectedly negative HBsAg: repeat both assays and consider occult infection or an HBsAg escape variant.

Immunosuppression: where mistakes occur

  • Ordering only HBsAg misses resolved infection and leaves patients with prior HBV unrecognized before rituximab or CAR-T therapy.
  • Using anti-HBs positivity as permission to skip prophylaxis with B-cell depletion is incorrect.
  • “Monitor liver enzymes” is not an adequate preemptive plan. HBV DNA must be included every 1–3 months, and the ordering clinician must own the results.
  • Stopping prophylaxis when chemotherapy ends ignores delayed reactivation, especially after B-cell depletion, CAR-T therapy, or transplantation.
  • New immunotherapies should be checked against current guidance. Checkpoint inhibitors can also cause immune-mediated hepatitis, so ALT elevation requires evaluation for both drug toxicity and HBV reactivation.

Stopping chronic HBV therapy

For HBeAg-negative adults, AASLD 2025 conditionally favors continuing therapy until HBsAg loss. Its implementation criteria for selected withdrawal include no advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis, prior decompensation, HCC, extrahepatic HBV disease, HIV, or HDV; undetectable HBV DNA for at least 2 years; quantitative HBsAg <100 IU/mL; and dependable follow-up. If initially HBeAg positive, HBeAg loss with anti-HBe positivity must have persisted for at least 1 year. The guideline's evidence question involved at least 3 years of NA therapy and 2 years of suppression, while Figure 4 describes 3 years of suppression. Reconcile timing with the treating hepatologist; the shortest interval is not an automatic stopping rule. This is a specialist shared decision based on very-low-certainty evidence.

Use the withdrawal monitoring schedule above. The guideline implementation text calls for immediate restart if HBV DNA is ≥10,000 IU/mL regardless of ALT, ALT is ≥5 times ULN regardless of DNA, bilirubin is >2.5 mg/dL, or hepatic decompensation develops. Restart may also be appropriate for ordinary treatment indications, extrahepatic disease, or patient preference.

October 2026 HBV update: bepirovirsen

FDA approved bepirovirsen (Hibsago) on October 20, 2026, after the AASLD guideline. It is added to nucleos(t)ide therapy in selected noncirrhotic adults treated for at least 6 months, with suppressed HBV DNA and HBsAg ≤3,000 IU/mL by an FDA-authorized test. Specialist selection and dependable laboratory access are essential.

The FDA label specifies 300 mg subcutaneously (two 150 mg injections) twice weekly in weeks 1–2, 3–4 days apart, then weekly through week 24. Continue nucleos(t)ide therapy for at least 24 weeks after the last bepirovirsen dose; withdrawal requires all section 2.7 criteria and at least one year of close follow-up. Preserve HIV treatment when applicable.

Contraindications include acute hepatic impairment, cirrhosis, and baseline platelets <100 × 109/L. Boxed warnings concern hepatic flares and thrombocytopenia. Obtain baseline viral, hepatic, platelet, and renal assessment, including eGFR and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio. Minimum monitoring is platelets weekly, ALT/bilirubin weekly through week 12 then every other week, and eGFR every other week through week 24. Abnormal results require the label's intensified testing and interruption/discontinuation rules.

No dose adjustment is recommended for mild renal impairment; safety data are insufficient in moderate/severe renal impairment, pregnancy, and HCV coinfection. HDV coinfection and concomitant peginterferon are not recommended. Review anticoagulants, antiplatelets, platelet-lowering drugs, and nephrotoxic drugs. Hold treatment and obtain platelets promptly for new bleeding; unexplained immune-mediated toxicity requires permanent discontinuation. This is a distinct labeled pathway, not permission for routine antiviral withdrawal.
