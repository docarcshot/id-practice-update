HIV care begins with a diagnosis, an immune assessment, and a durable treatment plan

This case-based primer is for residents and ID fellows, with an attending update pathway. The first block covers HIV diagnosis, ART, prevention, and longitudinal care. The second is a practical guide to opportunistic infections (OIs) and related infections. All cases are fictional teaching examples.

Resident essentials

Work through diagnosis, initial ART, OI recognition, and discharge planning. Allow 60-75 minutes for the core and 30-45 minutes for the OI workshop.

Advanced pathway

Add resistance, injectable treatment, ART timing controversies, and the original-trial discussion. Allow another 20-30 minutes.

After completing this module, you should be able to

  1. Interpret discordant HIV tests and select the next diagnostic test.
  2. Select an initial ART strategy using resistance history, HBV status, organ function, and interactions.
  3. Build a syndrome-directed OI evaluation and specify treatment, ART timing, and prophylaxis.
  4. Distinguish virologic failure, treatment toxicity, another diagnosis, and IRIS.
  5. Deliver a discharge and prevention plan that preserves medication access and follow-up.

Use the testing sequence and recognize acute HIV before antibodies are fully detectable

HIV RNA reflects viral replication; CD4 count helps estimate immune vulnerability. A high CD4 count does not remove the indication for treatment, and a low CD4 count does not establish the cause of an acute illness. Acute HIV may resemble influenza, COVID-19, EBV/CMV mononucleosis, or secondary syphilis, with fever, rash, pharyngitis, lymphadenopathy, diarrhea, or aseptic meningitis. [1] [2]

Send specimens before ART when feasible. In a clinically suitable patient, do not wait for CD4 or genotype results to start an appropriate rapid-start regimen. If HBV results are pending, use a regimen with HBV activity. CNS cryptococcosis and TB meningitis are important exceptions to reflex immediate ART. [4] [7] [3]

Advanced HIV is a prompt to look for an OI, not an instruction to send every available PCR. Choose specimens from the involved organ and identify which result would change treatment.

Choose an effective complete regimen and account for the exceptions

HIV integrates into host DNA and persists in a reservoir that current ART does not eradicate. NRTIs and NNRTIs inhibit reverse transcription, INSTIs inhibit integration, and protease inhibitors prevent viral maturation. Ritonavir and cobicistat commonly serve as pharmacokinetic boosters; they are not interchangeable with an active drug in the regimen. Capsid and entry-directed agents have specialized roles. [8]

For most treatment-naive adults without prior long-acting cabotegravir exposure, initial treatment uses bictegravir/TAF/FTC or dolutegravir plus two nucleos(t)ide agents. Here, TAF/TDF are tenofovir formulations, FTC is emtricitabine, and 3TC is lamivudine. Renal dysfunction, pregnancy, resistance, and interacting drugs can change the choice. [8]

Fellow and attending discussion

ART trial endpoints usually measure virologic suppression, not superiority in mortality. A switch study in already suppressed patients does not establish a rapid-start regimen. Distinguish the genetic barrier of a drug from the actual activity of the complete regimen against a patient’s archived virus. [8] [14]

Repeat HIV RNA about 4-8 weeks after initiation or a regimen change, then every 4-8 weeks until suppressed. Stable suppression generally permits monitoring every 3-4 months, sometimes every 6 months after sustained stability. Follow CD4 more closely when it determines OI prevention. [16]

For confirmed rebound at or above 200 copies/mL, assess access, adherence, interactions, and resistance. Obtain genotype while the failing regimen is still being taken, or preferably within 4 weeks of stopping oral ART. Review all historical resistance. A single low detectable result is not automatically failure. Do not add one active drug to a failing regimen. [14]

OI next steps start with the syndrome and severity

Second teaching block · OI diagnosis and treatment

Doses below are adult starting examples, not individualized orders. Adjust for renal/hepatic function, weight, pregnancy, formulation, and drug interactions; confirm with pharmacy.

CD4 thresholds change probability; they are not exclusion rules. Bacterial infections and TB can occur at any CD4 count. Below 200, PCP becomes prominent; below 100, consider toxoplasmosis and cryptococcosis; below 50, disseminated MAC and CMV end-organ disease become more likely. Histoplasmosis is particularly important with advanced HIV and relevant geography. [6] [5] [7] [17] [18] [19] [3]

Source control still matters. Drain an ordinary bacterial abscess, remove infected hardware when indicated, and obtain tissue when it will distinguish infection from malignancy. In cryptococcal meningitis, pressure management with therapeutic LP is essential alongside antifungal therapy.

PCP treatment depends on respiratory severity and is followed by secondary prophylaxis

Confirm with respiratory staining or PCR on an appropriate specimen. PCR may detect colonization; beta-D-glucan supports but does not establish the diagnosis. A normal chest radiograph does not exclude PCP. Consider coinfection. [6]

Treat for 21 days with TMP-SMX, dosed by TMP at 15-20 mg/kg/day, divided every 6-8 hours IV for moderate/severe disease; oral therapy is appropriate for mild disease or after improvement. Adjust for renal function. Monitor potassium, creatinine, CBC, liver tests, and rash. [6]

Add prednisone if room-air PaO₂ is <70 mmHg or the A-a gradient is ≥35 mmHg. Start ideally within 72 hours: 40 mg twice daily on days 1-5, 40 mg daily on days 6-10, then 20 mg daily on days 11-21. [6]

Alternatives include clindamycin-primaquine or IV pentamidine for moderate/severe disease, and atovaquone for mild disease. Check G6PD before primaquine or dapsone. Start ART within 2 weeks when feasible; begin secondary prophylaxis immediately after treatment. [6]

Before escalating for apparent failure, reassess oxygenation, absorption, toxicity, fluid status, pneumothorax, and another infection. Clinical worsening is not itself proof of resistance.

Cryptococcal meningitis requires antifungal treatment and active pressure management

Send serum and CSF CrAg and CSF fungal culture; measure opening pressure. A negative meningitis-panel PCR does not exclude cryptococcosis. Symptomatic elevated pressure requires therapeutic LPs, often repeated daily. Do not use serial CrAg titers to document clearance. [7]

U.S. preferred induction is liposomal amphotericin B 3-4 mg/kg IV daily plus flucytosine 25 mg/kg PO every 6 hours for 2 weeks, with renal adjustment and drug-level monitoring for flucytosine. Follow CBC, renal function, potassium, and magnesium. Reassess CSF culture at about days 7 and 14. [7]

Consolidate with fluconazole 800 mg daily for at least 8 weeks; once stable with sterile CSF and ART initiated, 400 mg daily may be used. Then fluconazole 200 mg daily for maintenance. Persistent positive cultures or instability require a revised induction/consolidation plan. [7]

Defer ART approximately 4-6 weeks after antifungal initiation. Stop maintenance only after ≥1 year of antifungals, absent symptoms, CD4 ≥100, and suppressed HIV RNA. Avoid routine steroids, acetazolamide, or mannitol for cryptococcal pressure management. [7]

Fellow and attending discussion

The single-dose liposomal amphotericin strategy studied in African settings includes 14 days of flucytosine and high-dose fluconazole. It is not one-dose monotherapy. The care setting and monitoring requirements matter. [7]

Treat likely toxoplasma encephalitis while preserving a path to another diagnosis

In advanced HIV, multiple enhancing lesions plus positive Toxoplasma IgG support a presumptive diagnosis. MRI is more sensitive than CT. Negative IgG or CSF PCR does not absolutely exclude disease. Consider lymphoma, TB, bacterial abscess, and fungal infection. [5]

Preferred acute therapy is TMP-SMX at TMP 5 mg/kg plus SMX 25 mg/kg IV or PO twice daily, or pyrimethamine-sulfadiazine-leucovorin. For the latter, give pyrimethamine 200 mg once, then 50 mg daily if ≤60 kg or 75 mg daily if >60 kg; sulfadiazine 1 g or 1.5 g every 6 hours respectively; leucovorin 10-25 mg daily. Treat ≥6 weeks, longer for extensive or incompletely resolved disease. [5]

Reconsider the diagnosis and biopsy if deteriorating early or not improving by 10-14 days. Use steroids only for clinically important edema/mass effect; lymphoma can also improve with steroids. Treat seizures if present. [5]

ART often begins around 2-3 weeks; evidence for an exact interval is limited. Maintenance options include TMP-SMX DS twice daily. Stop after completed acute therapy, no symptoms, and CD4 >200 for >6 months on ART. [5]

Diagnose CMV end-organ disease in the organ involved

Retinitis requires expert retinal examination. Plasma CMV PCR neither confirms nor excludes it. GI disease generally requires compatible endoscopy and tissue evidence; shedding or DNAemia alone is insufficient. Arrange baseline ophthalmology when CD4 <100 and urgent assessment for new visual symptoms. [18]

Retinitis induction uses valganciclovir 900 mg PO twice daily or ganciclovir 5 mg/kg IV every 12 hours for 14-21 days, guided by retinal response. Sight-threatening lesions may also require intravitreal therapy. Maintenance is usually valganciclovir 900 mg daily. Adjust for renal function and monitor CBC and creatinine. [18]

For colitis/esophagitis, IV ganciclovir may transition to oral valganciclovir when absorption is adequate; treat 21-42 days until clinical resolution. Maintenance is usually unnecessary after resolved GI disease unless concurrent retinitis, recurrence, or severe initial disease. Start ART within 1-2 weeks for most CMV end-organ disease. [18]

Retinitis maintenance can stop with ophthalmology after ≥3 months of therapy, inactive lesions, and CD4 ≥100 for ≥3 months on ART. Do not prescribe valganciclovir primary prophylaxis solely for low CD4 or positive blood PCR. [18]

Distinguish disseminated MAC, histoplasmosis, and TB before choosing long treatment

Disseminated MAC

Obtain mycobacterial blood culture or involved-tissue culture and macrolide susceptibility. A respiratory isolate alone does not establish disseminated disease. Use clarithromycin 500 mg PO twice daily plus ethambutol 15 mg/kg daily; azithromycin 500-600 mg daily is an alternative when interactions, intolerance, or adherence preclude clarithromycin. Consider rifabutin as a third agent in severe/high-burden disease or without viral suppression, adjusting for ART interactions. Start ART promptly, preferably with MAC treatment. [17]

Treat ≥12 months and until asymptomatic with CD4 >100 for ≥6 months on ART. Follow vision/color discrimination with ethambutol, liver tests, QT/interactions, and clinical response. Persistent symptoms require reassessment of cultures, resistance, another infection, lymphoma, or IRIS. [17]

Disseminated histoplasmosis

Consider with fever, weight loss, cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly, or elevated alkaline phosphatase, especially in the Mississippi/Ohio River valleys. Send urine/serum antigen and obtain fungal cultures or tissue when indicated; antigen cross-reactivity can limit specificity. [19]

Severe disease: liposomal amphotericin B 3 mg/kg IV daily for ≥2 weeks or until improved, then itraconazole 200 mg PO three times daily for 3 days followed by twice daily for ≥12 months. Mild/moderate disease can start itraconazole. Check formulation, interactions, levels after about 2 weeks, liver tests, and serial antigen. CNS disease requires a different, longer amphotericin induction. Start ART promptly after antifungals. [19]

Tuberculosis

Use airborne precautions for suspected pulmonary/laryngeal TB; obtain respiratory AFB smear, culture, NAAT and resistance testing. A negative IGRA does not exclude disease. Standard susceptible-TB therapy uses isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol initially, then isoniazid/rifampin when eligibility is established. Rifamycin-ART interactions must be resolved before prescribing. [3]

Without meningitis, start ART within 2 weeks if CD4 <50 and within 2-8 weeks if ≥50. TB meningitis needs a separate decision: NIH permits ART after ≥2 weeks once controlled, while ATS/CDC/IDSA guidance has favored waiting 8 weeks. Document the rationale with the TB team. [3]

Clinical worsening after ART is a diagnostic problem before it is an IRIS diagnosis

Unmasking IRIS reveals previously unrecognized infection; paradoxical IRIS worsens a treated infection after immune recovery begins. Reassess adherence, drug exposure, resistance, source control, toxicity, and a second process. The timing and a falling HIV RNA support the context but do not prove IRIS. [3] [7] [17] [5]

Usually continue ART and active antimicrobial treatment while investigating. Mild disease may need symptomatic treatment; severe, organ-threatening inflammation may need corticosteroids and expert management. CNS disease requires particular care. Do not use steroids before considering uncontrolled infection or malignancy. [3] [7] [20]

Fellow and attending discussion

In cryptococcal deterioration, repeat LP with opening pressure and fungal culture. Persistent antigen does not establish microbiologic failure. In apparent toxoplasmosis, steroid-responsive lymphoma can mislead. In MAC, lymphadenitis after ART may reflect IRIS but still requires the clinical and microbiologic context. [7] [5] [17]

Oral PrEP requires renal eligibility and serial HIV testing; FTC/TDF generally needs eCrCl ≥60 mL/min and FTC/TAF ≥30. Injectable PrEP avoids tenofovir renal exposure but still requires reliable testing and return visits. Never use a prevention-only regimen as HIV treatment. [29] [30]

For a substantial nonoccupational exposure, start PEP as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours and no later than 72 hours, for 28 days. Do not wait for pending source testing. Preferred adult options include BIC/FTC/TAF or DTG plus an appropriate tenofovir/FTC or 3TC backbone. Obtain baseline testing, contact at about 24 hours, and antigen-antibody plus NAT follow-up at 4-6 and 12 weeks after exposure under the CDC algorithm. Plan direct PEP-to-PrEP transition if risk continues. Occupational exposures follow the employee-health pathway. [32]

Sustained HIV RNA <200 copies/mL on ART prevents sexual HIV transmission (U=U). It does not prevent other STIs and should not be extrapolated to zero risk for breastfeeding or shared injection equipment. For patients starting ART, use another prevention method for at least 6 months and until HIV RNA <200 is documented; confirm sustained suppression. Support ongoing ART access. [33]

Suppression is the beginning of durable HIV care

Review vaccination history, hepatitis prevention/treatment, STI screening, cancer screening, smoking, blood pressure, metabolic health, bone health, mental health, and substance use. Coordinate HIV-specific cervical and anal screening pathways with available follow-up services. [4] [34]

Use current HIV immunization guidance for influenza, COVID-19, pneumococcal, hepatitis A/B, meningococcal, HPV, recombinant zoster, and exposure-based mpox vaccination. Live MMR and varicella vaccines require adequate immune status; avoid at CD4 <200 and assess pregnancy. Check HBV vaccine response when indicated. [34]

Current ACC/AHA multisociety guidance, endorsed by the May 2026 NIH statement, recommends statin primary prevention for people with HIV aged 40-75. Choose the statin and intensity using cardiovascular risk and ART interactions. [35]

A patient leaving with an ART prescription but no way to obtain it does not have a completed discharge plan. Confirm supply through the first outpatient visit, an actual appointment, pending-result ownership, and a contingency if a dose or visit is missed.

New options expand delivery choices but do not remove eligibility requirements

Long-acting CAB/RPV is a complete injectable maintenance regimen given monthly or every 2 months for appropriately selected patients, generally with HIV RNA <50 for at least 3 months. Review suppression, all resistance and failure history, HBV treatment, pregnancy, interactions, and ability to attend injections. A written bridging and missed-dose plan is part of prescribing. LATITUDE adds evidence for patients with prior adherence challenges after an initial supported oral-treatment phase. [36] [37] [38]

Lenacapavir has separate prevention and treatment roles. Yeztugo is the prevention product. Sunlenca is used with other active antiretrovirals in heavily treatment-experienced adults with multidrug-resistant HIV; it is not stand-alone treatment or routine initial ART. A long pharmacologic tail can expose virus to functional monotherapy when companion drugs or visits are missed. [31] [39]

Doravirine/islatravir (Idvynso), approved in April 2026, is a once-daily 100/0.25 mg two-drug switch regimen for suppressed adults on stable ART without prior virologic treatment failure or known doravirine resistance substitutions. It is not a rapid-start regimen. Strong CYP3A inducers and concomitant 3TC/FTC are contraindicated; it does not provide an HBV treatment backbone. [40]

Fellow and attending discussion

When discussing an investigational strategy, specify the population, stage of development, comparator, and outcome. Longer dosing intervals are not themselves evidence of better clinical outcomes, a cure, or an adequate resistance barrier. The selected updates here are not an exhaustive pipeline review.

Read the endpoint and population before applying a trial

START established the benefit of treatment at high CD4 counts

2015 · Randomized trial · 4,685 adults

Immediate ART versus deferral in asymptomatic adults with CD4 >500. Serious AIDS/non-AIDS events or death occurred in 1.8% versus 4.1%; HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.30-0.62. [41]

Supports treating all people with HIV. It does not answer ART timing during cryptococcal meningitis.

A5164 supports early ART in many acute OIs

2009 · Randomized strategy trial · 282 participants

Early ART at median 12 days versus deferred ART at median 45 days. AIDS progression/death occurred in 14% versus 24%; this was a secondary outcome, and the primary ordered endpoint was not significantly different. [42]

Most participants had PCP; TB was excluded. Do not generalize the average result to every CNS infection.

COAT demonstrates why cryptococcal meningitis is an exception

2014 · Randomized trial · 177 adults

ART at 1-2 weeks versus approximately 5 weeks after diagnosis. Mortality at 26 weeks was 45% versus 30%; HR 1.73, 95% CI 1.06-2.82. [43]

Early immune restoration can harm in this setting. Antifungal regimens and care setting affect applicability, but the trial supports delayed ART.

REPRIEVE supports cardiovascular prevention during treated HIV

2023 · Randomized trial · 7,769 participants

Pitavastatin 4 mg daily versus placebo in treated HIV with low-to-moderate cardiovascular risk. MACE rates were 4.81 versus 7.32 per 1,000 person-years; HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.48-0.90. [44]

Muscle symptoms and diabetes were more frequent with pitavastatin. Use current prevention guidance and interaction checks rather than extrapolating one dose to every statin.

PURPOSE 1 established strong preventive efficacy in young women

2024 · Randomized phase 3 trial

No incident HIV infections among 2,134 receiving lenacapavir in the primary analysis; incidence 0 per 100 person-years, 95% CI 0-0.19. Oral FTC/TAF and FTC/TDF groups had infections. [45]

The zero-event finding is an observed trial result, not a guarantee of lifelong or perfect protection. Oral adherence and modeled background incidence matter to interpretation.

PURPOSE 2 extends preventive evidence to men and gender-diverse people

2024 online publication · Randomized phase 3 trial

Two infections in the lenacapavir arm and nine with FTC/TDF in the primary report. The incidence ratio versus estimated background incidence was 0.04 (95% CI 0.01-0.18); versus FTC/TDF, 0.11 (0.02-0.51). [46]

Background incidence is not a randomized untreated control. Breakthrough infection and capsid resistance remain possible.

LATITUDE supports monthly injections after a supported oral phase

2026 · Randomized open-label trial · 453 enrolled in step 1; 306 randomized in step 2

Participants who achieved HIV RNA ≤200 during supported oral treatment could be randomized. Week-48 regimen failure was 22.8% with CAB/RPV versus 41.2% with oral standard care; difference -18.4 percentage points, 98.4% CI -32.4 to -4.3. [38]

The endpoint includes virologic failure or permanent discontinuation. This was not immediate injectable treatment of all unsuppressed patients, and the care support is part of applicability.

Trial summaries use the linked original publications or indexed abstracts. Full-text access was not available for every trial; no unreviewed subgroup analysis is asserted.

Use these cases to practice a defensible next step

Case 1 · An acute viral syndrome with discordant testing

A 26-year-old has fever, rash, pharyngitis, a reactive antigen-antibody screen, and negative antibody differentiation.

What is the next test, and what history changes empiric ART?
Discuss the next step

Obtain HIV RNA and ask about oral/injectable PrEP and PEP. Repeat an ambiguous result on a new specimen. If HIV followed CAB PrEP, request integrase genotype and use the recommended boosted-darunavir strategy while awaiting results. [1]

Case 2 · New HIV with hypoxemic pneumonia

A 42-year-old has CD4 38, diffuse ground-glass opacity, and room-air PaO₂ 62 mmHg.

What should happen today, and when should ART begin?
Discuss the next step

Obtain respiratory PCP testing while starting treatment-dose TMP-SMX and adjunctive prednisone. Assess bacterial/TB coinfection and oxygen needs. Plan ART within 2 weeks and subsequent PCP prophylaxis. [6]

Case 3 · Headache before rapid ART

A 35-year-old with CD4 24 has 2 weeks of headache and a positive serum CrAg.

Is same-day ART the next step?
Discuss the next step

First evaluate CNS disease with LP, including opening pressure and fungal culture, after imaging if indicated. Begin appropriate antifungals and manage pressure. CNS cryptococcosis usually calls for ART deferral around 4-6 weeks. [7]

Case 4 · Ring-enhancing lesions that do not improve

A patient with CD4 48, positive Toxoplasma IgG, and multiple lesions receives appropriate therapy but is unchanged at day 14.

What should prevent automatic extension of empiric therapy?
Discuss the next step

Reassess exposure and adherence and pursue alternative diagnoses, particularly CNS lymphoma. Discuss biopsy. A presumptive toxoplasma diagnosis should have a defined reassessment point. [5]

Case 5 · CMV PCR distracts from the involved organ

A patient with CD4 19 has diarrhea and a positive plasma CMV PCR.

Does this establish CMV colitis?
Discuss the next step

No. Obtain a syndrome-directed stool evaluation and consider endoscopy with biopsy. Ask about vision and arrange ophthalmology as indicated. Positive blood PCR does not by itself justify treatment for tissue disease. [18]

Case 6 · Fever and cytopenias before MAC prophylaxis

A patient newly diagnosed with HIV has CD4 12, fever, weight loss, cytopenias, and elevated alkaline phosphatase.

Should weekly azithromycin be started immediately?
Discuss the next step

Evaluate active MAC and disseminated histoplasmosis, with appropriate cultures, antigen testing, and tissue when needed. MAC prophylaxis can expose active disease to monotherapy. Severe histoplasmosis warrants prompt amphotericin-based treatment. [17] [19]

Case 7 · A suppressed patient wants fewer pills

A patient with HIV/HBV is suppressed on tenofovir-containing ART and requests CAB/RPV.

What cannot be lost in the switch?
Discuss the next step

HBV treatment. Review resistance and the rest of injectable eligibility, then preserve or separately provide appropriate HBV therapy. CAB/RPV itself does not treat HBV. [12] [36]

Case 8 · A new exposure presents overnight

An HIV-negative patient presents 18 hours after a substantial sexual exposure; the source result is pending.

What action is time sensitive?
Discuss the next step

Start indicated PEP without waiting, obtain baseline testing, provide the full 28-day plan, and arrange follow-up and a PrEP transition discussion. [32]

Check the decision and explain why it is justified

1. Can a negative antigen-antibody test exclude acute HIV after a recent exposure?

Show explanation

No. Obtain HIV RNA when the exposure and clinical syndrome justify concern; repeat testing as indicated. [1]

2. Can DTG/3TC be the default before HBV and genotype results?

Show explanation

No. Select a suitable rapid-start regimen with HBV activity while those results are pending. [8]

3. Does every patient with CD4 <50 need azithromycin prophylaxis?

Show explanation

No. Effective ART started immediately removes the routine indication; evaluate symptoms for active MAC before prophylaxis. [17]

4. What monitoring item is easy to miss with TMP-SMX?

Show explanation

Potassium, in addition to renal function, blood counts, rash, and other toxicity assessment. [6]

5. Does PCP prophylaxis have the same threshold in untreated and stably treated HIV?

Show explanation

No. For patients not on ART or starting ART, use CD4 <200. Restart decisions on ART incorporate CD4 and the viral-load pattern. [6]

6. Should ART start immediately for cryptococcal meningitis?

Show explanation

Usually no. Treat cryptococcosis and elevated intracranial pressure first; ART generally starts around 4-6 weeks. [7]

7. Does persistent CrAg mean induction has failed?

Show explanation

No. Follow clinical response, opening pressure, and CSF fungal cultures; antigen can persist. [7]

8. Can a negative plasma CMV PCR exclude CMV retinitis?

Show explanation

No. Retinal examination establishes the diagnosis in the appropriate clinical setting. [18]

9. Can a prevention lenacapavir injection treat established HIV by itself?

Show explanation

No. Treatment requires a fully active combination selected for treatment history and resistance. [39]

10. Does LATITUDE prove that all viremic patients should immediately receive CAB/RPV?

Show explanation

No. Randomization followed an initial supported oral phase and achievement of the trial viral-load threshold. Eligibility and implementation support matter. [38]

11. What is the definitive treatment principle for HIV-associated PML?

Show explanation

Rapid effective ART and immune restoration. There is no established JCV-specific antiviral regimen. [20]

12. What makes the discharge plan complete?

Show explanation

Medication supply through follow-up, a confirmed appointment, named ownership of pending results and toxicity monitoring, and clear return precautions. [2]

Communicate the HIV and OI plans as linked but separate decisions

Consult-note framework

HIV diagnosis and treatment history: [confirmed tests; prior ART/PrEP; prior resistance]. Current HIV RNA [ ] and CD4 [ ]. Current syndrome: [site and severity], with [leading diagnosis] and [alternatives]. Diagnostic plan: [specimens], with [result or reassessment date] that would change treatment. OI treatment: [drug, dose, route, renal adjustment, start date, duration, and maintenance plan]. ART: [complete regimen] starting [date], or deferred because [specific reason and reassessment date]. Monitoring: [toxicity tests and timing], interactions reviewed with [ ]. Prevention: [PCP/toxoplasma/MAC decision], vaccinations and partner prevention as appropriate. Transition: [medication supply], [HIV appointment], [pending-result owner], and [return precautions].

Primary-team communication example

“The pulmonary presentation is concerning for PCP. We are treating while respiratory testing is pending and have assessed the steroid threshold. Please keep the ART plan and start date visible in the discharge summary. Before discharge, we need both the treatment supply and the subsequent prophylaxis prescription, with HIV follow-up confirmed.”

Local confirmation needed: laboratory reflex HIV algorithm and resistance-panel components; access to flucytosine/pyrimethamine and antifungal drug levels; ART formulary and emergency supply; HIV clinic/ADAP access; ophthalmology, endoscopy, TB and public-health contacts.

HIV and OIs · One-page rounds summary

Teaching companion · September 15, 2026 · Faculty/local review pending. Use the full module for dosing, eligibility, evidence, and exceptions.

Confirm and stage

Ag/Ab → differentiation → RNA for discordance. Suspected acute HIV can need RNA despite a negative screen. Obtain CD4, RNA, genotype, organ function, HBV/HCV, and prior ART/PrEP history.

Start appropriate ART

Most: BIC/FTC/TAF or DTG + two NRTIs. Do not rapid-start DTG/3TC before HBV/genotype results. Prior CAB PrEP needs integrase genotype and an appropriate interim regimen. Preserve HBV treatment.

Recognize the OI

Pneumonia: PCP, bacteria, TB. Headache: CrAg/CSF with opening pressure. Focal deficit: MRI, toxoplasma/lymphoma/PML differential. Visual symptoms: urgent ophthalmology. Fever/cytopenias: MAC, histoplasmosis, TB, malignancy.

Match ART timing to disease

PCP: within 2 weeks. Cryptococcal meningitis: generally 4-6 weeks. TB without meningitis: within 2 weeks if CD4 <50; 2-8 weeks otherwise. TB meningitis: individualized guideline-informed plan. PML: immediate effective ART.

Prescribe prevention correctly

PCP: CD4 <200 if not on or starting ART. Toxoplasma: IgG+ and CD4 <100. MAC prophylaxis usually unnecessary with immediate effective ART; exclude active disease first. Secondary suppression has organism-specific stopping criteria.

Monitor and hand off

TMP-SMX: K+, creatinine, CBC. Amphotericin: renal function, K+/Mg. Flucytosine: CBC, renal adjustment/levels. Check interactions and absorption. Repeat HIV RNA at 4-8 weeks. Confirm medication supply, appointment, and result ownership.

Use prevention opportunities

Substantial exposure: indicated PEP immediately, no later than 72 hours, for 28 days. Arrange follow-up and PrEP transition. PrEP options include oral agents, CAB, and six-monthly LEN. Sustained suppression prevents sexual HIV transmission.

Know when to reconsider

No toxoplasma improvement by 10-14 days, new visual loss, rising oxygen need, persistent cultures, or new CNS symptoms require reassessment. Positive CMV blood PCR is not tissue disease; persistent CrAg is not proof of failure.
