Link the HIV diagnosis, immune assessment, and immediate clinical problem

Resident core · 30–45 minutes with discussion

The resident cases turn on three questions: is this acute HIV, does the presenting illness change when ART should begin, and what care must continue after discharge? The advanced section compares treatment phases, maintenance criteria, newer therapies, and the evidence behind them.

A positive HIV test is the beginning of the assessment. HIV RNA, CD4 count, symptoms, medication history, and access to continuing treatment determine what needs to happen next.

Confirm the diagnosis without missing acute infection

Begin with a laboratory HIV-1/2 antigen-antibody test, followed by an HIV-1/HIV-2 differentiation assay when reactive. If the differentiation assay is negative or indeterminate, obtain HIV RNA to resolve possible acute infection. With a compatible recent exposure or acute retroviral syndrome, order RNA even when the screening test is negative.

Recent PrEP or PEP, especially a long-acting product, can change test interpretation and resistance risk. In a person taking PrEP with negative antibody testing, a low-positive RNA below 200 copies/mL needs confirmation on a new specimen before ART. At RNA ≥200 copies/mL during PrEP, prompt initiation of an effective, complete ART regimen is recommended while confirmation is pending.

Collect HIV RNA, CD4, genotype, hepatitis serologies, renal/hepatic assessment, and other indicated baseline studies. Screen for symptoms suggesting an OI. These studies usually can be collected while arranging rapid ART; do not create an avoidable delay in an otherwise appropriate patient.

Sources: early, baseline, initiation.

Start a complete ART regimen and identify the exceptions

HBV screening and serology

For most people starting ART, an integrase inhibitor with two appropriate NRTIs is a standard approach. Common examples are bictegravir/TAF/FTC or dolutegravir with TAF/FTC or TDF/FTC when suitable. Selection depends on organ function, pregnancy, resistance risk, hepatitis B, interactions, and access.

Dolutegravir/lamivudine is a recommended initial option when HBV infection and relevant resistance have been excluded. The September 24, 2026 NIH update removed the previous baseline HIV RNA ceiling. Do not use it for rapid start while HBV and genotype results are pending. HIV/HBV coinfection generally requires two HBV-active components, usually tenofovir plus FTC or 3TC, in a fully suppressive HIV regimen. Stopping HBV-active drugs can precipitate hepatitis.

Prior long-acting cabotegravir (CAB-LA) PrEP raises the possibility of integrase resistance. If ART is needed before an integrase genotype is available, a boosted darunavir regimen with two appropriate nucleos(t)ide agents allows prompt treatment without assuming an integrase inhibitor will be active. Pregnancy and drug interactions affect the booster choice.

Timing matters: prompt ART is the usual approach, including during treatment of many OIs. Cryptococcal meningitis and TB meningitis require a separate timing plan because early immune recovery in the CNS can be harmful.

Sources: arv, hbv, initiation.

Treat PCP according to respiratory severity and plan what follows

For most adults with HIV-associated PCP, the preferred treatment is TMP-SMX for 21 days. Moderate or severe disease usually calls for IV treatment initially; oral treatment may be suitable for milder disease or after improvement. Renal function affects exposure. The regimen used to prevent PCP is not adequate treatment for active pneumonia.

Room-air PaO₂ below 70 mm Hg or an A–a gradient of at least 35 mm Hg identifies patients who benefit from adjunctive corticosteroids. Begin steroids promptly, ideally within 72 hours of starting PCP therapy. When feasible, ART begins within 2 weeks of the PCP diagnosis. Secondary prophylaxis follows immediately after treatment.

A rising oxygen requirement prompts a second look at coinfection, drug exposure, toxicity, and the expected early course. Potassium, renal function, blood counts, and rash help distinguish treatment toxicity from progression. The advanced section compares alternatives by severity.

Sources: pcp.

Check delivery and interactions before declaring the regimen ineffective

Ask whether the person has the medicines and can take them consistently. Review antacids and mineral supplements with integrase inhibitors, acid suppressants with rilpivirine, rifamycins, anticonvulsants, and all nonprescription products. Food requirements and absorption matter.

Recheck HIV RNA after treatment starts or changes according to the monitoring guideline, commonly within 4–8 weeks, and follow until suppressed. With persistent viremia, assess adherence, interactions, absorption, and resistance before changing therapy. Do not add a single active drug to a failing regimen.

Discharge planning should name the HIV clinician, provide uninterrupted medication access, specify pending-result ownership, and distinguish the ART, OI treatment, and prophylaxis stop or reassessment dates. Suppression is the start of ongoing preventive care.

Sources: monitor, failure, interactions.

Use three cases to identify the immediate decision

Case 1 · A discordant diagnostic sequence

A symptomatic patient with a recent exposure has a reactive antigen-antibody test and a negative differentiation assay.

Discuss the next action and the finding that would change it.

Discussion

The negative differentiation assay does not exclude acute HIV. HIV RNA resolves the next diagnostic question. Recent PrEP or PEP can alter the pattern; in particular, a low-positive RNA below 200 copies/mL with negative antibodies during PrEP needs confirmation on a new specimen before treatment.

Case 2 · Low CD4 with hypoxemic pneumonia

A patient with newly diagnosed HIV has CD4 42, diffuse opacities, and room-air PaO2 64 mm Hg.

Discuss the next action and the finding that would change it.

Discussion

With a room-air PaO₂ of 64 mm Hg, this patient meets the oxygen threshold for adjunctive steroids if PCP is suspected. Treatment should not wait for a respiratory specimen result when suspicion is high. Bacterial pneumonia and TB remain possible. If feasible, ART begins within 2 weeks of PCP diagnosis; secondary prophylaxis follows the acute course.

Case 3 · Meningitis changes the ART clock

A patient with advanced HIV has a positive CSF cryptococcal antigen and a high opening pressure.

Discuss the next action and the finding that would change it.

Discussion

The positive CSF antigen and elevated opening pressure make this a CNS infection. Urgent antifungal induction and therapeutic lumbar punctures address separate threats. Persistent signs of raised pressure call for prompt repeat drainage. Clinical response and CSF cultures guide the next treatment phase. If the person is not already taking ART, the usual time to start is 4–6 weeks after antifungal treatment begins.

Use the testing sequence and recognize acute HIV before antibodies are fully detectable

HIV RNA reflects viral replication; CD4 count helps estimate immune vulnerability. A high CD4 count does not remove the indication for treatment, and a low CD4 count does not establish the cause of an acute illness. Acute HIV may resemble influenza, COVID-19, EBV/CMV mononucleosis, or secondary syphilis, with fever, rash, pharyngitis, lymphadenopathy, diarrhea, or aseptic meningitis. [1] [2]

Send specimens before ART when feasible. In a clinically suitable patient, do not wait for CD4 or genotype results to start an appropriate rapid-start regimen. If HBV results are pending, use a regimen with HBV activity. CNS cryptococcosis and TB meningitis are important exceptions to reflex immediate ART. [4] [7] [3]

Advanced HIV is a prompt to look for an OI, not an instruction to send every available PCR. Choose specimens from the involved organ and identify which result would change treatment.

Choose an effective complete regimen and account for the exceptions

HIV integrates into host DNA and persists in a reservoir that current ART does not eradicate. NRTIs and NNRTIs inhibit reverse transcription, INSTIs inhibit integration, and protease inhibitors prevent viral maturation. Ritonavir and cobicistat commonly serve as pharmacokinetic boosters; they are not interchangeable with an active drug in the regimen. Capsid and entry-directed agents have specialized roles. [8]

For most treatment-naive adults without prior long-acting cabotegravir exposure, recommended initial options include bictegravir/TAF/FTC, dolutegravir plus two nucleos(t)ide agents, or dolutegravir/lamivudine when the exclusions below do not apply. Here, TAF/TDF are tenofovir formulations, FTC is emtricitabine, and 3TC is lamivudine. Renal dysfunction, pregnancy, resistance, and interacting drugs can change the choice. [8]

Repeat HIV RNA about 4-8 weeks after initiation or a regimen change, then every 4-8 weeks until suppressed. Stable suppression generally permits monitoring every 3-4 months, sometimes every 6 months after sustained stability. Follow CD4 more closely when it determines OI prevention. [16]

OI next steps start with the syndrome and severity

Second teaching block · OI diagnosis and treatment

Which organ is involved? How severe is the illness? These questions determine the initial treatment phase and whether rapid ART will help or harm. Formulation, organ function, pregnancy, and interacting drugs can change the regimen.

CD4 thresholds change probability; they are not exclusion rules. Bacterial infections and TB can occur at any CD4 count. Below 200, PCP becomes prominent; below 100, consider toxoplasmosis and cryptococcosis; below 50, disseminated MAC and CMV end-organ disease become more likely. Histoplasmosis is particularly important with advanced HIV and relevant geography. [6] [5] [7] [17] [18] [19] [3]

Source control still matters. Drain an ordinary bacterial abscess, remove infected hardware when indicated, and obtain tissue when it will distinguish infection from malignancy. In cryptococcal meningitis, pressure management with therapeutic LP is essential alongside antifungal therapy.

PCP treatment depends on respiratory severity and is followed by secondary prophylaxis

Confirm with respiratory staining or PCR on an appropriate specimen. PCR may detect colonization; beta-D-glucan supports but does not establish the diagnosis. A normal chest radiograph does not exclude PCP. Consider coinfection. [6]

TMP-SMX is the preferred treatment for most people with HIV-associated PCP, usually for 21 days. IV therapy is generally preferred initially in moderate or severe illness. Oral treatment can be suitable for milder disease or after clinical improvement. Renal function affects the regimen; potassium, creatinine, CBC, liver tests and rash help identify toxicity. [6]

Adjunctive prednisone is indicated for documented or suspected PCP with room-air PaO₂ <70 mm Hg or an A–a gradient ≥35 mm Hg. Begin promptly, ideally within 72 hours of starting PCP treatment. The course is tapered; the linked treatment guidance gives the regimen. [6]

Clindamycin plus primaquine or IV pentamidine are alternatives for moderate or severe disease; atovaquone is an oral option for mild-to-moderate disease. Check G6PD activity before primaquine or dapsone. G6PD deficiency calls for an alternative because of hemolysis and methemoglobinemia risk. When feasible, ART begins within 2 weeks of PCP diagnosis. Secondary prophylaxis begins when the initial treatment course ends. [6]

Before escalating for apparent failure, reassess oxygenation, absorption, toxicity, fluid status, pneumothorax, and another infection. Clinical worsening is not itself proof of resistance.

Cryptococcal meningitis requires antifungal treatment and active pressure management

Amphotericin formulations and flucytosine toxicity · Antifungal drug levels

Send serum and CSF CrAg and CSF fungal culture; measure opening pressure. A negative meningitis-panel PCR does not exclude cryptococcosis. Persistent headache or other signs of raised pressure call for urgent repeat therapeutic LP, at least daily until symptoms improve and opening pressure normalizes. NIH strongly advises considering an early repeat LP within 48 hours in patients without headache. It also advises strongly considering repeat LP within 72 hours when symptoms are minimal or the initial pressure is below 25 cm CSF. Do not use serial CrAg titers to document clearance. [7]

In a U.S. setting with access to electrolyte, renal and hematologic monitoring, preferred CNS induction combines IV liposomal amphotericin B with oral flucytosine, usually for 2 weeks. Flucytosine requires renal adjustment and drug-level monitoring where available. Known complete dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD) deficiency is a labeled contraindication. CBC, kidney function, potassium and magnesium track toxicity; follow-up LP at about 1 and/or 2 weeks assesses culture clearance, a separate decision from early pressure management. [7]

After successful induction, fluconazole consolidation lasts at least 8 weeks, before lower-intensity maintenance. A clinically stable patient can start consolidation after the initial 2 weeks while cultures are pending; reducing consolidation intensity requires sterile CSF, clinical stability, and ART initiation. Persistent culture positivity requires reassessment of the antifungal regimen and at least 8 weeks of consolidation counted from documented CSF clearance. Clinical instability is a reason to continue or restart induction rather than advance automatically. [7]

For a person with CNS cryptococcosis who is not already taking ART, treatment for HIV generally begins 4–6 weeks after antifungal initiation. That delay does not apply to all positive serum antigen tests: non-CNS disease follows a separate assessment, with about 2 weeks of antifungal treatment before ART considered prudent. Maintenance can stop only after at least 1 year of antifungal therapy, resolution of symptoms, CD4 ≥100 and suppressed HIV RNA. Therapeutic LPs address symptomatic intracranial hypertension; routine steroids, acetazolamide and mannitol do not substitute for pressure relief. [7]

Fellow and attending discussion

The single-dose liposomal amphotericin strategy studied in African settings includes 14 days of flucytosine and high-dose fluconazole. It is not one-dose monotherapy. The care setting and monitoring requirements matter. [7]

Treat likely toxoplasma encephalitis while preserving a path to another diagnosis

In advanced HIV, multiple enhancing lesions plus positive Toxoplasma IgG support a presumptive diagnosis. MRI is more sensitive than CT. Negative IgG or CSF PCR does not absolutely exclude disease. Consider lymphoma, TB, bacterial abscess, and fungal infection. [5]

Preferred acute options for presumed or confirmed toxoplasma encephalitis include TMP-SMX or pyrimethamine plus sulfadiazine and leucovorin. Leucovorin protects against pyrimethamine-related marrow toxicity. Acute treatment generally lasts at least 6 weeks; extensive disease or incomplete clinical or radiographic response may require longer. [5]

Reconsider the diagnosis and biopsy if deteriorating early or not improving by 10-14 days. Use steroids only for clinically important edema/mass effect; lymphoma can also improve with steroids. Treat seizures if present. [5]

Many clinicians begin ART about 2–3 weeks after toxoplasmosis treatment starts, but there is no trial-defined interval specific to toxoplasma encephalitis. Secondary maintenance, for example with TMP-SMX, follows acute therapy. Stopping requires completion of acute treatment, no symptoms, and CD4 >200 for more than 6 months in response to ART. [5]

Diagnose CMV end-organ disease in the organ involved

Retinitis requires expert retinal examination. Plasma CMV PCR neither confirms nor excludes it. GI disease generally requires compatible endoscopy and tissue evidence; shedding or DNAemia alone is insufficient. Arrange baseline ophthalmology when CD4 <100 and urgent assessment for new visual symptoms. [18]

For retinitis, oral valganciclovir or IV ganciclovir provides systemic induction, generally for 14–21 days and guided by retinal response. Lesions immediately threatening sight require urgent ophthalmology involvement and usually intravitreal therapy in addition to systemic treatment. Maintenance generally uses oral valganciclovir. Renal function and marrow toxicity affect selection and monitoring. [18]

For CMV colitis or esophagitis, IV ganciclovir can transition to oral valganciclovir when absorption is adequate. Treatment generally lasts 21–42 days, until symptoms resolve. Maintenance is usually unnecessary after resolved GI disease unless retinitis, recurrence, or severe initial disease changes that decision. For most end-organ CMV, ART should begin no later than 1–2 weeks after anti-CMV treatment starts. [18]

Retinitis maintenance can stop with ophthalmology after ≥3 months of therapy, inactive lesions, and CD4 ≥100 for ≥3 months on ART. Do not prescribe valganciclovir primary prophylaxis solely for low CD4 or positive blood PCR. [18]

Distinguish disseminated MAC, histoplasmosis, and TB before choosing long treatment

Pulmonary NTM diagnostic criteria

Disseminated MAC

Disseminated MAC requires microbiologic confirmation from blood or involved tissue; a respiratory isolate alone is insufficient. Initial therapy pairs a macrolide with ethambutol to limit resistance. Clarithromycin is one option; azithromycin is an alternative when interactions, intolerance, or feasibility favor it. Macrolide susceptibility matters. Severe or high-burden disease or lack of effective ART can favor adding rifabutin after interaction review. ART should start promptly, preferably when MAC treatment begins. [17]

Treatment is generally at least 12 months. Stopping requires no MAC symptoms and at least 6 months with CD4 >100 and HIV viral suppression on ART. Follow vision/color discrimination with ethambutol, liver tests, QT/interactions, and clinical response. Persistent symptoms require reassessment of cultures, resistance, another infection, lymphoma, or IRIS. [17]

Disseminated histoplasmosis

Consider with fever, weight loss, cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly, or elevated alkaline phosphatase, especially in the Mississippi/Ohio River valleys. Send urine/serum antigen and obtain fungal cultures or tissue when indicated; antigen cross-reactivity can limit specificity. [19]

For severe disseminated histoplasmosis, liposomal amphotericin B provides induction, generally for at least 2 weeks or until clinical improvement, followed by itraconazole for at least 12 months. Mild-to-moderate disease can usually begin with itraconazole. Formulation, interactions and absorption matter; an itraconazole level after about 2 weeks helps assess exposure. Liver tests, symptoms and serial antigen inform follow-up. CNS disease requires a different, longer induction. ART generally starts promptly after antifungals. [19]

Tuberculosis

Use airborne precautions for suspected pulmonary/laryngeal TB; obtain respiratory AFB smear, culture, NAAT and resistance testing. A negative IGRA does not exclude disease. Standard susceptible-TB therapy uses isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol initially, then isoniazid/rifampin when eligibility is established. Rifamycin-ART interactions must be resolved before prescribing. [3]

For active TB without suspected meningitis, ART generally starts within 2 weeks of beginning TB treatment if CD4 is below 50, or within 2–8 weeks if CD4 is at least 50. During pregnancy with active TB, start ART as early as feasible regardless of CD4 count, accounting for pregnancy and rifamycin interactions. Suspected TB meningitis requires a separate timing plan. In TB meningitis, early immune recovery can cause dangerous inflammation in a confined space. NIH guidance allows ART after at least 2 weeks of TB treatment once meningitis is controlled by clinical or CSF improvement. ATS/CDC/IDSA guidance has favored waiting until 8 weeks of TB therapy are complete. This difference merits an individualized plan with the TB/HIV team. [3] [48]

Refractory mucocutaneous HSV

FDA approved pritelivir (Hovilpri) in October 2026 for immunocompromised adults with mucocutaneous HSV refractory to acyclovir, valacyclovir or famciclovir, with or without documented resistance. The prescribing information describes oral treatment until lesions heal. The pivotal trial excluded ocular, CNS and disseminated HSV; it does not establish a role for pritelivir in those conditions or in VZV. [47]

Acid suppression can reduce pritelivir exposure. Avoid esomeprazole and rabeprazole; other proton-pump inhibitors have product-specific limits. H2 blockers and antacids require specific spacing, and BCRP-substrate interactions require review. The label’s interaction and dosing sections give the exact limits and intervals. No adjustment is recommended for mild through severe renal impairment or Child-Pugh A/B disease; hemodialysis and Child-Pugh C were not evaluated. Human pregnancy and lactation data are absent. Headache and liver-enzyme elevations were reported.

Kaposi sarcoma, lymphoma, medication toxicity, inflammatory disease, and ordinary community infections belong in the differential. A positive low-specificity test should not prevent tissue diagnosis when the clinical picture is inconsistent.

Clinical worsening after ART is a diagnostic problem before it is an IRIS diagnosis

Unmasking IRIS reveals previously unrecognized infection; paradoxical IRIS worsens a treated infection after immune recovery begins. Reassess adherence, drug exposure, resistance, source control, toxicity, and a second process. The timing and a falling HIV RNA support the context but do not prove IRIS. [3] [7] [17] [5]

Fellow and attending discussion

In cryptococcal deterioration, repeat LP with opening pressure and fungal culture. Persistent antigen does not establish microbiologic failure. In apparent toxoplasmosis, steroid-responsive lymphoma can mislead. In MAC, lymphadenitis after ART may reflect IRIS but still requires the clinical and microbiologic context. [7] [5] [17]

Oral PrEP requires renal eligibility and serial HIV testing. For initiation, CDC specifies Cockcroft–Gault estimated creatinine clearance (eCrCl) >60 mL/min for FTC/TDF and ≥30 mL/min for FTC/TAF; reassess kidney function during use. The Truvada label advises against FTC/TDF PrEP below 60 mL/min. Injectable PrEP avoids tenofovir renal exposure but still requires reliable testing and return visits. Never use a prevention-only regimen as HIV treatment. [29] [30] [49]

Nonoccupational PEP is indicated after a substantial exposure when the source has HIV without sustained viral suppression or suppression status is unknown. If the source’s HIV status is unknown, the decision is case by case. When indicated, start as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours and no later than 72 hours, for 28 days. Pending laboratory or source results should not delay the first dose. Adult options include BIC/FTC/TAF or DTG with an appropriate tenofovir and FTC or 3TC backbone. Baseline testing, contact at about 24 hours, and follow-up Ag/Ab plus NAT at 4–6 and 12 weeks after exposure follow the CDC algorithm. If risk continues, plan transition to PrEP. Occupational exposures follow a separate pathway. [32]

Sustained HIV RNA <200 copies/mL on ART prevents sexual HIV transmission (U=U). It does not prevent other STIs and should not be extrapolated to zero risk for breastfeeding or shared injection equipment. For patients starting ART, use another prevention method for at least 6 months and until HIV RNA <200 is documented; confirm sustained suppression. Support ongoing ART access. [33]

Suppression is the beginning of durable HIV care

Review vaccination history, hepatitis prevention/treatment, STI screening, cancer screening, smoking, blood pressure, metabolic health, bone health, mental health, and substance use. Coordinate HIV-specific cervical and anal screening pathways with available follow-up services. [4] [34]

Use current HIV immunization guidance for influenza, COVID-19, pneumococcal, hepatitis A/B, meningococcal, HPV, recombinant zoster, and exposure-based mpox vaccination. Live MMR and varicella vaccines require adequate immune status; avoid at CD4 <200 and assess pregnancy. Check HBV vaccine response when indicated. [34]

NIH recommends a statin for primary prevention in people with HIV aged 40–75 on stable ART, regardless of LDL cholesterol or estimated ASCVD risk. Use cardiovascular risk and ART interactions to choose the agent and intensity. For primary prevention, defer initiation during pregnancy or when planning pregnancy and stop if pregnancy occurs. [35]

A patient leaving with an ART prescription but no way to obtain it does not have a completed discharge plan. Confirm supply through the first outpatient visit, an actual appointment, pending-result ownership, and a contingency if a dose or visit is missed.

New options expand delivery choices but do not remove eligibility requirements

Long-acting CAB/RPV is a complete injectable maintenance regimen given monthly or every 2 months for appropriately selected patients, generally with HIV RNA <50 for at least 3 months. Review suppression, all resistance and failure history, HBV treatment, pregnancy, interactions, and ability to attend injections. A written bridging and missed-dose plan is part of prescribing. LATITUDE adds evidence for patients with prior adherence challenges after an initial supported oral-treatment phase. [36] [37] [38]

Before switching off tenofovir, check recent HBV serology. Current HBV infection requires continued HBV-active treatment. For an HBsAg-negative, anti-HBc-positive patient switching to a tenofovir-sparing regimen, NIH recommends ALT monitoring every 1–3 months for the first 6 months and every 6 months thereafter. A rise in ALT prompts HBV DNA testing. Confirmed reactivation requires prompt HBV-active therapy alongside a fully suppressive ART regimen. [12] [36]

Lenacapavir has separate prevention and treatment roles. Yeztugo is the prevention product. Sunlenca is used with other active antiretrovirals in heavily treatment-experienced adults with multidrug-resistant HIV; it is not stand-alone treatment or routine initial ART. A long pharmacologic tail can expose virus to functional monotherapy when companion drugs or visits are missed. [31] [39]

The FDA indication for bictegravir/lenacapavir (Bixlenvo) covers adults with HIV RNA <50 copies/mL on stable ART and no known or suspected resistance to either component. This oral switch uses two days of oral Sunlenca loading with Bixlenvo, then Bixlenvo alone for HIV maintenance. When HBV treatment is indicated, preserve it separately.

Doravirine/islatravir (Idvynso) is a switch option for adults with HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL on stable ART, no prior virologic treatment failure, and no known doravirine resistance substitutions. It is not an initial or rapid-start regimen and does not treat HBV. The U.S. label does not recommend it at eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m² or with Child-Pugh C hepatic impairment; dialysis was not studied. Strong CYP3A inducers and concomitant 3TC or FTC are contraindicated. Rifabutin requires separate supplemental doravirine according to the label, not a second combination tablet. HBV-active therapy must be maintained when indicated. [40]

Fellow and attending discussion

When discussing an investigational strategy, specify the population, stage of development, comparator, and outcome. Longer dosing intervals are not themselves evidence of better clinical outcomes, a cure, or an adequate resistance barrier. The selected updates here are not an exhaustive pipeline review.

Read the endpoint and population before applying a trial

START established the benefit of treatment at high CD4 counts

2015 · Randomized trial · 4,685 adults

Immediate ART versus deferral in asymptomatic adults with CD4 >500. Serious AIDS/non-AIDS events or death occurred in 1.8% versus 4.1%; HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.30-0.62. [41]

Supports treating all people with HIV. It does not answer ART timing during cryptococcal meningitis.

A5164 supports early ART in many acute OIs

2009 · Randomized strategy trial · 282 participants

Early ART at median 12 days versus deferred ART at median 45 days. AIDS progression/death occurred in 14% versus 24%; this was a secondary outcome, and the primary ordered endpoint was not significantly different. [42]

Most participants had PCP; TB was excluded. Do not generalize the average result to every CNS infection.

COAT demonstrates why cryptococcal meningitis is an exception

2014 · Randomized trial · 177 adults

ART at 1-2 weeks versus approximately 5 weeks after diagnosis. Mortality at 26 weeks was 45% versus 30%; HR 1.73, 95% CI 1.06-2.82. [43]

Early immune restoration can harm in this setting. Antifungal regimens and care setting affect applicability, but the trial supports delayed ART.

REPRIEVE supports cardiovascular prevention during treated HIV

2023 · Randomized trial · 7,769 participants

Pitavastatin versus placebo in treated HIV with low-to-moderate cardiovascular risk. MACE rates were 4.81 versus 7.32 per 1,000 person-years; HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.48-0.90. [44]

Muscle symptoms and diabetes were more frequent with pitavastatin. The trial addresses cardiovascular prevention in its enrolled population; the choice of statin still depends on cardiovascular risk and interactions with ART.

PURPOSE 1 established strong preventive efficacy in young women

2024 · Randomized phase 3 trial

No incident HIV infections among 2,134 receiving lenacapavir in the primary analysis; incidence 0 per 100 person-years, 95% CI 0-0.19. Oral FTC/TAF and FTC/TDF groups had infections. [45]

The zero-event finding is an observed trial result, not a guarantee of lifelong or perfect protection. Oral adherence and modeled background incidence matter to interpretation.

PURPOSE 2 extends preventive evidence to men and gender-diverse people

2024 online publication · Randomized phase 3 trial

Two infections in the lenacapavir arm and nine with FTC/TDF in the primary report. The incidence ratio versus estimated background incidence was 0.04 (95% CI 0.01-0.18); versus FTC/TDF, 0.11 (0.02-0.51). [46]

Background incidence is not a randomized untreated control. Breakthrough infection and capsid resistance remain possible.

LATITUDE supports monthly injections after a supported oral phase

2026 · Randomized open-label trial · 453 enrolled in step 1; 306 randomized in step 2

Participants who achieved HIV RNA ≤200 during supported oral treatment could be randomized. Week-48 regimen failure was 22.8% with CAB/RPV versus 41.2% with oral standard care; difference -18.4 percentage points, 98.4% CI -32.4 to -4.3. [38]

The endpoint includes virologic failure or permanent discontinuation. This was not immediate injectable treatment of all unsuppressed patients, and the care support is part of applicability.

Trial summaries use the linked original publications or indexed abstracts. Full-text access was not available for every trial; no unreviewed subgroup analysis is asserted.

Use these cases to practice a defensible next step

Case 1 · An acute viral syndrome with discordant testing

A 26-year-old has fever, rash, pharyngitis, a reactive antigen-antibody screen, and negative antibody differentiation.

What is the next test, and what history changes empiric ART?

Discuss the next step

A reactive screen followed by negative differentiation leaves acute infection possible; HIV RNA and the PrEP/PEP history help resolve it. In a PrEP user with negative antibodies, a low-positive RNA below 200 copies/mL needs confirmation on a new specimen before ART. At RNA ≥200 copies/mL on PrEP, prompt initiation of an effective, complete ART regimen is recommended while confirmation is pending. Prior CAB-LA PrEP specifically raises concern for integrase resistance; if genotype results are pending, boosted darunavir with an appropriate two-NRTI backbone is an interim treatment option. [1]

Case 2 · New HIV with hypoxemic pneumonia

A 42-year-old has CD4 38, diffuse ground-glass opacity, and room-air PaO₂ 62 mmHg.

What should happen today, and when should ART begin?

Discuss the next step

The room-air PaO₂ of 62 mm Hg meets the adjunctive steroid threshold. With a compatible illness and high suspicion, PCP treatment and steroids should begin promptly while respiratory testing, oxygen support, and assessment for bacterial pneumonia or TB proceed. ART generally starts within 2 weeks of PCP diagnosis when feasible. Secondary prophylaxis follows the treatment course. [6]

Case 3 · Headache before rapid ART

A 35-year-old with CD4 24 has 2 weeks of headache and a positive serum CrAg.

Is same-day ART the next step?

Discuss the next step

Headache with positive serum CrAg raises concern for meningitis; antigenemia alone does not establish CNS disease. Urgent CSF evaluation, including opening pressure and fungal culture, helps distinguish the syndromes when LP is safe. Confirmed CNS disease needs prompt antifungals and pressure management; if the person is not already taking ART, HIV treatment generally begins 4–6 weeks after antifungal initiation. [7]

Case 4 · Ring-enhancing lesions that do not improve

A patient with CD4 48, positive Toxoplasma IgG, and multiple lesions receives appropriate therapy but is unchanged at day 14.

What should prevent automatic extension of empiric therapy?

Discuss the next step

Reassess exposure and adherence and pursue alternative diagnoses, particularly CNS lymphoma. Discuss biopsy. A presumptive toxoplasma diagnosis should have a defined reassessment point. [5]

Case 5 · CMV PCR distracts from the involved organ

A patient with CD4 19 has diarrhea and a positive plasma CMV PCR.

Does this establish CMV colitis?

Discuss the next step

No. Obtain a syndrome-directed stool evaluation and consider endoscopy with biopsy. Ask about vision and arrange ophthalmology as indicated. Positive blood PCR does not by itself justify treatment for tissue disease. [18]

Case 6 · Fever and cytopenias before MAC prophylaxis

A patient newly diagnosed with HIV has CD4 12, fever, weight loss, cytopenias, and elevated alkaline phosphatase.

Would primary MAC prophylaxis be appropriate before the cause of the systemic illness is known?

Discuss the next step

The systemic symptoms raise concern for active MAC, histoplasmosis and other causes. Blood cultures for mycobacteria, Histoplasma antigen and tissue when indicated help distinguish them. Macrolide prophylaxis alone could select resistance in undiagnosed active MAC; when a blood culture is sent to exclude disseminated MAC, prophylaxis waits for the result. Severe disseminated histoplasmosis requires prompt liposomal amphotericin-based induction. [17] [19]

Case 7 · A suppressed patient wants fewer pills

A patient with HIV/HBV is suppressed on tenofovir-containing ART and requests CAB/RPV.

What cannot be lost in the switch?

Discuss the next step

HBV treatment. Review resistance and the rest of injectable eligibility, then preserve or separately provide appropriate HBV therapy. CAB/RPV itself does not treat HBV. [12] [36]

Case 8 · A new exposure presents overnight

An HIV-negative patient presents 18 hours after a substantial sexual exposure; the source result is pending.

What action is time sensitive?

Discuss the next step

First assess exposure type and the source’s HIV and suppression status. If the source’s HIV status is unknown, nPEP is a case-by-case decision. At 18 hours an indicated course should start now; pending tests are not a reason to wait. A full 28-day plan includes follow-up HIV testing and discussion of PrEP if future exposure is likely. [32]

Check the decision and explain why it is justified

1. Can a negative antigen-antibody test exclude acute HIV after a recent exposure?

Show explanation

No. Obtain HIV RNA when the exposure and clinical syndrome justify concern; repeat testing as indicated. [1]

2. Can DTG/3TC be the default before HBV and genotype results?

Show explanation

No. Select a suitable rapid-start regimen with HBV activity while those results are pending. [8]

3. Does every patient with CD4 <50 need azithromycin prophylaxis?

Show explanation

No. Effective ART started immediately removes the routine indication; evaluate symptoms for active MAC before prophylaxis. [17]

4. What monitoring item is easy to miss with TMP-SMX?

Show explanation

Potassium, in addition to renal function, blood counts, rash, and other toxicity assessment. [6]

5. Does PCP prophylaxis have the same threshold in untreated and stably treated HIV?

Show explanation

No. For patients not on ART or starting ART, use CD4 <200. Restart decisions on ART incorporate CD4 and the viral-load pattern. [6]

6. Should ART start immediately for cryptococcal meningitis?

Show explanation

Usually no. For a person not already taking ART, cryptococcal meningitis and raised intracranial pressure require treatment first. ART generally starts 4–6 weeks after antifungal therapy begins. [7]

7. Does persistent CrAg mean induction has failed?

Show explanation

No. Follow clinical response, opening pressure, and CSF fungal cultures; antigen can persist. [7]

8. Can a negative plasma CMV PCR exclude CMV retinitis?

Show explanation

No. Retinal examination establishes the diagnosis in the appropriate clinical setting. [18]

9. Can a prevention lenacapavir injection treat established HIV by itself?

Show explanation

No. Treatment requires a fully active combination selected for treatment history and resistance. [39]

10. Does LATITUDE prove that all viremic patients should immediately receive CAB/RPV?

Show explanation

No. Randomization followed an initial supported oral phase and achievement of the trial viral-load threshold. Eligibility and implementation support matter. [38]

11. What is the definitive treatment principle for HIV-associated PML?

Show explanation

Rapid effective ART and immune restoration. There is no established JCV-specific antiviral regimen. [20]

12. What makes the discharge plan complete?

Show explanation

Medication supply through follow-up, a confirmed appointment, named ownership of pending results and toxicity monitoring, and clear return precautions. [2]

HIV and OIs · One-page rounds summary

Teaching companion · Edited November 10, 2026. The full module explains eligibility, evidence, and exceptions; linked guidelines and prescribing information give regimen-specific doses.

Confirm and stage

Ag/Ab → differentiation → RNA for discordance. Suspected acute HIV can need RNA despite a negative screen. Obtain CD4, RNA, genotype, organ function, HBV/HCV, and prior ART/PrEP history.

Start appropriate ART

Initial options: BIC/FTC/TAF, DTG + two NRTIs, or eligible DTG/3TC. Baseline RNA >500,000 no longer excludes DTG/3TC. Do not use DTG/3TC for rapid start or before HBV/genotype results. Prior CAB PrEP needs integrase genotype and an appropriate interim regimen. Preserve HBV treatment.

Recognize the OI

Pneumonia: PCP, bacteria, TB. Headache: CrAg/CSF with opening pressure. For cryptococcal meningitis, consider an early repeat therapeutic LP even with low initial pressure. Focal deficit: MRI, toxoplasma/lymphoma/PML differential. Visual symptoms: urgent ophthalmology. Fever/cytopenias: MAC, histoplasmosis, TB, malignancy.

Match ART timing to disease

For people not already taking ART: PCP, start within 2 weeks of diagnosis when feasible; CNS cryptococcosis, usually 4–6 weeks after antifungal initiation. TB without meningitis: start ART within 2 weeks of TB treatment if CD4 <50 or within 2–8 weeks if CD4 ≥50. In pregnancy, start as early as feasible regardless of CD4. TB meningitis requires a separate timing plan. HIV-associated PML: effective ART promptly; no routine CNS-wide delay.

Who needs OI prophylaxis?

PCP primary prophylaxis: CD4 <200 when not on ART or starting ART. Restart on established ART at CD4 <100 regardless of RNA, or at CD4 100–200 with HIV RNA consistently detectable. Primary toxoplasma prophylaxis: IgG positive and CD4 <100. Active MAC must be excluded before primary prophylaxis; effective ART started immediately usually removes the indication. Secondary maintenance has different stopping rules.

Monitor and hand off

TMP-SMX: K+, creatinine, CBC. Amphotericin: renal function, K+/Mg. Flucytosine: CBC, renal adjustment/levels. Check interactions and absorption. Repeat HIV RNA at 4-8 weeks. Confirm medication supply, appointment, and result ownership.

Use prevention opportunities

For a substantial nonoccupational exposure, assess the source. HIV without sustained suppression or with unknown suppression supports nPEP; unknown source HIV status requires a case-by-case decision. Start indicated PEP promptly, within 72 hours, for 28 days. Follow-up testing and transition to PrEP matter when risk continues. Oral PrEP and long-acting CAB or LEN have distinct eligibility and visit requirements. Sustained viral suppression prevents sexual HIV transmission.

Know when to reconsider

No toxoplasma improvement by 10-14 days, new visual loss, rising oxygen need, persistent cultures, or new CNS symptoms require reassessment. Positive CMV blood PCR is not tissue disease; persistent CrAg is not proof of failure.
