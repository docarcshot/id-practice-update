Determine whether the isolate is causing disease

Resident core

Decide whether NTM is causing disease, whether to treat, which species-specific regimen is appropriate, and how to follow efficacy and toxicity.

Advanced teaching

Resistance, treatment failure, mixed isolates, organ dysfunction, surgery, drug levels and newer evidence.

Jump to fellow section

The main pulmonary recommendations follow the 2020 multisociety guideline for adults without HIV or cystic fibrosis. HIV-associated and CF-associated NTM require their own guidance and specialist assessment. Skin, bone, joint, device and disseminated infections require different specimens, source control and treatment endpoints.

HIV-associated disseminated MAC and TB   |   TB treatment module

An AFB-positive smear does not distinguish NTM from tuberculosis. Use the TB diagnostic and airborne-precaution pathway when pulmonary TB remains plausible. Do not give a partial TB regimen while assuming the isolate is NTM.

Establish clinical, radiographic and microbiologic evidence

Related lecture · TB NAAT, culture, and interpretation

Clinical illness

Chronic cough, sputum, fatigue, weight loss, hemoptysis or impaired function. Identify which symptoms could instead reflect bronchiectasis, aspiration or another infection.

Compatible imaging

Nodular-bronchiectatic disease with small nodules or tree-in-bud changes, or fibrocavitary disease. Neither pattern is specific for NTM.

Microbiologic confirmation

Usually at least two separate sputum cultures with the same species, or one positive bronchial wash/BAL. M. abscessus requires agreement at the subspecies level.

Obtain at least three respiratory samples over at least a week when evaluating chronic NTM disease. Use induced sputum if needed; bronchoscopy is usually reserved for patients unable to produce specimens despite induction. Biopsy can meet criteria when mycobacterial histologic features are accompanied by a positive tissue culture, or by a positive respiratory culture. Exclude competing diagnoses.

Ask the laboratory for species identification, clinically relevant susceptibilities and M. abscessus subspeciation. A negative smear does not exclude disease. IGRA is not an NTM diagnostic test; some species, including M. kansasii and M. marinum, can cross-react.

Some respiratory NTM isolates are much less likely to represent disease

Contamination means the organism entered the specimen during collection, processing or bronchoscopy. Transient respiratory isolation means it was recovered from the airway without established disease. Neither can always be distinguished from early infection on the first culture. Document "NTM isolated; pulmonary disease not established" when that is what the evidence supports.

These are probability judgments, not a list of organisms that can never cause infection. Species identification, immune status and geographic epidemiology matter. Published respiratory cohorts support particularly low clinical relevance for M. gordonae and M. mucogenicum; their proportions are not universal diagnostic cutoffs.

Sources [1] [8] [15]

One positive BAL meets the microbiologic criterion, not the entire diagnosis

Compatible symptoms and imaging, exclusion of alternative explanations, and assessment of the species are still required. BAL is not immune to contamination. A low-pathogenicity organism from one BAL should not trigger treatment simply because the specimen came from bronchoscopy.

For a stable patient with a discordant result, review prior cultures and CT evolution, confirm the identification with microbiology, and obtain separate expectorated or induced sputum samples when feasible. Do not repeat bronchoscopy solely to satisfy a culture count when less invasive sampling is available. Progressive disease or substantial immune suppression warrants more urgent assessment.

Sources [1] [15]

Look for a collection or bronchoscopy problem when results cluster

Tap water, rinse water and contaminated bronchoscopy equipment can produce false-positive respiratory cultures. CDC describes bronchoscopy-associated pseudo-outbreaks involving M. gordonae, M. chelonae and M. xenopi. The latter two can also cause real disease; a reported pseudo-outbreak association does not make every isolate a contaminant.

If several patients have an unexpected species after a shared procedure, or microbiology conflicts sharply with the clinical findings, involve microbiology and infection prevention. Review collection technique, water exposure, procedure dates and equipment/reprocessing records. A pseudo-outbreak is a cluster of positive results without corresponding infection; procedural contamination can also cause true infection, so assess each patient.

Sources [16] [17]

M. gordonae from BAL during a malignancy workup

A patient with a solitary lung mass has one smear-negative BAL culture growing M. gordonae, without a compatible diffuse nodular-bronchiectatic or cavitary syndrome.

The culture does not establish NTM disease or explain the mass. Continue the mass evaluation, review collection/procedure concerns and obtain follow-up respiratory cultures if clinically indicated. Avoid starting an NTM regimen on this result alone.

Choose treatment or structured observation with the patient

Factors favoring treatment

Cavitation, positive AFB smears, radiographic progression, substantial symptoms or weight loss, extensive disease, immune suppression, and a more pathogenic or treatable species.

Factors favoring observation

Mild stable noncavitary disease, uncertain attribution of symptoms, high toxicity risk and patient preference. Meeting diagnostic criteria alone does not mandate antibiotics.

Observation needs an explicit plan for longitudinal assessment of symptoms, cultures and imaging. Individualize follow-up and document the findings that would trigger treatment.

Treat the underlying lung disease in either pathway

Arrange individualized airway clearance with respiratory therapy, exercise as tolerated, nutritional assessment and smoking cessation. Assess aspiration or reflux when suggested by the history. Review the indication for corticosteroids and other immunosuppression. Consider an underlying bronchiectasis cause, including immune deficiency or CF when the phenotype warrants it.

Avoid macrolide monotherapy for bronchiectasis in someone with potentially active MAC or M. abscessus disease. It can select resistance to a key treatment drug.

Airway clearance and nutrition are supportive care. Observational associations do not prove they prevent NTM recurrence or replace antibiotics in progressive disease.

Sources [3]

When a rifamycin is difficult to use

A small open-label trial found sputum conversion in 55.0% with clarithromycin and ethambutol and 40.6% when rifampin was added. Drug exposures and sample size limit interpretation. An uncontrolled cohort reported microbiologic cure in 72 of 108 patients who completed at least 12 months on two drugs. Among 18 nonconverters with paired isolates, two developed macrolide resistance.

MAC2v3 randomized previously untreated patients with noncavitary pulmonary MAC to intermittent azithromycin and ethambutol with or without rifampin. In the 2026 published meeting abstract, the two-drug arm was better tolerated. The per-protocol analysis counting treatment intensification as failure estimated a difference in culture negativity at 12 months of −5.0 percentage points (95% CI, −15.5 to 5.1). The interval crossed the prespecified −10-point margin. Noninferiority and equivalent protection against macrolide resistance were not established.

The 2020 guideline suggests at least three drugs, including a macrolide and ethambutol; this is a conditional recommendation based on very low-certainty evidence. The role of the third drug in preventing acquired macrolide resistance remains an important uncertainty. In selected macrolide-susceptible, noncavitary disease with rifamycin intolerance or interactions, discuss two drugs with an NTM specialist, alongside a clofazimine-containing three-drug alternative. Do not extrapolate these findings to cavitary/severe or macrolide-resistant disease, or to disseminated HIV-associated MAC. Maintain ethambutol, serial cultures and reassessment for resistance if response fails.

M. abscessus requires subspecies and resistance interpretation

M. abscessus complex includes subspecies abscessus, massiliense and bolletii. Subspecies massiliense usually has a nonfunctional erm(41) gene and is more likely to retain macrolide activity. Subspecies identification alone cannot exclude acquired resistance.

A preliminary susceptible result may change
Laboratory resultWhat it meansTreatment implication
Macrolide susceptible early, resistant after extended incubationInducible resistance, usually from functional erm(41).Do not count azithromycin or clarithromycin as active.
Macrolide resistant early, or resistance-associated rrl mutationConstitutive/acquired resistance.Macrolide is not an active component even if the subspecies is massiliense.
No inducible or mutational resistanceMacrolide activity is retained.Include a macrolide in the active multidrug regimen.

Ask for extended macrolide testing, generally through 14 days, and/or molecular assessment of erm(41), plus evaluation for mutational resistance. Test amikacin and the other candidate drugs using a reference laboratory when needed.

The initial phase typically combines IV agents with oral companions. The 2020 guideline suggests at least three active drugs in the initial phase (Question XX, conditional, very low certainty). Its treatment summary suggests at least four active drugs when feasible for inducible or mutational macrolide resistance. An inactive macrolide does not count toward that total. IV amikacin, imipenem or cefoxitin, and tigecycline are common options; oral choices may include clofazimine or linezolid. Selection depends on susceptibility, exposure and tolerability.

After the IV phase, continuation therapy usually retains at least two to three active agents, sometimes including inhaled amikacin. Neither the IV-phase length nor the total treatment duration has a well-established universal standard. Plan both with an NTM specialist. The MAC post-conversion duration does not apply.

A macrolide sometimes remains in a resistant M. abscessus regimen for immunomodulation. It does not count toward the active-drug total. Monitor for other NTM species that could acquire resistance during that exposure.

Follow symptoms, cultures and toxicity separately

Related lecture · Monitoring during outpatient IV treatment

Before treatment, record weight, functional limitations, sputum burden, CT findings and baseline cultures. Obtain CBC, creatinine/electrolytes and hepatic tests. Add ECG, visual acuity/color testing and audiometry according to the planned drugs. Reconcile all medications and supplements.

Outside the lung, tissue diagnosis and source control become central

Related lecture · Bone, joint, and implant infection assessment

Obtain deep tissue or aspirate for mycobacterial culture and histopathology; superficial swabs are less useful. Send a separate unfixed specimen for culture, not only tissue in formalin. Tell the laboratory about water exposure and suspected species because special growth conditions may be needed. Negative AFB stains do not exclude infection.

Clinical cases

One positive MAC culture

A patient with mild bronchiectasis has stable weight and CT findings, and MAC in one of three sputum cultures.

Repeat cultures, optimize airway clearance and look for other causes of symptoms. The current evidence does not justify committing to prolonged MAC therapy solely on that result.

Still culture-positive at month seven

A patient taking daily MAC treatment reports missing ethambutol most days because of visual concerns.

New visual symptoms warrant holding ethambutol and urgent eye assessment. Reconstruct the drugs actually taken and repeat susceptibility testing. Seven months elapsed does not establish six consecutive months of adequate multidrug treatment; ALIS cannot compensate for ineffective companion therapy.

A susceptibility result changes

M. abscessus is clarithromycin-susceptible at the early reading and resistant at day 14.

This is inducible resistance. The macrolide cannot be counted as active. Rebuild the active-drug count with expert input before proceeding.

Complex disease starts with a reassessment of the diagnosis

Related lecture · Antifungal spectrum when a concurrent fungal infection is established

Optional fellow section

Before escalating, reconstruct the course from culture dates, species, smear burden, CT changes, actual doses and interruptions. Review absorption, access, interactions and whether the patient is taking the intended regimen.

Persistent symptoms are not synonymous with persistent NTM

Evaluate bacterial bronchiectasis exacerbation, aspiration, malignancy, drug toxicity and Aspergillus-related disease when suggested by the presentation. Aspergillus isolation or BAL galactomannan alone does not establish chronic pulmonary aspergillosis or ABPA. Use the appropriate clinical, radiographic and immunologic criteria. Rifamycins can make azole treatment ineffective.

Mixed MAC and M. abscessus cultures

Identify each isolate and reassess whether it meets criteria for clinically significant disease. The pulmonary NTM guideline advises surveillance for other NTM species during treatment, particularly when a macrolide is used for immunomodulation in macrolide-resistant M. abscessus disease. Concurrent disease warrants expert consultation.

Macrolide activity must be interpreted for each organism. Do not count a macrolide as active against an inducibly or mutationally resistant M. abscessus isolate.

Recurrence after apparent cure

Reculture and repeat species identification and relevant susceptibility testing. Reinfection with a new strain is common in structural lung disease; recurrence does not prove nonadherence or inadequate prior duration. Distinguishing same-species relapse from reinfection can require comparison of stored isolates with molecular typing.

Drug levels answer specific exposure and toxicity questions

Routine TDM is most established for IV aminoglycosides. For oral drugs, consider it when poor response, malabsorption, major interactions or toxicity raises a specific question. A target concentration is not a guarantee of cure.

Use drug levels with a defined clinical question
SituationPractical interpretation
IV amikacinAn individualized pharmacokinetic assessment relates drug exposure to renal clearance, cumulative treatment and toxicity risk.
Amikacin concentrationsFor parenteral amikacin, reference peaks are 35–45 mcg/mL with daily therapy and 65–80 mcg/mL with intermittent therapy. The guideline lists a trough below 5 mg/L. Interpret the value against the actual regimen, collection time and the method used to estimate the peak.
Timing of amikacin levelsEstimating a peak may require more than one postinfusion sample. The infusion and collection times determine whether a reported concentration can be compared with a peak or trough.
Long-term linezolidA correctly timed predose concentration can help explain toxicity during prolonged use, particularly with renal dysfunction or cytopenias. A trough below 2 mg/L has been proposed as a safety goal; the strongest clinical exposure-toxicity data cited here come from TB. This is neither an NTM efficacy target nor a guarantee against injury. Interpret the result alongside symptoms, CBC, susceptibility and companion drugs.
Selected oral-agent TDMConsider for unexpected failure or absorption/interactions. A low early level may reflect delayed absorption; paired later sampling can help. Coordinate assay availability and interpretation before ordering.

For ethambutol, impaired renal clearance may require a longer interval. For IV amikacin, a lower dose, a longer interval or both may be appropriate; renal function and timed concentrations guide the choice. Rifampin affects anticoagulants, ART and transplant medications. Rifabutin has fewer induction effects but is not interaction-free. QT-active combinations require an individualized ECG and electrolyte plan.

Salvage treatment requires evidence and realistic goals

ALIS for refractory MACIn CONVERT, culture conversion by month six occurred in 29.0% with ALIS plus background therapy versus 8.9% with background therapy alone.CONVERT enrolled adults with persistent cultures despite multidrug treatment. Serious respiratory toxicity can occur. The June 2026 US label limits ALIS to the refractory MAC population described above; use for M. abscessus is off-label.
ALIS in newly diagnosed pulmonary MACENCORE randomized 425 adults untreated for their current episode to ALIS or inhaled placebo, each with daily azithromycin and ethambutol. Respiratory symptom scores improved 3.11 points more with ALIS at month 13. Culture conversion by month 13, a prespecified secondary endpoint, occurred in 82.4% versus 55.6%.The comparator was azithromycin and ethambutol without a rifamycin. These results do not compare ALIS with standard rifamycin-based triple therapy or extend the current US indication. In September 2026, Insmed reported a January 28, 2027 FDA target action date for its indication expansion application. Check the current FDA label for any subsequent change.
Clofazimine replacing rifampin in MACA small open-label randomized trial enrolled 40 patients. Six-month conversion was 62% with clofazimine and 58% with rifampin, each combined with ethambutol and a macrolide.Useful support for a selected alternative, not proof of universal interchangeability. When to consider pulmonary surgery

Refer selected patients with localized destructive disease, persistent culture positivity despite an adequate regimen, resistance, substantial hemoptysis or inability to sustain useful medical treatment. Surgery supplements antimicrobial therapy. Assess predicted postoperative reserve, distribution of disease, nutrition, comorbidities and operative expertise.

Balance the potential benefit of resection against operative risk and the distribution of disease. Seek a surgical team experienced in NTM disease.

When cure is not a realistic near-term endpoint

When patients cannot tolerate an effective combination, clarify the goals they most value, such as control of symptoms or preserving function. An NTM specialist can help weigh any further chance of microbiologic response against treatment burden.

Associations between culture conversion and survival are subject to confounding. Neither an observational mortality comparison nor a single successful case proves that a particular regimen prolongs survival.

NTM rounds reference

Establish disease

Symptoms, compatible imaging, exclusion of mimics and usually two same-species sputum cultures or one wash/BAL. M. abscessus requires the same subspecies.

Choose an endpoint

Treatment or observation, with explicit goals and follow-up. Cavities, smear positivity and progression favor treatment.

MAC

Susceptible MAC: azithromycin, ethambutol, rifamycin. Intermittent for nonsevere noncavitary nodular-bronchiectatic disease. Cavitary or severe disease favors daily therapy. For cavitary, advanced/severe bronchiectatic or macrolide-resistant MAC, the guideline suggests initial parenteral amikacin or streptomycin if activity and toxicity risk permit. ≥12 months after conversion.

M. abscessus

The guideline suggests ≥3 active drugs initially. For inducible or mutational macrolide resistance, the guideline summary suggests ≥4 when feasible. An inactive macrolide does not count.

M. kansasii

Rifampin + ethambutol + isoniazid or macrolide when rifampin-susceptible. At least 12 months total; no pyrazinamide.

Monitoring

Serial cultures, weight, functional status and adherence. Ask about hearing/balance with ototoxic drugs, vision with ethambutol or linezolid, and neuropathy with linezolid or isoniazid.

Extrapulmonary

Deep specimens, species identification and source control. Pulmonary diagnostic and duration rules do not automatically apply.

Apparent failure

Check exposure, susceptibility and toxicity. ALIS is an add-on for adults with few or no alternatives and positive sputum after ≥6 consecutive months of multidrug MAC therapy.

Sources and evidence limits

Adult pulmonary treatment recommendations are principally based on the 2020 multisociety guideline. Extrapulmonary recommendations draw on older guidance and species-specific reports and have less certain treatment durations. Much of the evidence for toxicity surveillance comes from clinical experience rather than comparisons of monitoring strategies.
