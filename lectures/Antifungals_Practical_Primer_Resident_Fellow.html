Before prescribing

This primer covers antifungal selection, administration, adverse effects, and monitoring. The resident section is designed for a 30-45 minute discussion, followed by additional pharmacology and evidence for fellows.

Resident core

Recognize the major coverage gaps, choose a formulation and route, identify toxicities, and arrange monitoring.

Fellow section

Interpret exposure and susceptibility together, distinguish CSF from brain tissue concentrations, and evaluate the evidence for newer agents.

Jump to fellow section

Review the diagnosis, likely organism, severity, infection site, source control, prior antifungal exposure, and drug interactions. Candida from respiratory secretions usually represents colonization; a respiratory isolate alone does not establish invasive disease. [1]

A susceptible isolate may still require a different drug because of poor urine, CSF, or vitreous exposure. Abscesses and infected devices also require source control.

Fellow teaching Species and MIC interpretation

Candida auris

For adult clinical infection, CDC recommends an echinocandin initially, for example micafungin 100 mg IV daily. Send susceptibility testing and reassess the clinical response. Consider liposomal amphotericin B 5 mg/kg/day for echinocandin resistance or lack of improvement after 5 days, while reassessing source control and the diagnosis. Pan-resistant infection warrants expert/reference-laboratory input and consideration of investigational access. [45]

Do not treat asymptomatic colonization or attempt antifungal decolonization. Notify infection prevention and public health promptly. In acute care use Contact Precautions, clean shared equipment, and use an EPA List P disinfectant. Communicate status on transfer. Clearing an infection does not mean colonization has cleared. [48] [47]

Azole administration and adverse effects

Fluconazole / Diflucan

Oral absorption is reliable; use oral therapy when the patient can absorb it. Renal clearance makes maintenance dose adjustment important. It reaches urine and CSF well.

Nausea and headache are common. Watch liver injury and QT prolongation. Less common effects include severe skin reactions, reversible alopecia with prolonged treatment, and reported adrenal insufficiency. Warfarin and other CYP substrates still require review. [4]

Itraconazole / Sporanox; SUBA formulation / Tolsura

Conventional capsules depend on food and gastric acidity. Solution is better absorbed fasting. SUBA formulations have different dosing and administration instructions. They are not interchangeable milligram for milligram.

GI intolerance and liver injury can occur. Negative inotropic effects can cause edema or heart failure. Peripheral neuropathy, hearing loss, and pseudohyperaldosteronism are additional reported toxicities. New dyspnea or edema warrants evaluation for heart failure. [5] [3]

Voriconazole / Vfend

Take oral doses at least an hour before or after food. Variable CYP2C19 metabolism and nonlinear kinetics make TDM central to treatment.

Transient visual changes are common. Hallucinations or encephalopathy may reflect excessive exposure. Also watch liver injury and QT prolongation. Prolonged treatment is associated with photosensitivity, cutaneous malignancy, and fluoride-associated periostitis. Bone pain with elevated alkaline phosphatase should prompt consideration of periostitis. Recommend sun protection and skin surveillance during prolonged treatment. [6]

Posaconazole / Noxafil

Delayed-release tablets or IV generally give more dependable exposure than conventional suspension. Suspension absorption depends on food and gastric conditions; do not substitute the formulations using the same dose.

GI effects, liver injury, and QT prolongation occur. Hypertension plus hypokalemia can represent pseudohyperaldosteronism, often with low renin and aldosterone. Check BP, potassium, and posaconazole levels; reassess the antifungal if pseudohyperaldosteronism is suspected. [7] [35]

Isavuconazonium / Cresemba

Isavuconazonium is converted to active isavuconazole. 372 mg isavuconazonium sulfate equals 200 mg isavuconazole. Oral absorption is reliable and not materially food dependent; IV does not require cyclodextrin.

It shortens QT and is contraindicated in familial short-QT syndrome. GI symptoms and liver abnormalities occur; serious hypersensitivity is possible. It often has fewer visual/skin effects than voriconazole, but retains clinically important CYP3A interactions. [8] [20]

Oral voriconazole does not require renal dose reduction. IV voriconazole and IV posaconazole contain SBECD, which can accumulate in renal impairment. Product labels favor avoiding these IV formulations below the specified renal threshold unless benefit outweighs risk. Consider oral therapy and monitor renal function. [6] [7]

Echinocandins, including micafungin

Blood-culture clearance · Source control in bloodstream infection

Micafungin is IV only. It usually needs neither renal nor hepatic dose adjustment under U.S. labeling, and no dialysis replacement dose. It has fewer CYP interactions than azoles; review concomitant medications. Routine drug levels are not standard. [9]

For candidemia, an echinocandin is a usual initial choice. Fluconazole step-down requires a clinically stable patient, susceptible isolate, and cleared blood cultures. Infected devices and undrained foci still need source control. [1]

Micafungin has poor urinary, CSF, and vitreous exposure. See the urinary decision table for the distinction between collecting-system infection and hematogenous renal infection.

Adverse effects include GI symptoms and liver test abnormalities. Infusion reactions can cause rash, flushing, or hypotension. Rare serious effects include hemolysis, hepatic failure, renal injury, and anaphylaxis. Monitor liver and renal function during treatment. [9]

Amphotericin B formulations and adverse effects

Write the full formulation in the order. Liposomal amphotericin B, amphotericin B lipid complex, and deoxycholate differ in dose and toxicity. They are not interchangeable. Liposomal amphotericin reduces, but does not remove, nephrotoxicity and infusion reactions. [10]

Follow creatinine, potassium, magnesium, CBC, and liver tests. Check renal function and electrolytes daily initially, replace potassium and magnesium as needed, and review other nephrotoxic drugs. Consider saline hydration if volume status allows. Prepare and dilute the drug according to the formulation's label; saline hydration and the drug infusion are separate orders.

Common problems include AKI, potassium/magnesium wasting, infusion fever or rigors, and anemia. Less common serious events include hypersensitivity, chest/back pain during infusion, and arrhythmias, including those promoted by electrolyte disturbances. Unexpected phosphate elevation with AmBisome may be assay interference. [10]

Amphotericin formulation matters for urinary infection. See the urinary exposure table; liposomal amphotericin is not a substitute for deoxycholate in this setting.

Flucytosine / Ancobon

Flucytosine reaches the CNS and is used in selected combination induction regimens. Renal accumulation can cause marrow suppression and hepatotoxicity; GI intolerance is also common. Adjust for renal function and dialysis. Follow CBC, renal function, and liver tests closely, especially when amphotericin worsens renal clearance. [11]

Candiduria and urinary drug concentrations

Candiduria alone does not establish infection. Asymptomatic candiduria usually needs risk-factor and catheter management, not an antifungal. IDSA exceptions include neutropenia, very low-birth-weight infants, and planned urologic manipulation. Diabetes or a chronic catheter alone is not an indication to treat.

PresentationTreatment decision
Asymptomatic candiduriaUsually observe and remove or replace an unnecessary catheter. Assess the exceptions above.
Compatible cystitis symptomsExclude other causes. If the Candida isolate is fluconazole susceptible, fluconazole is the usual preferred drug.
Ascending pyelonephritis or obstructed collecting systemUse a urine-active drug guided by susceptibility. Relieve obstruction; consider stent/nephrostomy removal or exchange. Fungus balls generally require intervention.

Toxicity monitoring and interactions

Outpatient laboratory monitoring and result ownership

Obtain baseline renal function, electrolytes, and liver tests for systemic therapy; add CBC when using amphotericin or flucytosine. Review ECG/QTc when an azole or interacting drugs create arrhythmia risk. Correct potassium and magnesium abnormalities. During prolonged azole treatment, repeat liver tests early, often weekly initially, then space them according to stability and the agent's label. Voriconazole labeling specifies at least weekly liver tests for the first month, then monthly if stable. Check BP and potassium on posaconazole or itraconazole. For amphotericin and flucytosine, renal changes require prompt reassessment of dosing, toxicity, and monitoring frequency. [6] [7] [10] [11]

Check interactions when starting and stopping

Review calcineurin/mTOR inhibitors, venetoclax and other targeted cancer therapies, vinca alkaloids, DOACs, warfarin, statins, sedatives, and QT-active drugs. Rifamycins and other strong inducers can cause antifungal underexposure. The interaction can reverse after stopping the azole, requiring another dose adjustment and repeat monitoring. [3]

Therapeutic drug monitoring

Document the last dose, draw time, formulation, recent missed doses, food/tube feed administration, interacting drugs, and the indication-specific target. mg/L and mcg/mL are numerically equivalent. Repeat a level after a meaningful dose, route, formulation, interaction, absorption, or clinical change. [14]

Itraconazole and hydroxyitraconazole levels

Example: itraconazole 0.7 mg/L + hydroxyitraconazole 1.3 mg/L = combined 2.0 mg/L. The result meets a combined target of 2.0 mg/L or greater, but does not meet a parent-only target greater than 1.0 mg/L. Compare the result with the range for that assay.

Voriconazole N-oxide has minimal antifungal activity and is not added to the voriconazole trough to determine treatment exposure. [6] [13]

Clinical cases

Case 1. Candida in blood and urine

A critically ill patient has candidemia and candiduria. Is micafungin inadequate because it has low urine levels?

Not necessarily. It is a reasonable initial candidemia drug. Determine whether candiduria is colonization, collecting-system disease, obstruction, or hematogenous renal involvement. A separate symptomatic urinary focus may require a different drug and source control. A positive urine culture alone does not establish a Candida UTI. [1]

Case 2. Hallucinations during voriconazole treatment

A patient develops hallucinations on day 6 after the voriconazole dose was increased. What should be checked?

Check dose history, timing, interacting drugs, liver function, electrolytes, and a correctly timed voriconazole level. Also assess other causes of encephalopathy. Serious toxicity may require holding or changing therapy while results are pending. Nonlinear kinetics mean that doubling the dose can produce more than double the exposure. [6]

Case 3. A therapeutic level with new edema

A patient receiving itraconazole has a satisfactory combined level but develops edema and dyspnea. Does the drug level exclude toxicity?

No. Evaluate for heart failure and reconsider itraconazole promptly. Heart failure can occur despite a therapeutic level. Review interacting calcium channel blockers and other contributors. [5]

Rounds sheet

Before the first dose

Identify the organism and infection site. Identify source control. Confirm formulation, load, organ function, interaction review, and the monitoring owner.

Azole structures and pharmacokinetics

The triazole ring is the shared pharmacophore. Substituents around that ring change binding to fungal CYP51, lipophilicity, molecular size, protein binding, metabolism, and interactions. These changes can expand antifungal activity without improving CNS exposure.

Small size and a substantial unbound fraction favor access to fluid compartments. Lipophilicity can favor membrane passage and tissue partitioning, but very high lipophilicity can accompany extensive protein binding and low CSF concentrations. Efflux, ionization, inflammation, and the measured compartment also matter. Structure alone does not reliably predict CNS exposure.

Advanced clinical considerations

Fellow teaching

Species-specific susceptibility interpretation

For C. glabrata, CLSI fluconazole results use susceptible-dose dependent or resistant categories rather than a standard susceptible category. A susceptible-dose dependent result requires an appropriate dose and exposure. Prior echinocandin treatment or persistent infection should prompt echinocandin testing and repeat evaluation for resistance. For C. parapsilosis, use species-specific interpretation: a naturally higher MIC does not by itself mean an echinocandin will fail. [1]

Fellow teaching

C. auris susceptibility thresholds

CDC's tentative resistance thresholds include fluconazole MIC at least 32 mg/L, amphotericin B at least 2 mg/L, and micafungin at least 4 mg/L. They are interim guides with uncertain outcome correlation, not universal susceptible breakpoints. The October 2026 CDC page notes an established CLSI C. auris-specific rezafungin breakpoint, in addition to the tentative thresholds for other agents. Ask the laboratory which method and interpretive standard it uses; do not transfer thresholds between drugs or methods. [46]

Fellow teaching

Dematiaceous molds

Melanized molds such as Alternaria, Curvularia, Exophiala, and Cladophialophora differ in susceptibility, syndrome, and optimal therapy. Itraconazole, voriconazole, and posaconazole have activity against many species, with species-specific variation. Isavuconazole has variable in vitro activity with less clinical evidence; fluconazole is unreliable. CNS disease requires attention to penetration, species identification, susceptibility results, and surgical source control. Isavuconazole susceptibility cannot be inferred from results for voriconazole or posaconazole. [43] [44]

The VITAL cryptococcosis/endemic-fungus subset included only 38 patients across five genera and had no randomized comparator. Comparative efficacy for each organism remains uncertain. [42]

Fellow teaching

Echinocandin resistance

FKS mutations can confer class resistance. Prior echinocandin exposure, persistent candidemia, and species identification affect the need for susceptibility testing. C. parapsilosis has higher baseline echinocandin MICs, but that alone does not establish treatment failure. Request susceptibility data and interpret the breakpoint for that species and drug. [1]

Fellow teaching

CSF and brain tissue concentrations

Meningitis, an angioinvasive lesion, and an encapsulated abscess are different compartments. Isavuconazole tissue measurements from two patients showed substantially different concentrations in inflamed versus normal brain. Amphotericin B can be effective in CNS infection despite low CSF levels. A concentration in homogenized tissue does not establish free extracellular exposure or comparative clinical efficacy. [16] [1]

Fellow teaching

Parent-only and combined itraconazole targets

The 2026 IDSA aspergillosis pharmacology appendix describes combined parent-plus-hydroxy targets, whereas current IDSA histoplasmosis guidance favors a parent component >1 mg/L and <3-4 mg/L, and states that a universal combined cutoff has not been established. It notes that combined levels >2 mg/L may provide similar responses to parent levels >1 mg/L. Mayo also provides distinct parent and combined interpretation. State the assay and treatment target explicitly. A combined result can meet its target while the parent level remains below a parent-only target. [3] [12] [13]

Key studies

Voriconazole versus itraconazole in histoplasmosis

A single-center retrospective cohort compared 175 itraconazole-treated patients with 19 voriconazole-treated patients. Voriconazole was associated with higher mortality during the first 42 days after treatment initiation (adjusted hazard ratio 4.30; 95% CI 1.3-13.9). The small voriconazole group, nonrandomized treatment assignment, and potential residual confounding limit causal inference. These findings support itraconazole preference and argue against treating voriconazole as an interchangeable alternative. [56]

Isavuconazole versus voriconazole: SECURE

Day-42 mortality was 19% with isavuconazole versus 20% with voriconazole, meeting noninferiority. Drug-related adverse events were less frequent with isavuconazole. The trial did not establish superiority or equivalence for every rare mold or CNS infection. [20]

Posaconazole for primary invasive aspergillosis treatment

The 2021 randomized trial met noninferiority versus voriconazole for primary invasive aspergillosis treatment. Day-42 mortality was 15% versus 21% in the intention-to-treat population. The confidence interval for the treatment difference crossed zero, so mortality superiority was not established. The regimen used modern IV/tablet formulations, not conventional suspension. [21]

Voriconazole TDM trials

A 110-patient single-center randomized study supported voriconazole TDM, with less discontinuation from toxicity and better treatment response. A later multicenter cluster randomized crossover study did not show improved treatment outcomes with TDM-guided dosing. PK variability and exposure-related toxicity support monitoring, although a consistent survival benefit has not been shown. [22] [23]

Breakthrough fungal infection

Confirm infection and seek species identification, histopathology, and susceptibility testing when feasible. Review adherence, formulation, absorption, interactions, achieved exposure, devices, drainage, immune recovery, and whether a second fungus lies outside the current spectrum. Persistent infection may reflect inadequate source control despite a low MIC. [2]

Fellow teaching

MIC interpretation across drug classes

Breakpoints depend on organism, method, drug exposure, and available outcomes. An epidemiological cutoff identifies a non-wild-type population; it is not a clinical susceptible breakpoint. For Aspergillus tested against echinocandins, laboratories often assess minimum effective concentration rather than the same MIC endpoint used for azoles. MIC values cannot be compared directly across classes to rank clinical efficacy. [2]

Hypoalbuminemia, obesity, critical illness, ECMO, and impaired absorption can complicate interpretation of total plasma levels. Review dosing and level interpretation with pharmacy when these factors are present. [3]

Newer experimental posaconazole data show that infection alters cerebral diffusion. These animal data do not establish a human CNS dose or clinical equivalence to voriconazole. [36]

Oteseconazole / Vivjoa
Tetrazole CYP51 inhibitor		Reduction of recurrent VVC in females who are not of reproductive potential.Not a systemic mold or candidemia treatment. Contraindicated in females of reproductive potential, pregnancy, and lactation; prolonged exposure underlies the restriction. Drug interactions still require review.

Fellow teaching

New mechanisms and resistance

Olorofim is not an azole, and fosmanogepix is not an echinocandin. A new target may retain activity against isolates resistant to older classes. Spectrum, clinical efficacy, and emergence of resistance still require study. Assess the enrolled organisms, comparator, outcomes, follow-up, and safety data before applying trial results. [27] [32]
