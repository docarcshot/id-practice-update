Choose the regimen for the patient, organism, and disease site

TB treatment is more than a prescription for four drugs. The first decisions are whether disease is active, whether the patient may transmit it, what resistance is present, and whether the evidence for a particular regimen applies to this patient.

This shared core module is intended for internal medicine residents, ID fellows and attending physicians. Allow 60-75 minutes for the resident pathway and another 20-30 minutes for the evidence discussion. Cases are fictional teaching examples.

  1. Separate TB infection from disease and common mimics; select diagnostic tests and infection-prevention actions.
  2. Select a standard or shortened regimen for drug-susceptible pulmonary TB and identify reasons to use a different course.
  3. Distinguish BPaLM, BPaL and newer WHO regimens for resistant TB.
  4. Select preventive treatment for latent TB and build a toxicity, interaction, microbiology and follow-up plan.
  5. Critique newer treatment studies using population, comparator, endpoint, effect size and limitations.

Resident pathway

Read the initial approach, diagnostics, mimics, prevention, drug-susceptible and resistant-TB sections, drug monitoring, cases and rounds summary. Be able to explain the regimen and what would change it.

Fellow pathway

Add the evidence review and special-population discussion. Pay particular attention to trial eligibility, the distinction between regimen and strategy trials, resistance mechanisms, and how drug toxicity can weaken a regimen.

Attending review pathway

Start with diagnostic discordance and mimics, then review shortened-regimen eligibility, resistant-TB options, preventive care and the trial tables. Use the cases to ask which finding would change the plan. Allow 30–40 minutes for a focused update or 80–105 minutes for the full primer.

Protect others and establish the diagnosis while planning treatment

The clinical spectrum

Inhaled M. tuberculosis can cause infection without symptoms or evidence of disease. Latent TB infection is not contagious. Progression to disease may follow recent acquisition or occur years later, particularly with impaired immunity. Pulmonary and laryngeal disease drive most transmission; extrapulmonary disease can coexist with an infectious pulmonary focus. Treat infection to prevent progression, and treat disease with an adequate combination to cure it and prevent resistance. [5]

RecognizeSymptoms, exposures, immune status and imaging.
IsolateAirborne precautions when pulmonary or laryngeal TB is suspected.
SampleAFB smear, culture, NAAT and resistance testing.
TreatActive multidrug therapy when indicated.
CoordinateID, infection prevention and public health.

Cough, fever, night sweats, weight loss, hemoptysis or compatible imaging should prompt consideration of TB. Advanced immunosuppression can produce atypical pulmonary findings or disseminated disease. An IGRA or TST measures immune sensitization. It does not distinguish latent infection from disease, establish infectiousness, or exclude active TB when negative. [5] [22]

Obtain three sputum specimens for AFB smear and mycobacterial culture, generally 8-24 hours apart with at least one early-morning specimen. Send a respiratory NAAT on at least one specimen and request rapid molecular resistance testing as appropriate. Induced sputum or bronchoscopy may be needed if adequate sputum cannot be obtained. AFB smear positivity is not specific for M. tuberculosis; culture and organism identification still matter. [5]

A positive NAAT can accelerate treatment and public-health action. A negative result does not independently exclude smear-negative TB. Obtain cultures for susceptibility testing and treatment monitoring even when NAAT is positive. If the patient is seriously ill or suspicion is high, obtain specimens promptly and start an appropriate multidrug regimen without waiting weeks for culture. [5] [2]

Airborne precautions are a separate decision

Use an airborne infection isolation room and appropriate respiratory protection. Mask the patient for necessary transport. The traditional CDC hospital pathway includes three negative AFB smears, but its application differs between a patient being evaluated for TB and one with confirmed infectious TB. Confirmed disease also requires assessment of effective treatment and clinical improvement. Persistent suspicion, resistance, or inadequate treatment changes the decision. Follow the current institutional NAAT-based or smear-based pathway with infection prevention; neither one negative test nor a fixed number of treatment days guarantees clearance.

[6]

Notify the local/state TB program promptly for suspected or confirmed disease. Coordinate contact evaluation, reporting and discharge planning. Do not leave the first public-health call until discharge. [7] [6]

A chest radiograph is a starting point; CT can clarify cavities, nodules, adenopathy or a sampling target. Upper-lobe cavitation is suggestive but neither required nor specific. IGRA and TST: useful for infection, weak shortcuts for disease

IGRA avoids BCG-related false positivity and is generally preferred for BCG-vaccinated people aged at least 5 years. An indeterminate, borderline or invalid result is not a negative test; repeat testing or TST may help after reviewing immune status and collection issues. Recent infection may not yet be detectable: repeat contact testing 8–10 weeks after the last exposure when indicated. Do not order serial IGRAs to demonstrate treatment response. [30]

Read a TST at 48–72 hours, measuring transverse induration rather than redness. Positivity depends on risk: at least 5 mm for HIV, recent close contacts, transplant recipients and major immunosuppression; at least 10 mm for groups such as people born in high-incidence countries, high-risk congregate settings, diabetes and children under 5; at least 15 mm when no risk factors are known. Consult the complete risk list. BCG history does not change these risk-based cutoffs. [31]

For an unexpectedly positive screening test in someone truly at low risk, reconsider why testing was done and use the guideline's confirmatory second-test approach. For high-risk exposure or compatible illness, pursue the relevant infection or disease pathway even when an immune test is negative. [29]

Keep a competing diagnosis until the evidence separates it from TB

A positive IGRA can coexist with cancer, fungal infection or bacterial pneumonia. It does not explain a cavity, lymph node or constitutional syndrome. Ask whether the proposed diagnosis accounts for the time course, immune status, exposures and microbiology.

NTM pulmonary disease generally requires the same species in at least two separately collected sputum cultures, or a qualifying bronchoscopic or biopsy result, alongside compatible illness and imaging and exclusion of alternatives. Meeting diagnostic criteria does not automatically require treatment; species, progression and patient priorities matter. Do not apply an empiric TB regimen to all AFB-positive disease. [32]

Attending checkpoint

If the patient is not improving, reconsider the diagnosis before reflexively extending treatment. Review adherence, drug exposure, resistance, a second infection or malignancy, and paradoxical inflammation. Clinical deterioration is a reason to reassess and resample, not proof of either resistance or IRIS.

Ethambutol protects the initial regimen while susceptibility is unknown. It can be stopped once isoniazid and rifampin susceptibility is established and the treatment plan remains appropriate. Count completed doses and interruptions, not simply elapsed calendar time. [2]

Before choosing rifapentine-moxifloxacin

Confirm pulmonary disease, age at least 12 years, weight at least 40 kg, no known or suspected resistance to a component, and acceptable interactions and QT risk. For people with HIV, CDC implementation guidance specifies CD4 counts at least 100 cells/µL and efavirenz-based ART. Do not assume that another ART regimen is compatible with daily high-dose rifapentine. The evidence does not justify routine use during pregnancy or breastfeeding, in most extrapulmonary disease, or when resistance is suspected. Prior TB therapy or recent exposure to fluoroquinolones warrants careful review. [4]

The regimen is 119 daily doses with food. Isoniazid is 300 mg daily, rifapentine 1,200 mg daily and moxifloxacin 400 mg daily; pyrazinamide is weight-based during the first 8 weeks. Add pyridoxine 25-50 mg daily. Ensure the complete course, follow-up testing and observed-therapy support are available before selecting it. [4] [1]

A shorter regimen is an option, not a reason to improvise

If the patient cannot receive moxifloxacin, simply removing it from the four-month regimen is not supported. Similarly, stopping standard HRZE/HR at four months because sputum converted early does not reproduce Study 31.

[10]

Rifampin resistance changes the entire regimen

RR-TB means rifampin-resistant TB. MDR-TB is resistant to at least isoniazid and rifampin. Fluoroquinolone susceptibility is a major treatment branch point. Confirm the molecular result, obtain expanded susceptibility testing, review previous TB drugs and contact a TB expert promptly. Never add one drug to a failing regimen. [7] [8]

Abbreviations in resistant-TB combinations: B, bedaquiline; Pa, pretomanid; L, linezolid; M, moxifloxacin; D, delamanid; Lfx, levofloxacin; C, clofazimine; Z, pyrazinamide. These letters name the full combination and do not authorize substitutions.

The 2025 U.S. panel recommends 26 weeks of BPaL/BPaLM, despite the 24-week TB-PRACTECAL study regimen. The usual adult framework uses pretomanid 200 mg daily, linezolid 600 mg daily and, for BPaLM, moxifloxacin 400 mg daily. One established bedaquiline schedule is 400 mg daily for 2 weeks, then 200 mg three times weekly, separated by at least 48 hours. Use the exact selected protocol; alternative bedaquiline schedules and toxicity-driven changes should not be mixed casually. [1]

Resistance is more than a rifampin result

Prior bedaquiline/clofazimine or nitroimidazole exposure can matter even when the patient has never received the proposed full combination. Review molecular and phenotypic results with the reference laboratory; the availability and interpretation of newer-drug DST differ from routine antibacterial testing. Loss of linezolid to toxicity can leave too few active companion drugs. Persistent or recurrent culture positivity should prompt renewed DST and adherence/exposure review, not automatic prolongation of the same drugs. [9] [1]

WHO recommendation, FDA labeling and local access are different questions. In the U.S., arrange expert consultation and access before prescribing delamanid-based or other nonstandard combinations. No local formulary availability is assumed here.

Baseline and follow-up plan

Document weight, HIV status, pregnancy possibility, complete medication history, prior TB therapy and disease sites. Obtain CBC, creatinine/eGFR, AST/ALT and bilirubin, with hepatitis testing based on risk. Add baseline vision testing for ethambutol or prolonged linezolid and ECG/electrolytes for QT-active regimens. [2] [1]

Review symptoms, adherence, weight and toxicity at least monthly. In pulmonary disease, obtain sputum cultures monthly until conversion is documented; the end-of-intensive-phase culture is particularly important. Continue cultures and other response assessment as required by the selected drug-resistant protocol. Smear or NAAT positivity alone is not proof of viable bacilli or failure. [2] [3] [9]

For prolonged linezolid, check CBC early and frequently, commonly weekly initially, with ongoing neurologic and visual assessment. Use the published regimen-specific ECG and laboratory schedule for BPaL/BPaLM or BDLLfxC. Increase surveillance when renal dysfunction, abnormal baseline results, QT-active combinations or new symptoms raise risk. Therapeutic drug monitoring may help with toxicity, suspected malabsorption or poor response. [1] [9]

New hepatitis needs a same-day plan

Hold hepatotoxic TB drugs and evaluate when ALT is at least 3 times the upper limit of normal with hepatitis symptoms, or at least 5 times without symptoms. Assess bilirubin, INR and other causes. In severe disease, arrange an active nonhepatotoxic interim combination with a TB expert. Reintroduction is supervised and sequential after recovery; do not leave the patient on an accidentally ineffective partial regimen.

[2]

With creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min or hemodialysis, pyrazinamide and ethambutol generally require interval adjustment, often three times weekly after dialysis. Isoniazid and rifampin usually retain standard dosing. Confirm the exact weight-based dose and monitoring with pharmacy. [28] [2]

For patients with HIV who are not receiving ART and do not have TB meningitis, NIH recommends starting ART within 2 weeks of TB treatment if CD4 is below 50 cells/mm³, and within 2-8 weeks if CD4 is at least 50. This is the timing used in the companion HIV module. For TB meningitis, NIH permits ART after at least 2 weeks once meningitis is controlled, while ATS/CDC/IDSA guidance favors waiting 8 weeks. Source control in TB is selective

Antimicrobial therapy is central. Drainage, decompression or surgery is indicated for specific complications, such as neurologic compromise, mechanical instability, selected abscesses or empyema, rather than for every residual lesion. Obtain tissue for histology, mycobacterial culture and DST when diagnosis remains uncertain. Coordinate specimen handling so the only diagnostic sample is not placed entirely in formalin. [2] [5]

Read the endpoint before accepting a shorter treatment claim

Noninferiority means that the confidence interval stayed within a prespecified margin for a specified endpoint in a specified population. It does not establish identical efficacy, prove superiority or show that the regimen works at every disease site. A composite outcome can include loss to follow-up, treatment changes and death as well as microbiologic failure.

Phase 3 RCT

Study 31/A5349 established an eligible four-month option

Among 2,516 randomized participants, the four-month rifapentine-moxifloxacin regimen had unfavorable outcomes in 15.5% versus 14.6% with standard therapy in the microbiologically eligible population. The reported difference was 1.0 percentage point, 95% CI -2.6 to 4.5, within a 6-percentage-point noninferiority margin. The rifapentine regimen without moxifloxacin did not establish noninferiority.

Practice implication: use the complete studied regimen in eligible pulmonary TB. The result does not support arbitrary four-month courses, and the trial exclusions remain clinically important. [10] [4]

Randomized trial

TB-PRACTECAL changed the burden of resistant-TB treatment

The 2022 report evaluated 24-week BPaLM against 9-20 months of standard care. Of 301 stage-2 participants, 128 were included in the modified intention-to-treat efficacy population. Unfavorable outcomes at 72 weeks occurred in 11% versus 48%; the risk difference was -37 percentage points, 96.6% CI -53 to -22. Grade 3 or higher or serious adverse events occurred in 19% versus 59%.

The comparator, open-label design, early stopping and composite endpoint matter. This was a major efficacy/tolerability advance over the comparator regimens, but the 37-point difference should not be described as a mortality benefit or as a comparison against every modern oral regimen. [11]

Dose/duration RCT

ZeNix helped reduce linezolid exposure while preserving activity

All 181 participants received bedaquiline and pretomanid; linezolid was randomized to 1,200 or 600 mg daily for 26 or 9 weeks. Favorable outcomes were 93%, 89%, 91% and 84%, respectively. In the 600-mg/26-week group, peripheral neuropathy occurred in 24% and myelosuppression in 2%.

This supports the current lower-dose approach and intensive toxicity surveillance. It was not a comparison of BPaL with an external standard-care control, and four modest-sized groups cannot exclude important efficacy differences. [12] [1]

Phase 3 RCT

endTB provided several nine-month alternatives

The trial randomized 754 participants with fluoroquinolone-susceptible RR pulmonary TB; 699 entered the modified intention-to-treat analysis. At week 73, the control favorable-outcome rate was 80.7%. Differences versus control were +9.8 points for BCLLfxZ (95% CI 0.9 to 18.7), +8.3 for BLMZ (-0.8 to 17.4), and +4.6 for BDLLfxZ (-4.9 to 14.1). These three regimens had consistent noninferiority support across analyses, using a 12-point margin.

DCMZ met the modified intention-to-treat criterion but was not supported by the per-protocol analysis. Grade 3 or higher adverse events were common across arms. This trial expands options; it does not establish that nine-month therapy is preferable to BPaLM. [13]

Phase 3 RCT · 2026

BEAT-TB supports a pretomanid-free six-month strategy

This pragmatic South African trial randomized 403 participants to six-month bedaquiline, delamanid and linezolid with levofloxacin and/or clofazimine, adjusted to susceptibility, or the local standard strategy. Treatment success sustained through week 76 occurred in 174/202 (86.1%) versus 172/200 (86.0%). The adjusted difference was -0.2 points, 95% CI -6.9 to 6.5, meeting the trial noninferiority criterion.

The study included groups often excluded elsewhere, including pregnancy and people with HIV. Inclusion does not make every subgroup estimate precise. Its comparator was not BPaLM. WHO incorporated the underlying BEAT evidence into 2025 recommendations before this 2026 journal publication. [14] [9]

The next advances must demonstrate durable cure and acceptable toxicity

Investigational strategy

TRUNCATE-TB is not routine two-month treatment

The strategy trial included 674 participants in its intention-to-treat population. Initial eight-week bedaquiline-linezolid, with isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol, was followed by extension for persistent disease, surveillance and retreatment for relapse. At week 96, death, ongoing treatment or active TB occurred in 5.8% versus 3.9% with standard treatment. The adjusted difference was 0.8 points, 97.5% CI -3.4 to 5.1. Mean total treatment time was 85 versus 180 days. [15]

The 2025 regimen analysis used a different question. Unfavorable outcomes, mainly relapse, occurred in 26/189 (14%) on the bedaquiline-linezolid regimen versus 7/181 (4%) with standard therapy; adjusted difference 9.3 points, 95% Bayesian credible interval 4.3 to 14.9. Successful retreatment could rescue the strategy outcome while the initial regimen still had more relapses. Do not present these results as equivalent durable cure after eight weeks. [16]

Phase 2b/c · 2026

Quabodepistat offers a new target and a rifamycin-free combination

Quabodepistat inhibits DprE1, a cell-wall synthesis target. A proof-of-concept trial randomized 122 participants with drug-susceptible pulmonary TB to four months of delamanid-bedaquiline-quabodepistat at three dose levels or six months of standard treatment. In the modified intention-to-treat population, end-of-treatment sputum culture conversion was 96/100 (96%) across pooled experimental groups and 21/21 (100%) in control. The difference was -4.0 points, with an 80% CI of -7.4 to 3.4, meeting its 12-point margin.

This is encouraging regimen-development evidence, not proof of equal relapse-free cure. The small control arm, pooled doses, surrogate primary endpoint and 80% rather than 95% interval limit certainty. It does not establish effectiveness in drug-resistant or extrapulmonary TB, and it is not a routine prescribing option. [17]

Phase 2b dose finding

Sutezolid aims to improve on linezolid's therapeutic window

PanACEA-SUDOCU-01 randomized 75 participants to different sutezolid doses or no sutezolid on a bedaquiline-delamanid-moxifloxacin background. The pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic model estimated a 16.7% increase in time-to-positivity slope steepness at exposure typical of 1,200 mg, 95% CI 0.7 to 35.0, relative to no exposure. No neuropathy was reported.

This is a model-based bacteriologic effect, not a 16.7% improvement in cure. The study did not establish a final dose, durable-cure advantage or comparative long-course safety against linezolid. Sutezolid remains a research option. [18]

Phase 2 early activity

Telacebec demonstrates activity without establishing a treatment regimen

In a 64-patient randomized trial, telacebec 100, 200 or 300 mg daily was compared with standard four-drug treatment for 14 days. At 300 mg, the mean daily decline in sputum bacterial load was 0.097 log CFU versus 0.200 with the four-drug control. Activity was dose-dependent and tolerability was favorable during this short observation period.

The study confirms human antimycobacterial activity of an energy-production target. It does not establish monotherapy as treatment, a cure rate, resistance protection or safety over months. Combination studies with relapse follow-up are needed. [19]

Negative phase 3 result

HARVEST did not show a survival benefit from high-dose rifampin in TB meningitis

In 499 adults, adding rifampin to reach 35 mg/kg daily for 8 weeks was compared with 10 mg/kg daily, both within multidrug treatment. Six-month mortality estimates were 44.6% versus 40.7%; hazard ratio 1.17, 95% CI 0.89 to 1.54, P=0.25.

Improved drug exposure is not itself clinical benefit. This trial does not support routine high-dose oral rifampin to improve adult TB-meningitis survival. The interval also does not prove the two strategies equally safe. [20]

Host-directed therapy needs the same evidentiary discipline

In the 2026 genotype-stratified dexamethasone trial in HIV-negative TB meningitis, neither noninferiority nor superiority of placebo was established in the LTA4H CC/CT groups. This does not justify routine genotype-guided steroid omission. Serious adverse events were similar, 52.8% with dexamethasone versus 51.9% with placebo. [21]

For other host-directed agents or long-acting formulations, distinguish preclinical activity, exposure studies and registered trials from demonstrated patient benefit. A proposed anti-inflammatory mechanism or convenient dosing interval is not a reason to add a drug to a standard regimen.

For every new avenue, ask whether it reduces relapse, death, toxicity or treatment burden, and whether resistance was measured. This is a selected evidence review, not an exhaustive inventory of every compound in development. No unpublished cure rates are assumed.

Preventive treatment is a different treatment problem

Exclude active TB before prescribing preventive monotherapy or dual therapy. Symptoms, radiographic abnormalities or significant immunosuppression may require sputum testing even when the referral says “latent TB.” [24]

Turn a positive test into a complete preventive-care plan

First exclude disease. Then establish exposure timing, source-case susceptibility, prior treatment, pregnancy status, liver risk and all medications. Recent contacts and people facing substantial immunosuppression deserve prompt coordination. Prefer a short rifamycin regimen when feasible; interactions may favor isoniazid instead. Apply the regimen and evidence to the bedside

Case 1. A negative IGRA is not an exit from the workup

A 47-year-old receiving anti-TNF therapy has 6 weeks of cough, weight loss and an upper-lobe cavity. The IGRA is negative.

Can airborne precautions and the TB evaluation be stopped?

Reason through the decision

No. Continue the disease evaluation and airborne precautions. Collect appropriate respiratory specimens, NAAT and cultures. Immunosuppression can blunt immune-based tests. Decide on empiric multidrug treatment from the full clinical picture, not the IGRA alone. [5] [6]

Case 2. A genuine four-month candidate

A 28-year-old, 65-kg patient has pulmonary TB with susceptible testing, no extrapulmonary disease, no pregnancy, no relevant interactions and acceptable QT risk.

What is the shorter option, and what is not interchangeable?

Reason through the decision

Discuss 8 weeks of HPZM followed by 9 weeks of HPM with the TB program. Confirm the full daily 119-dose course and follow-up. Four months of standard RIPE-based therapy or rifapentine without moxifloxacin does not reproduce the evidence. [4] [10]

Case 3. Cavitation and a positive two-month culture

A patient on standard HRZE had cavitary disease at baseline. The culture obtained at completion of 2 months remains positive and is still drug susceptible.

Is this automatically treatment failure?

Reason through the decision

It is a relapse-risk signal, not automatic failure at two months. Review observed doses, interactions, absorption and DST. With cavitation plus the positive two-month culture, plan a 7-month continuation phase, usually 9 months total, under TB-program direction. [3] [2]

Case 4. Rifampin resistance is not solved by adding moxifloxacin

A respiratory NAAT detects rifampin resistance. Fluoroquinolone susceptibility is pending.

What information must guide the next regimen?

Reason through the decision

Arrange urgent TB-expert consultation, expanded molecular and phenotypic DST, prior-drug history and an active resistant-TB combination. FQ susceptibility distinguishes BPaLM from BPaL eligibility. Pregnancy, disease site and component resistance can rule out both. Do not simply add one drug to HRZE. [7] [1]

Case 5. Linezolid toxicity threatens the combination

During BPaLM therapy, a patient develops falling hemoglobin and new distal tingling. Cultures have converted.

Can linezolid simply be stopped for the remainder?

Reason through the decision

Assess severity and hold or modify the offending drug when clinically necessary, but make the revised regimen with a TB expert. Culture conversion does not eliminate the need for active companion drugs. Check CBC, neurologic findings, visual symptoms, drug exposure and other causes. The duration of prior linezolid and activity of remaining drugs determine the options. [1] [9]

Case 6. An eight-week headline does not establish an eight-week cure

A patient asks for the “two-month TB treatment” after reading about TRUNCATE-TB.

How would you explain the actual evidence?

Reason through the decision

The trial shortened initial treatment and then closely monitored patients, extending or restarting therapy when needed. The later analysis found more unfavorable outcomes with the initial short regimen. It is not a routine alternative to the recommended four- or six-month course. [15] [16]

Case 7. An AFB-positive result without a TB NAAT signal

A 70-year-old with bronchiectasis has chronic cough. The sputum is smear positive but the TB NAAT is negative.

Is this confirmed TB?

Reason through the decision

No. Consider NTM, specimen or assay issues, and the remaining probability of TB. Ask the laboratory about the discordance, collect additional specimens and await identification/culture. Make the isolation decision with IP; one result does not replace the institutional pathway. If NTM is isolated, assess full disease criteria before treating. [29] [32]

Case 8. A positive IGRA and a growing lung mass

A patient with remote TB exposure has a positive IGRA and an enlarging cavitary lung lesion. Initial sputum studies are unrevealing.

Does the IGRA justify closing the differential?

Reason through the decision

No. Infection testing does not identify the cause of the lesion. Continue a directed TB evaluation and consider tissue for malignancy, fungal infection and other mimics. Coordinate fresh tissue for cultures alongside pathology. [5] [34]

Case 9. Prevention before anti-TNF therapy

An asymptomatic adult has a positive IGRA before anti-TNF treatment, a normal chest radiograph and no evidence of active disease.

What makes a useful LTBI plan?

Reason through the decision

Choose a compatible short-course regimen when possible after medication and exposure review. Specify doses, monitoring, adherence support and documentation. Coordinate timing of immunosuppression with the treating team; do not apply one universal interval to every biologic and clinical urgency. [35] [24]

A useful consult note specifies what happens next

Communicate the disease classification, infectiousness assessment, regimen and its rationale, completed doses, pending resistance results, toxicity plan and person responsible for follow-up. A prescription without an access and follow-up plan is incomplete.

Suspected/confirmed TB at [site], with [microbiology and resistance results]. Airborne precautions [status and criteria to reassess with infection prevention]. Regimen [full drug names, doses, start date and rationale]. Pending tests [culture, molecular and phenotypic DST, other sites]. Monitoring [specific tests, next date and clinician/program reviewing them]. Duration [provisional course and findings that would change it]. Public-health coordination [program, contact and plan for DOT/vDOT]. Discharge [medication supply, follow-up, household risk assessment and transport plan].

Local adaptation

Insert the current BJC isolation algorithm, laboratory ordering names, reporting contacts, local TB-program pathway and pharmacy access instructions before using this as institutional policy. These have not been supplied or verified. Do not substitute a lecture summary for the local isolation decision.

Check that you can explain the decisions

Pause before reading each answer. These are discussion questions for individual study or facilitated teaching.

1. Why should a positive NAAT still be followed by culture?

Answer and rationale

Culture supports organism recovery, susceptibility testing and response assessment. A molecular result does not replace those functions. [5]

2. Which two findings commonly extend the standard pulmonary course to nine months?

Answer and rationale

Baseline cavitation plus a positive sputum culture at completion of two months. Investigate adherence, susceptibility and exposure as part of the same decision. [3]

3. How does daily rifapentine for active TB differ from 3HP?

Answer and rationale

The active-TB regimen uses daily rifapentine within HPZM/HPM for 17 weeks. 3HP uses weekly isoniazid-rifapentine for prevention. Neither the schedules nor the treatment purposes are interchangeable. [4]

4. When does BPaL replace BPaLM in the U.S. guideline?

Answer and rationale

In otherwise eligible rifampin-resistant pulmonary disease with fluoroquinolone resistance or intolerance. Component activity and other eligibility restrictions still apply. [1]

5. What is the response to symptomatic ALT elevation above three times the upper limit of normal?

Answer and rationale

Hold hepatotoxic drugs and evaluate promptly. Determine severity and an active interim/reintroduction plan with the TB team rather than continuing an ineffective partial regimen. [2]

6. What does the quabodepistat primary endpoint fail to prove?

Answer and rationale

End-of-treatment sputum conversion is not the same as relapse-free cure. A small proof-of-concept study cannot establish effectiveness across disease sites or resistance patterns. [17]

7. Why can the TRUNCATE strategy succeed while its initial regimen has more relapse?

Answer and rationale

The strategy includes detection and retreatment of relapse; successful retreatment can prevent a week-96 strategy failure. That does not erase failure of the initial course. [16]

8. Can a contact with cough and an abnormal radiograph start levofloxacin prevention immediately?

Answer and rationale

Evaluate for active TB first. If disease is present, monotherapy is inappropriate and risks acquired resistance. Source-case DST also informs preventive selection. [25]

9. Does a positive IGRA explain a cavitary lung lesion?

Answer and rationale

No. It supports infection but does not establish the cause of the lesion. Evaluate TB disease and competing diagnoses, with microbiology and tissue when indicated. [5]

10. What must happen before starting 4R for latent infection?

Answer and rationale

Exclude active TB, review source resistance and drug interactions, and arrange dosing, counseling and monthly follow-up. Rifampin alone is inappropriate treatment for active TB. [35]

TB on rounds: diagnose, distinguish, treat

Adult-oriented memory aid • September 15, 2026 • Use with the eligibility and dosing details in the full module.

Do not anchor on TB

Consider NTM, endemic fungi, bacterial abscess/emboli, Nocardia, cancer and inflammatory disease. IGRA positivity does not explain a lesion; target culture and tissue.

Treat latent infection

Exclude active disease first. Prefer eligible 3HP, 4R or 3HR; use 6H/9H when appropriate. Check interactions, source resistance and pregnancy; arrange monthly review.

Recognize and isolate

Suspected pulmonary/laryngeal TB: airborne precautions, appropriate respirator and prompt IP/TB-program contact. IGRA/TST cannot rule out disease.

Get the right samples

Sputum AFB smear/culture, typically three specimens 8-24 h apart with one early morning; respiratory NAAT and rapid resistance testing. Preserve culture for DST.

Drug-susceptible pulmonary TB

Standard 2HRZE/4HR. Eligible age ≥12, weight ≥40 kg: 8 weeks HPZM then 9 weeks HPM. Pregnancy, most extrapulmonary disease, interactions or resistance may preclude shortening.

Reassess duration

For the standard regimen, cavitation plus positive culture at 2 months usually means 9 months total. Review doses, DST and exposure. CNS and bone/joint disease need site-specific plans.

Rifampin-resistant disease

Urgent expert input. FQ susceptible: consider BPaLM; FQ resistant/intolerant: consider BPaL if otherwise eligible. U.S. guidance uses 26 weeks. Never add one drug to failure.

Monitor the combination

Monthly response/adherence review and sputum cultures until conversion. Liver tests; CBC/neuropathy/vision for linezolid; vision for ethambutol; ECG and K+/Mg2+/Ca2+ for QT-active regimens.

Act on toxicity

Hepatitis symptoms plus ALT ≥3× ULN or ALT ≥5× without symptoms: hold hepatotoxic agents and evaluate. New neuropathy, cytopenia or visual change requires prompt review.

Keep evidence in context

BEAT and endTB expand selected options. Quabodepistat/sutezolid/telacebec remain investigational. TRUNCATE tested surveillance and retreatment, not universal two-month cure.
