This lecture is intended as a supplementary resource for clinicians and trainees to review evidence and discuss clinical cases.

Edited November 4, 2026. Adult clinical ID teaching. Organism identity does not replace isolate-specific susceptibility testing, infection-site assessment, and source control. Drug examples assume susceptible isolates and appropriate exposure; verify contemporary CLSI breakpoints and local formulary.

Learning objectives

  1. Predict important intrinsic resistance from species identification before the antibiogram returns.
  2. Distinguish inducible AmpC, ESBL, carbapenemases, and non-beta-lactam resistance mechanisms.
  3. Explain why seemingly active antibiotics can be poor choices at particular sites.
  4. Recognize unusual species/phenotypes that require a focused search for the source, endovascular involvement, or metastatic infection.
  5. Interpret rare nonfermenters cautiously, including when a respiratory isolate may be colonization.

Resident section

Initial interpretation of gram-negative cultures

  • Is the isolate causing infection, colonization, or specimen contamination? Check sample quality, burden, repeated recovery, syndrome, and host factors.
  • Enterobacterales, nonfermenter, fastidious organism, or enteric pathogen? Species identification often predicts important gaps.
  • Is this a urinary, biliary, GI, pulmonary, vascular, bone/joint, CNS, or device infection? Look for source control and a plausible portal of entry.
  • Is resistance intrinsic, inducible, or acquired? Which susceptibility results are actually validated for that organism?
  • Is antibiotic exposure sufficient at the infected site? An active MIC does not guarantee clinical efficacy.

AmpC species differences

Organism groupPractical approach
Enterobacter cloacae complex, Klebsiella aerogenes, Citrobacter freundii, Hafnia alveiAn isolate may initially test susceptible to ceftriaxone and become resistant as AmpC expression increases. Clinical reports support this risk for the first three species; inclusion of H. alvei rests mainly on laboratory evidence. For invasive disease, cefepime is preferred when the result is susceptible or susceptible dose-dependent, the required exposure is achievable, and no ESBL gene has been identified.1
Serratia marcescens, Morganella morganii, Providencia spp.Clinically significant AmpC overexpression is less common in this group. Susceptibility results usually guide selection. A high bacterial burden and limited source control, as with endocarditis, may still favor cefepime even when ceftriaxone tests susceptible.1

For these Enterobacterales, a cefepime MIC of 4–8 µg/mL is susceptible dose-dependent in the cited guidance. That category assumes a regimen that achieves increased exposure. The MIC alone does not identify an ESBL.1 Nitrofurantoin is not used for pyelonephritis or bacteremia. Oral fosfomycin is not initial therapy for pyelonephritis or routine bacteremia treatment; selected ESBL-producing E. coli cUTI transition use after active IV therapy, when other oral options are unavailable, is discussed in the Antibiotics Primer.

Organism-specific characteristics

Pseudomonas aeruginosa

  • Appropriate antipseudomonal fluoroquinolones are ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin when susceptible, not moxifloxacin.
  • Ertapenem and ceftriaxone do not cover Pseudomonas.
  • Carbapenem resistance does not guarantee ceftolozane-tazobactam resistance; mechanism matters. Likewise, resistance to one newer agent cannot be extrapolated to all.
  • For DTR P. aeruginosa infections outside the urinary tract, when no carbapenemase has been detected, preferred options include active ceftolozane-tazobactam, ceftazidime-avibactam, or imipenem-relebactam; cefiderocol is an alternative. In pneumonia, observational comparative data support a preference for susceptible ceftolozane-tazobactam. That preference does not extend to KPC or MBL producers. For NDM, VIM or IMP producers, the guidance favors cefiderocol. Site, drug-specific susceptibility, and renal function remain relevant.1
  • Recurrent same-species infection: evaluate obstruction, hardware, biofilm, abscess or inadequate source control before simply broadening therapy.

Stenotrophomonas maltophilia

  • Environmental nonfermenter; recovered frequently from respiratory samples with uncertain significance. A positive BAL is not sufficient by itself to diagnose pneumonia.
  • Intrinsically resistant to carbapenems and many beta-lactams through L1 metallo-beta-lactamase and L2 serine beta-lactamase, with other mechanisms.
  • When S. maltophilia causes invasive infection, the 2026 preference for cefiderocol alone rests mainly on susceptibility results and animal infection models. Human studies have not established superior outcomes. Alternatives include aztreonam-avibactam, preferably with another active agent, or combination regimens incorporating TMP-SMX, minocycline, or levofloxacin when appropriate. Growth in a respiratory specimen alone is not an indication for these regimens.1
  • Older ceftazidime susceptibility results are not a sound reason to use ceftazidime monotherapy.

Acinetobacter baumannii complex

  • Distinguish colonization from infection especially in respiratory cultures and chronic wounds.
  • For invasive CRAB infection, sulbactam-durlobactam plus imipenem or meropenem is preferred when active. An MBL or demonstrated resistance to sulbactam-durlobactam undermines the usual sulbactam strategy.1
  • The sulbactam component is active against the organism, not merely a beta-lactamase inhibitor.

Achromobacter xylosoxidans

  • Seen in chronic lung disease and healthcare-associated bloodstream/device infections. Resistance via efflux pumps and beta-lactamases is common, but susceptibility is isolate-dependent.
  • Potential agents include piperacillin-tazobactam, TMP-SMX, ceftazidime, or meropenem when active. Do not presume cefepime, aztreonam, aminoglycosides or fluoroquinolones are reliable.
  • Evidence is limited, laboratory AST methods and interpretive criteria matter, and there is no universal fixed regimen. For bacteremia investigate catheter involvement.

Burkholderia cepacia complex

  • Important in cystic fibrosis and chronic granulomatous disease, and can be healthcare associated. Intrinsic resistance to many agents, including polymyxins and often aminoglycosides.
  • TMP-SMX, ceftazidime, meropenem and minocycline have each been used for B. cepacia complex infections. A measured MIC is not proof that any of them will work. CLSI withdrew disk diffusion breakpoints in 2024 and MIC breakpoints in 2025; interim epidemiological cutoff values were withdrawn in 2026. Those cutoffs were never clinical susceptibility breakpoints. Borrowing Pseudomonas breakpoints would give unwarranted certainty. Interpretation depends on the testing method and the clinical syndrome.6,12

Elizabethkingia species

  • Unusual nonfermenters with frequent resistance to carbapenems and many beta-lactams. Reported susceptibility profiles vary considerably by species and testing platform.
  • Confirm identification and AST with microbiology; avoid blanket drug recommendations from a genus name alone.

Klebsiella pneumoniae: hypervirulence versus hypermucoviscosity

  • Hypervirulent lineages can cause community-onset liver abscess, bacteremia, endophthalmitis, and CNS metastatic infection, sometimes without typical host risk factors.
  • Hypermucoviscosity is a phenotype and is not equivalent to hypervirulence. A positive string test alone does not establish a hypervirulent strain; molecular virulence markers are more informative.
  • New visual symptoms in a patient with K. pneumoniae bacteremia or liver abscess warrant urgent ophthalmologic evaluation for endophthalmitis. A cryptogenic abscess or another metastatic focus also raises concern. These findings support targeted assessment; bacteremia alone does not establish ocular infection.9

Proteus, Morganella, Providencia, Serratia

  • Proteus mirabilis makes urease; recurrent infection can signal struvite stones or a retained urinary nidus. Nitrofurantoin is not reliable.
  • Morganella/Providencia/Serratia should not be collapsed into the moderate-risk AmpC category. Polymyxins are generally inactive, and susceptibility to tetracycline derivatives may be problematic.
  • Serratia can cause healthcare-associated infection, device infection and endocarditis; prolonged bacteremia requires source evaluation, not simply automatic carbapenem treatment.

Enteric pathogens and invasive complications

  • Salmonella: typhoidal versus nontyphoidal disease; why bacteremia and vascular infection change the evaluation.
  • Shigella: low inoculum, transmission, and emerging extensively resistant strains; request isolate-specific AST when treating.
  • Campylobacter: C. jejuni enteritis versus C. fetus systemic disease; macrolide versus fluoroquinolone resistance patterns.
  • Yersinia enterocolitica: terminal ileitis/mesenteric adenitis mimicking appendicitis; iron-related susceptibility in invasive disease.
  • Vibrio and Aeromonas: exposure history can change acute management, particularly when a rapidly progressive wound infection is present.

Fellow section: additional resistance and phenotypes

Common errors in susceptibility interpretation

  1. In E. cloacae complex bacteremia, ceftriaxone susceptibility describes the initial isolate. AmpC expression can increase during treatment, which is why cefepime is generally favored when active, exposure is adequate, and an ESBL has not been identified. Source control remains a separate decision.1
  2. A cefepime-susceptible result in ESBL-producing E. coli bacteremia does not make cefepime a reliable definitive choice. The bloodstream syndrome and ESBL mechanism favor a susceptible carbapenem.1
  3. For invasive NDM-producing Klebsiella infection, aztreonam-avibactam and cefiderocol are preferred options if active. Ceftazidime-avibactam alone does not overcome NDM. No direct comparison establishes aztreonam-avibactam as superior to cefiderocol.1
  4. DTR Pseudomonas susceptible to a novel agent. Determine infection site, dosing, MIC and source control. Do not assume all new beta-lactams are interchangeable or that meropenem-vaborbactam is an antipseudomonal salvage option.
  5. Stenotrophomonas from BAL in a patient whose imaging and oxygen requirement are improving without active therapy. Colonization may be more plausible than invasive infection. Treat the patient and syndrome, not the culture in isolation.

Susceptibility testing limitations

  • A susceptibility result is meaningful only if the agent-organism combination has a validated interpretive framework; automated panels can be unreliable for unusual nonfermenters.
  • Confirm unusual carbapenemase genotypes and discordant phenotypes with the laboratory. Phenotypic carbapenem resistance does not identify the underlying enzyme.
  • When the isolate remains clinically important but routine panel lacks modern agents, request supplemental AST through the local laboratory/reference lab; do not infer cefiderocol, aztreonam-avibactam or ceftolozane-tazobactam activity from a different drug.
  • Interpret susceptibility alongside required exposure, inoculum, drug distribution and source control. No drug is uniquely required for an entire species in every syndrome.

Evidence limitations in the 2026 guidance

  • Most important practical changes: protected aztreonam availability for NDM Enterobacterales; cefiderocol monotherapy moving to preferred for invasive S. maltophilia; firmer prioritization of sulbactam-durlobactam in invasive CRAB; refinement of DTR Pseudomonas pneumonia treatment.
  • For AmpC, the revised organism grouping and the narrow ceftriaxone continuation exception answer different questions: what resistance might emerge, and how a patient is already responding after source control.
  • Discuss uncertainty: the Stenotrophomonas preference rests on limited human comparative evidence. Mechanism-directed treatment is crucial, but many uncommon resistant species remain poorly studied.

Fellow discussion questions

  1. A stable patient with a nonsevere E. cloacae intra-abdominal infection is improving on empiric ceftriaxone after adequate source control. How would the discussion change if an abscess remained undrained or the patient became unstable?
  2. A patient has NDM-producing K. pneumoniae bacteremia. Which agents are preferred, how is aztreonam protected, and why is ceftazidime-avibactam alone inadequate?
  3. An ICU BAL grows S. maltophilia. When would treatment be unnecessary? If invasive infection is established, why might cefiderocol monotherapy be favored now, and why should confidence in comparative clinical efficacy remain limited?

Clinical cases

Case 1: initially ceftriaxone-susceptible Enterobacter bacteremia

An obstructed urinary tract is the source of E. cloacae complex bacteremia. The isolate initially tests susceptible to ceftriaxone and cefepime. Why does this differ from cystitis? Obstruction is an unresolved source, and AmpC expression may undermine ceftriaxone during therapy. Drainage and an active drug that reaches the bloodstream address different parts of the problem. For cefepime, the actual MIC and achievable exposure matter.1

Case 2: Serratia bacteremia in a patient with a prosthetic valve

Two blood culture sets grow S. marcescens in a patient with a prosthetic valve. The species carries less risk of AmpC overexpression than E. cloacae complex, but a focus with high bacterial burden and limited source control may still influence drug selection.1 Bacteremia in a patient with a prosthetic valve raises concern for endocarditis without proving it. What would repeat cultures and echocardiography clarify? Which findings would prompt assessment with the valve team?

Case 3: Klebsiella liver abscess and blurred vision

A patient with K. pneumoniae bacteremia and a liver abscess reports new blurred vision. Possible endophthalmitis warrants urgent ophthalmologic assessment while the team addresses the abscess and bloodstream infection. A positive string test describes hypermucoviscosity; it cannot establish hypervirulence or decide whether the eye needs evaluation.5,9

Case 4: BAL growing Stenotrophomonas

A ventilated patient is improving without an antibiotic active against the S. maltophilia recovered from BAL, and another diagnosis explains the infiltrates. Colonization is plausible. What new clinical findings would make pneumonia more likely? Only if infection is supported do the susceptibility result and the limited human evidence for treatment options become relevant.1

Case 5: NDM Klebsiella bloodstream infection

Blood cultures grow NDM-producing K. pneumoniae. Ceftazidime-avibactam alone does not address NDM; other beta-lactamases can inactivate aztreonam given alone. Aztreonam-avibactam or cefiderocol are preferred options if active. If the coformulation is unavailable, ceftazidime-avibactam plus aztreonam is an alternative. This bloodstream example illustrates guidance for invasive NDM-producing Enterobacterales, not a separate U.S. bacteremia indication for aztreonam-avibactam.1,7

Case 6: Pseudomonas and the wrong fluoroquinolone

A urine culture grows P. aeruginosa susceptible to ciprofloxacin. A trainee recommends oral moxifloxacin. Explain why same-class labeling is not sufficient, and why moxifloxacin is also a poor urinary agent.

Case 7: painful leg after freshwater injury

Rapidly progressive pain and bullae after freshwater trauma raise concern for a necrotizing infection. Do not wait for cultures before urgent surgical assessment and empiric antibiotics. When the wound grows Aeromonas, exposure history and isolate-specific testing help narrow therapy; cefazolin alone is unreliable.10,11

Summary

A species name helps predict which susceptibility results deserve a second look. The next question is whether this isolate explains the illness and whether an active drug will reach the infected site. With an unusual isolate, knowing how the laboratory identified and tested it can be as useful as the reported MIC.
