Gram-negative bacteria: species matter

Resident primer (20 minutes) + fellow section (30-40 minutes)

Updated October 27, 2026. Adult clinical ID teaching. Organism identity does not replace isolate-specific susceptibility testing, infection-site assessment, and source control. Drug examples assume susceptible isolates and appropriate exposure; verify contemporary CLSI breakpoints and local formulary. The lecture intentionally does not reproduce internal institutional material.

Learning objectives

  1. Predict important intrinsic resistance from species identification before the antibiogram returns.
  2. Distinguish inducible AmpC, ESBL, carbapenemases, and non-beta-lactam resistance mechanisms.
  3. Explain why seemingly active antibiotics can be poor choices at particular sites.
  4. Recognize unusual species/phenotypes that require a focused search for the source, endovascular involvement, or metastatic infection.
  5. Interpret rare nonfermenters cautiously, including when a respiratory isolate may be colonization.

Resident section: a practical framework

First questions after a gram-negative culture

  • Is the isolate causing infection, colonization, or specimen contamination? Check sample quality, burden, repeated recovery, syndrome, and host factors.
  • Enterobacterales, nonfermenter, fastidious organism, or enteric pathogen? Species identification often predicts important gaps.
  • Is this a urinary, biliary, GI, pulmonary, vascular, bone/joint, CNS, or device infection? Look for source control and a plausible portal of entry.
  • Is resistance intrinsic, inducible, or acquired? Which susceptibility results are actually validated for that organism?
  • Is antibiotic exposure sufficient at the infected site? An active MIC does not guarantee clinical efficacy.

AmpC: do not lump every species together

Organism groupPractical approach
Enterobacter cloacae complex, Klebsiella aerogenes, Citrobacter freundii, Hafnia alveiModerate risk of clinically significant derepression of chromosomal inducible AmpC. Ceftriaxone susceptibility at baseline can be misleading in invasive infection. Cefepime is an option when susceptible, appropriately dosed, and no important ESBL co-production concern. Carbapenem in selected severe/resistant scenarios.
Serratia marcescens, Morganella morganii, Providencia spp.Lower risk of clinically significant AmpC overexpression. Do not automatically mandate cefepime/carbapenem for all infections. Follow AST, infection severity, inoculum and source control.
E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilisAcquired plasmid AmpC and ESBL remain possible. Do not assume every third-generation cephalosporin resistance is inducible AmpC.

Cefepime susceptibility must be interpreted with the MIC, appropriate dosage, site of infection, and possibility of additional beta-lactamases. Nitrofurantoin and oral fosfomycin do not treat pyelonephritis or bacteremia.

Distinguish MDR, XDR and DTR. DTR Pseudomonas describes resistance to a particular set of conventional antipseudomonal agents, not simply resistance to one carbapenem. New resistance can arise on treatment: repeat cultures and AST for clinical failure, relapse, or breakthrough bacteremia.

Organism-focused exceptions

Pseudomonas aeruginosa

  • Appropriate antipseudomonal fluoroquinolones are ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin when susceptible, not moxifloxacin.
  • Ertapenem and ceftriaxone do not cover Pseudomonas.
  • Carbapenem resistance does not guarantee ceftolozane-tazobactam resistance; mechanism matters. Likewise, resistance to one newer agent cannot be extrapolated to all.
  • For DTR infections outside the urinary tract, the 2026 IDSA preferred agents include ceftolozane-tazobactam, ceftazidime-avibactam and imipenem-relebactam; cefiderocol is an alternative. For DTR pneumonia, guidance particularly favors ceftolozane-tazobactam. Document AST and adjust for renal function.
  • Recurrent same-species infection: evaluate obstruction, hardware, biofilm, abscess or inadequate source control before simply broadening therapy.

Stenotrophomonas maltophilia

  • Environmental nonfermenter; recovered frequently from respiratory samples with uncertain significance. A positive BAL is not sufficient by itself to diagnose pneumonia.
  • Intrinsically resistant to carbapenems and many beta-lactams through L1 metallo-beta-lactamase and L2 serine beta-lactamase, with other mechanisms.
  • For invasive infection, the 2026 IDSA guidance prefers cefiderocol monotherapy, largely on preclinical evidence rather than comparative clinical trials. Aztreonam-avibactam (preferably with another active agent), or combination regimens including TMP-SMX, minocycline, or levofloxacin are alternatives. Clinical outcome evidence remains limited; review AST, severity and tolerability. The historical assertion that TMP-SMX is the single required first-line treatment is outdated.
  • Older ceftazidime susceptibility results are not a sound reason to use ceftazidime monotherapy.

Acinetobacter baumannii complex

  • Distinguish colonization from infection especially in respiratory cultures and chronic wounds.
  • Carbapenem-resistant isolates often have extensive intrinsic/acquired resistance; treat invasive CRAB with a sulbactam-containing regimen, preferably sulbactam-durlobactam plus imipenem/meropenem where appropriate.
  • The sulbactam component is active against the organism, not merely a beta-lactamase inhibitor.

Achromobacter xylosoxidans

  • Seen in chronic lung disease and healthcare-associated bloodstream/device infections. Resistance via efflux pumps and beta-lactamases is common, but susceptibility is isolate-dependent.
  • Potential agents include piperacillin-tazobactam, TMP-SMX, ceftazidime, or meropenem when active. Do not presume cefepime, aztreonam, aminoglycosides or fluoroquinolones are reliable.
  • Evidence is limited, laboratory AST methods and interpretive criteria matter, and there is no universal fixed regimen. For bacteremia investigate catheter involvement.

Burkholderia cepacia complex

  • Important in cystic fibrosis and chronic granulomatous disease, and can be healthcare associated. Intrinsic resistance to many agents, including polymyxins and often aminoglycosides.
  • TMP-SMX, ceftazidime, meropenem, and minocycline may be candidates based on species and AST. Do not extrapolate Pseudomonas susceptibility patterns.

Elizabethkingia species

  • Unusual nonfermenters with frequent resistance to carbapenems and many beta-lactams. Reported susceptibility profiles vary considerably by species and testing platform.
  • Confirm identification and AST with microbiology; avoid blanket drug recommendations from a genus name alone.

Klebsiella pneumoniae: hypervirulence versus hypermucoviscosity

  • Hypervirulent lineages can cause community-onset liver abscess, bacteremia, endophthalmitis, and CNS metastatic infection, sometimes without typical host risk factors.
  • Hypermucoviscosity is a phenotype and is not equivalent to hypervirulence. A positive string test alone does not establish a hypervirulent strain; molecular virulence markers are more informative.
  • Liver abscess plus bacteremia or visual symptoms should trigger assessment for metastatic disease and urgent ophthalmologic evaluation for symptoms concerning for endophthalmitis.

Proteus, Morganella, Providencia, Serratia

  • Proteus mirabilis makes urease; recurrent infection can signal struvite stones or a retained urinary nidus. Nitrofurantoin is not reliable.
  • Morganella/Providencia/Serratia should not be collapsed into the moderate-risk AmpC category. Polymyxins are generally inactive, and susceptibility to tetracycline derivatives may be problematic.
  • Serratia can cause healthcare-associated infection, device infection and endocarditis; prolonged bacteremia requires source evaluation, not simply automatic carbapenem treatment.

Enteric organisms: brief bridge to a future enteric-infections module

  • Salmonella: typhoidal versus nontyphoidal disease; why bacteremia and vascular infection change the evaluation.
  • Shigella: low inoculum, transmission, and emerging extensively resistant strains; request isolate-specific AST when treating.
  • Campylobacter: J. jejuni enteritis versus C. fetus systemic disease; macrolide versus fluoroquinolone resistance patterns.

Fellow section: nuances and uncommon phenotypes

Five interpretive traps

  1. Ceftriaxone-susceptible Enterobacter cloacae bacteremia. Susceptible now does not exclude derepressed AmpC emergence. Cefepime or another appropriate non-third-generation-cephalosporin agent based on severity, susceptibility and source is generally preferable.
  2. Cefepime-susceptible ESBL E. coli bacteremia. Invasive ESBL disease is not adequately addressed by assuming reported cefepime susceptibility predicts clinical success; favor a carbapenem.
  3. Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella with NDM. Mechanism-directed therapy is required. In the 2026 guidance, aztreonam-avibactam or cefiderocol are preferred options for invasive NDM Enterobacterales infection; ceftazidime-avibactam alone is not active against NDM.
  4. DTR Pseudomonas susceptible to a novel agent. Determine infection site, dosing, MIC and source control. Do not assume all new beta-lactams are interchangeable or that meropenem-vaborbactam is an antipseudomonal salvage option.
  5. Stenotrophomonas from BAL in a patient whose imaging and oxygen requirement are improving without active therapy. Colonization may be more plausible than invasive infection. Treat the patient and syndrome, not the culture in isolation.

These are treatment pathways, not empiric guarantees. Verify local availability, FDA indication, renal dosing, infusion strategy and organism-specific susceptibility. MBL Pseudomonas is a separate problem; cefiderocol is favored in 2026 guidance.

Testing pitfalls

  • A susceptibility result is meaningful only if the agent-organism combination has a validated interpretive framework; automated panels can be unreliable for unusual nonfermenters.
  • Confirm unusual carbapenemase genotypes and discordant phenotypes with the laboratory. Phenotypic carbapenem resistance does not identify the underlying enzyme.
  • When the isolate remains clinically important but routine panel lacks modern agents, request supplemental AST through the local laboratory/reference lab; do not infer cefiderocol, aztreonam-avibactam or ceftolozane-tazobactam activity from a different drug.
  • Interpret susceptibility alongside required exposure, inoculum, drug distribution and source control. No drug is uniquely required for an entire species in every syndrome.

Fellow-level interpretation: stronger wording is not stronger evidence

  • Most important practical changes: protected aztreonam availability for NDM Enterobacterales; cefiderocol monotherapy moving to preferred for invasive S. maltophilia; firmer prioritization of sulbactam-durlobactam in invasive CRAB; refinement of DTR Pseudomonas pneumonia treatment.
  • Refinements rather than reversals: ESBL ceftriaxone proxy threshold, the Hafnia AmpC classification, allowance to continue ceftriaxone in selected improving nonsevere AmpC infections, and discouraging piperacillin-tazobactam for invasive moderate-risk AmpC.
  • Discuss uncertainty: the Stenotrophomonas preference rests on limited human comparative evidence. Mechanism-directed treatment is crucial, but many uncommon resistant species remain poorly studied.

Three rapid questions for fellows

  1. A stable patient with source-controlled E. cloacae intra-abdominal infection is improving on ceftriaxone. Does the 2026 exception allow continuation? Discuss the nonsevere/improving/source-controlled conditions and why it does not make ceftriaxone routinely preferred for invasive moderate-risk AmpC.
  2. A patient has NDM-producing K. pneumoniae bacteremia. Which agents are preferred, how is aztreonam protected, and why is ceftazidime-avibactam alone inadequate?
  3. An ICU BAL grows S. maltophilia. When would treatment be unnecessary? If invasive infection is established, why might cefiderocol monotherapy be favored now, and why should confidence in comparative clinical efficacy remain limited?

Interactive cases / discussion prompts

Case 1: initially ceftriaxone-susceptible Enterobacter bacteremia

A patient has E. cloacae complex bloodstream infection from an obstructed urinary system. The isolate tests susceptible to ceftriaxone and cefepime. Ask trainees whether the reported ceftriaxone result is adequate for definitive invasive-infection therapy. Discuss moderate-risk AmpC, cefepime exposure, drainage and the difference between cystitis and bloodstream infection.

Case 2: Serratia prosthetic-valve endocarditis

Two blood culture sets recover Serratia marcescens in a patient with a prosthetic valve. Ask why one should not automatically equate Serratia with E. cloacae-level AmpC risk, while still taking invasive endovascular infection seriously. Discuss repeat cultures, echocardiography and multidisciplinary surgical assessment.

Case 3: Klebsiella liver abscess and blurred vision

A patient with K. pneumoniae bloodstream infection has a liver abscess and acute blurred vision. Ask about urgent eye examination, abscess drainage and whether a positive string test establishes hypervirulence (it does not).

Case 4: BAL growing Stenotrophomonas

A mechanically ventilated patient has a BAL isolate of S. maltophilia but stable/improving oxygenation and an alternative explanation for infiltrates. Decide whether infection criteria are met before choosing drugs. If infection is compelling, review updated organism-specific options and susceptibility limitations.

Case 5: NDM Klebsiella bloodstream infection

A blood isolate is meropenem resistant, NDM positive. Ask why ceftazidime-avibactam monotherapy fails mechanistically and why aztreonam-avibactam, cefiderocol, or a protected-aztreonam combination may be considered depending on availability and AST.

Case 6: Pseudomonas and the wrong fluoroquinolone

A urine culture grows P. aeruginosa susceptible to ciprofloxacin. A trainee recommends oral moxifloxacin. Explain why same-class labeling is not sufficient, and why moxifloxacin is also a poor urinary agent.

Case 7: painful leg after freshwater injury

A patient develops rapidly progressive painful cellulitis with bullae after freshwater trauma; cultures grow Aeromonas. Discuss urgent surgical evaluation, exposure-based empiric therapy and inadequate coverage with cefazolin alone.

Suggested teaching close

Species identification is an early warning system, not a substitute for susceptibility testing. Most errors come from three mistakes: assuming a drug covers an entire taxonomic group, ignoring the location or source of infection, or treating a colonizing environmental organism as a pathogen. For unusual isolates, ask the microbiology laboratory how the species and susceptibility result were established.
