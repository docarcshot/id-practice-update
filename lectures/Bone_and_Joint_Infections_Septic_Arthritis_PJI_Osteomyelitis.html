Bone and joint infections

A practical 30–45 minute approach to septic arthritis, prosthetic joint infection, and osteomyelitis. Detailed regimens, evidence, and additional cases follow as optional reference material.

Core 35–45 min Advanced section optional Version 1.2 · September 20, 2026 Faculty, orthopedic surgery, wound care, and pharmacy review pending

The first question is not “how many weeks?” It is “what remains infected?”

Bone and joint infection is easiest to manage when the anatomy, microbiology, source-control plan, and treatment intent are named explicitly. Acute, subacute, and chronic describe tempo. They do not, by themselves, determine antibiotic duration.

Scope for the live session

Teach through the core sections and three cases, then stop. The advanced section is a post-lecture reference for fellows, attendings, and questions that arise on service.

Learning objectives

  1. Obtain useful cultures and identify urgent source control.
  2. Separate native-joint infection, PJI, osteomyelitis, and chronic wound colonization.
  3. Choose route and duration from the operative plan, retained material, and treatment intent.
  4. Manage an open sacral wound without reflexively prescribing six weeks of antibiotics.

Evidence and local implementation

Sources were checked through September 20, 2026. Several major US guidelines in this field are old, especially the 2013 IDSA PJI guideline and 2015 vertebral osteomyelitis guideline. The PJI pathway reflects the teaching service's clinical practice. Local treatment choices are distinguished from published guideline recommendations and trial findings. Local formulary, operative pathways, outpatient monitoring, and specialty practice still require case-level review.

Five questions organize every bone and joint infection

What is infected?Native joint, prosthesis, bone, spine, diabetic foot, pressure injury, or fracture hardware.
What is the best specimen?Blood, synovial fluid, bone, or multiple deep operative samples. A superficial wound swab rarely defines bone therapy.
What remains?Pus, necrotic bone, biofilm-coated implant, abscess, sinus tract, ischemic tissue, or an open wound.
Can it be controlled?Drain, debride, exchange, remove, reconstruct, revascularize, or manage noncuratively.
What is the intent?Cure, treatment with retained material, chronic suppression, or treatment of acute flares only.

Stable and unstable patients follow different clocks

If the patient is stable, obtain the highest-value specimen before antibiotics. If septic, hemodynamically unstable, or neurologically threatened, obtain blood cultures promptly but do not delay active therapy or urgent source control.

A hot native joint requires aspiration, drainage, and active antibiotics

Diagnose

Aspirate promptly if feasible. Send bacterial culture, cell count with differential, and crystals. Obtain blood cultures when febrile, systemically ill, or bacteremia is plausible. Crystals do not exclude infection, and no single synovial WBC cutoff proves or excludes it.

Treat now

After sampling, vancomycin plus ceftriaxone is a common empiric regimen. Substitute an antipseudomonal beta-lactam when resistant gram-negative risk is meaningful. Narrow once cultures return.

Control the source

Large-joint infection usually requires urgent orthopedic drainage. Failure to improve should prompt reassessment of drainage, adjacent osteomyelitis, and the diagnosis before automatic broadening or extension.

In PJI, the operation determines what antibiotics can accomplish

Establish the diagnosis

A sinus tract communicating with the prosthesis is diagnostic. If stable, aspirate before antibiotics when results will change management. At surgery, obtain multiple deep tissue cultures. Do not build months of therapy around a superficial wound or sinus swab.

Look for bacteremia

Obtain blood cultures with fever, systemic illness, acute hematogenous presentation, or suspected S. aureus. Search for the bloodstream source and complications when bacteremia is present.

Use “suppression” precisely

The fixed oral phase after DAIR is part of the planned curative-intent treatment course: 3 months for hip/shoulder and 6 months for knee in the local pathway. Indefinite suppressive antimicrobial therapy after that is a separate, noncurative decision and is not routine.

Do not define DAIR candidacy by postoperative timing alone

Symptom duration, implant stability, sinus tract, soft tissue, pathogen, host factors, and ability to exchange modular components all affect the chance of cure.

Source

Prefer blood cultures when hematogenous disease is plausible and bone or deep operative specimens when available. A superficial swab usually reflects colonizing flora.

Duration

With adequate source control and no retained implant, treatment generally does not exceed 6 weeks. Complete resection of infected bone may require no osteomyelitis course. Residual soft-tissue infection is treated separately.

Route

Osteomyelitis does not automatically require a PICC. An active, well-absorbed oral regimen is reasonable when the patient is stable, source control is adequate, absorption is reliable, and adherence and interactions have been addressed.

Common anchors: most bacterial vertebral osteomyelitis, 6 weeks; diabetic foot osteomyelitis without resection, 6 weeks; minor amputation with a positive bone margin, up to 3 weeks. These are syndrome-specific anchors, not a universal rule.

Diabetic foot osteomyelitis has a separate diagnostic and treatment pathway

Diagnose in sequence

Start with the clinical examination, probe-to-bone test, plain radiographs, and ESR or CRP when the examination is equivocal. Obtain MRI only when doubt persists. When culture will change treatment, obtain bone rather than superficial wound tissue.

Match empiric breadth to severity

For a mild infection in North America without recent antibiotics, target S. aureus and beta-hemolytic streptococci. Do not add empiric antipseudomonal therapy in a temperate climate unless prior cultures or a specific exposure makes it credible.

Exposed bone and MRI are not enough

Exposed bone may show remodeling without histologic osteomyelitis, and MRI can overcall infection in chronic pressure injury. Antibiotics without debridement and closure have not shown durable benefit.

Antibiotic route and duration follow source control, not the imaging label

Before treatment

Obtain the best specimen first when the patient is stable. Start empiric therapy promptly when unstable, then narrow to the organism and operative findings.

Before oral transition

Confirm clinical stability, adequate source control, an active oral drug with appropriate exposure, reliable absorption and adherence, and manageable interactions and toxicities.

Before extending

Reassess drainage, retained material, ischemia, wound closure, an alternative diagnosis, and drug exposure. Persistent imaging abnormalities alone do not prove failure.

Before suppressing

Name the unremovable infected source, organism, active oral option, monitoring plan, expected benefit, and stop criteria. Suppression is not simply an extra-long curative course.

Three cases are enough for the live discussion

Case 1 · Crystals in a hot native knee

A febrile patient has an acutely swollen knee. Synovial fluid contains urate crystals and 72,000 WBC/µL with 92% neutrophils.

What should happen next?

Crystals do not exclude infection. Send synovial culture and blood cultures, begin empiric therapy after aspiration if stable enough to sample first, and obtain urgent orthopedic input for drainage.

Case 2 · Acute postoperative knee PJI

Three weeks after TKA, the patient develops 3 days of pain, effusion, and drainage. Components are fixed and aspiration grows MSSA.

What determines the plan?

This may be a DAIR candidate if there is no sinus tract, soft tissue is suitable, and modular components can be exchanged. Obtain multiple deep cultures. In the local treatment approach, give at least 7–10 days IV, then active oral therapy to complete 6 months for a knee DAIR. Rifampin is only a companion drug after debridement and confirmation of susceptibility.

Case 3 · Exposed sacral bone without a flap option

A chronically immobile patient has an open stage IV sacral wound and MRI changes called osteomyelitis, but no cellulitis, abscess, fever, bacteremia, or instability. Durable closure is not feasible.

Would six weeks of antibiotics help?

No durable benefit has been shown. Do not treat the imaging label alone. Focus on pressure relief, wound and moisture care, nutrition, debridement when appropriate, symptom control, and goals of care. Treat future discrete cellulitis, abscess, bacteremia, or sepsis episodes when they occur.

Closing sentence

Name the infected structure, best microbiology, source control completed or impossible, retained material, treatment intent, and the event that determines duration.

Do not let the MRI choose the treatment

MRI is sensitive for osteomyelitis but can remain abnormal during healing and can be difficult to interpret near surgery, hardware, neuropathic bone, or chronic pressure injury. Treat a clinical syndrome with a source-control plan, not an imaging phrase in isolation.

Native septic arthritis is a drainage problem plus an antibiotic problem

Before antibiotics

Unless the patient is septic or unstable, aspirate the joint promptly before starting antibiotics. Send synovial bacterial culture, cell count with differential, and crystals; obtain blood cultures when febrile, systemically ill, or bacteremia is plausible. A synovial WBC above 50,000/µL increases suspicion but is not diagnostic, a lower count does not exclude infection, and crystals do not exclude concomitant bacterial arthritis.

Gram stain has limited sensitivity but useful specificity. If the joint is difficult to access, image-guided aspiration is reasonable. Sacroiliac, sternoclavicular, and other axial infections deserve early assessment for adjacent osteomyelitis, abscess, or endovascular source.

The two-week trial is easy to overgeneralize

A randomized trial found 2 weeks noninferior to 4 weeks after surgical drainage, but 99 of 154 cases involved the hand or wrist. It should not be used as blanket evidence for two weeks in a bacteremic native knee, shoulder, hip, axial joint, or infection with adjacent osteomyelitis.

Failure to improve should trigger repeat examination and reassessment of drainage, not automatic broadening or extension.

Sources sanjo, 2v4, gono

PJI diagnosis starts with timing, implant status, and the quality of the specimen

The 2021 EBJIS framework classifies PJI as infection unlikely, likely, or confirmed rather than relying on one cutoff. A sinus tract that communicates with the prosthesis confirms PJI. Acute pain in a previously well-functioning prosthesis, persistent drainage, unexplained chronic pain, or loosening should trigger evaluation. ESR and CRP are useful screening tests when the diagnosis is not already obvious, but normal markers do not exclude indolent PJI.

Preoperative sampling

Aspirate the joint when the diagnosis is uncertain or when microbiology will change treatment. Send synovial cell count/differential and aerobic/anaerobic cultures. Exact cutoffs vary by joint, time from implantation, and criteria set.

If clinically stable

Avoid starting antibiotics just because PJI is suspected. When medically safe, an antibiotic-free interval of about 2 weeks is recommended before aspiration or operative cultures to improve yield.

Blood cultures

Obtain them with fever, acute onset, systemic illness, S. aureus, or concern for hematogenous seeding.

At surgery

Obtain at least 5 deep tissue specimens using separate instruments for aerobic and anaerobic culture. Do not use a superficial sinus or wound swab to select months of therapy.

Adjunct diagnostics

Synovial alpha-defensin, leukocyte esterase, optimized culture techniques, histopathology, sonication, and molecular methods can help in selected culture-negative or equivocal cases. None converts a low-quality specimen into a definitive etiologic diagnosis.

Sources pji-2013, pji-review

No curative surgery Operative risk, patient preference, or anatomy makes curative source control unacceptable. Treat acute sepsis, abscess, or bacteremia first and obtain the best possible microbiology. Then consider culture-directed suppressive therapy only if there is a defined organism, active tolerable oral option, and meaningful functional goal. Suppression is noncurative and can fail from resistance, toxicity, loosening, sinus formation, or progression.

Local standard for DAIR

Use 3 months for hip/shoulder and 6 months for knee DAIR. The 2013 IDSA staphylococcal DAIR pathway uses the same joint-specific totals, and DATIPO showed that 6 weeks was not noninferior to 12 weeks across surgically managed PJI. Other contemporary groups use approximately 12 weeks for all DAIR, while the approach taught here uses the joint-specific totals above.

Do not overgeneralize SOLARIO

SOLARIO found that no more than 7 days of systemic antibiotics was noninferior to at least 4 weeks after definitive curative surgery plus a local antibiotic carrier. DAIR and suppressive procedures were excluded, and the PJI subgroup was small and not independently powered. The trial is practice-informing for carefully selected definitive surgery, but it does not shorten the local DAIR, one-stage, or two-stage pathways.

Rifampin rules

  • Use rifampin for a susceptible staphylococcal biofilm infection only as part of combination therapy. Never use rifampin monotherapy.
  • Do not add it empirically before knowing the organism. Reduce the bacterial burden surgically first.
  • Review drug interactions, hepatotoxicity, absorption, and companion-drug activity. Fluoroquinolones have the strongest companion evidence, but other agents are used when appropriate.
  • The exact best time to start rifampin is not established. A rigid postoperative day rule is not evidence based.

Sources pji-2013, datipo, solario, wiki, pji-review

Osteomyelitis without a retained implant usually does not need more than six weeks

First define the route and anatomy: hematogenous, contiguous/post-traumatic, diabetic foot, vertebral, pressure-injury pelvic osteomyelitis, or hardware-associated infection. Chronic osteomyelitis with a sequestrum behaves differently from acute hematogenous disease even if the same organism is isolated.

Diabetic foot osteomyelitis deserves its own pathway

The current 2023 IWGDF/IDSA guideline recommends probe-to-bone, plain radiographs, and ESR/CRP/PCT as initial studies, followed by MRI when doubt persists. Obtain bone rather than soft-tissue culture when feasible. For mild infection in North America, initially target aerobic gram-positive pathogens; do not empirically cover Pseudomonas in a temperate climate unless recent cultures or a specific exposure supports it.

For osteomyelitis treated without bone resection or amputation, use 6 weeks. After minor amputation with a positive bone-margin culture, use up to 3 weeks. Antibiotic-only treatment is most defensible for selected forefoot osteomyelitis without an urgent drainage need, peripheral arterial disease, or exposed bone. Moderate or severe infection with necrosis, gangrene, deep abscess, or ischemia requires early surgical assessment; combine debridement with urgent vascular assessment/revascularization when ischemic. Pressure offloading, wound care, and glycemic management remain essential.

Oral is a pharmacologic decision, not a concession

For a stable patient with adequate source control, reliable absorption, and an active high-bioavailability regimen with clinical evidence, a PICC is not required solely because the diagnosis is osteomyelitis. OVIVA directly supports oral therapy for appropriately selected complex bone and joint infections.

Sources wiki, oviva, dfi, brodie

Stable vertebral osteomyelitis is one of the situations where delaying empiric therapy can improve the diagnosis

Obtain blood cultures and MRI. S. aureus bacteremia with a compatible MRI often establishes the diagnosis without disc-space biopsy. If the patient is hemodynamically stable with a stable neurologic examination and blood cultures are nondiagnostic, the IDSA guideline supports withholding empiric therapy while obtaining a microbiologic diagnosis. Sepsis, shock, or severe/progressive neurologic symptoms require prompt treatment while the diagnostic workup proceeds.

Medical treatment

For most bacterial native vertebral osteomyelitis, use 6 weeks of parenteral or highly bioavailable oral therapy. Routine extension beyond 6 weeks because MRI still looks abnormal is not supported.

Call spine surgery early for

Progressive neurologic deficit, progressive deformity, spinal instability, persistent/recurrent bacteremia without another source, or worsening pain despite appropriate therapy.

Follow the patient clinically. ESR/CRP can support trend assessment, but persistent pain, an elevated marker, or residual MRI abnormalities alone do not establish treatment failure.

Source nvo

Chronic sacral or pelvic osteomyelitis with an open pressure wound is not a six-week-antibiotic diagnosis

Exposed bone does not prove osteomyelitis. Chronically exposed bone can show reactive remodeling, and MRI may not reliably distinguish that from infection. If the wound cannot be debrided and durably closed, evidence has not shown that prolonged antibiotics sterilize the underlying bone or improve durable outcomes. The treatment goal becomes wound stability, symptom control, prevention of new pressure injury, and treatment of discrete acute infections.

Sepsis or bacteremia plausibly arising from an infected pressure injury Start prompt broad empiric therapy after blood/deep cultures when feasible. A common severe-infection approach is vancomycin plus cefepime plus metronidazole, or vancomycin plus piperacillin-tazobactam, modified for prior resistant isolates and local antibiogram. Narrow quickly. Look for abscess, necrotizing infection, fecal/urinary contamination, or another source. Treat the systemic syndrome and source. Do not automatically convert clinical improvement into six weeks of therapy.
Debridement and flap reconstruction planned When stable, hold unnecessary empiric antibiotics until operative deep bone/tissue cultures if feasible. After adequate debridement and flap coverage, 6 weeks remains a conventional standard. Selected cortical-only disease may need less, but evidence for 2–4 weeks is not yet strong enough to use as a universal default. Multidisciplinary surgery, durable coverage, pressure offloading, nutritional optimization, continence management, and a realistic postoperative pressure-relief plan.

Do not substitute suppressive antibiotics for wound closure

Long-term suppression is sometimes rational for a retained infected orthopedic implant or extensive necrotic bone that cannot be removed. That logic should not be automatically transferred to an open stage IV sacral wound. Ongoing contamination and absent coverage make durable microbial suppression unlikely, and the best available reviews recommend no systemic antibiotics in the absence of an acute soft-tissue/systemic infection or a reconstructive surgical plan.

Non-ID treatment is the treatment

Pressure and wound mechanics

Regular pressure redistribution/repositioning, appropriate support surface, minimizing shear, absorbent dressings, moisture balance, and reducing dressing trauma/frequency when palliation is the goal.

Debridement and reconstruction

Wound surgery/plastics evaluation for debridement, bone resection when needed, and flap coverage. Assess whether postoperative offloading and wound protection are realistically sustainable before major reconstruction.

Host optimization

Nutrition and protein/calorie adequacy, perfusion, smoking cessation when relevant, glycemic management, management of spasticity/contractures, urinary/fecal incontinence, pain, odor, mobility, and caregiver resources.

Biofilm-active is not a synonym for “add rifampin”

For susceptible staphylococcal retained-prosthesis infection, rifampin combination therapy has a specific evidence base. For gram-negative retained-prosthesis infection, fluoroquinolone activity is valuable when susceptible. For other organisms and drugs, the clinical evidence for “biofilm activity” is much less mature than in-vitro terminology implies.

Oral transition checklist

  • Hemodynamics and local infection are stable.
  • No undrained abscess or source-control procedure remains immediately necessary.
  • No persistent bacteremia.
  • The oral drug is active and has adequate systemic/site exposure at the intended dose.
  • GI absorption and adherence are reliable, and drug interactions are addressed.
  • Monitoring is practical. TMP-SMX requires K+, creatinine, and CBC; linezolid requires CBC and longer-course toxicity review; rifampin has major interaction potential.

Suppression is a treatment intent, not a longer version of curative therapy

Suppressive antimicrobial therapy is reasonable to consider when infected hardware or necrotic bone cannot be removed, curative surgery is too risky or declined, the organism is known, and a tolerable active oral agent exists. The goal is to reduce relapse, symptoms, or additional surgery. It does not sterilize the retained source.

When suppression makes sense
Question Favors suppression Argues against suppression
Is there a defined target? Repeated high-quality deep cultures identify a pathogen with a reliable oral option. Culture-negative disease or only superficial wound cultures.
Is the retained source finite? Stable prosthesis or fixation hardware that cannot currently be removed; extensive necrotic bone not amenable to resection. Continuously open contaminated pressure wound without a closure plan.
Would recurrence matter? Relapse threatens function, hardware survival, hospitalization, or major surgery. The drug is unlikely to change symptoms or goals.
Can the regimen be sustained? Narrow active oral drug, tolerable toxicity profile, feasible labs and follow-up. Resistance likely, no oral option, major interactions, recurrent C. difficile, progressive toxicity, or poor adherence.

Common organism-directed suppressive choices include cephalexin or dicloxacillin for susceptible MSSA, doxycycline/minocycline or TMP-SMX for selected susceptible staphylococci, and a susceptible fluoroquinolone or TMP-SMX for some gram-negative infections. These are examples, not universal defaults. Rifampin monotherapy should not be used for chronic suppression.

The 2026 PJI systematic review found highly heterogeneous practice and definitions. Reported pooled success was about 74% overall, but observational confounding is substantial and “success” varied across studies. In US practice suppression is often indefinite; finite strategies also exist. There is no evidence-based universal stopping point.

Reassess the decision periodically

At follow-up ask whether the original goal is still being achieved, whether loosening or sinus formation is progressing, whether toxicity or resistance has emerged, and whether surgery has become feasible or desirable.

The highest-value studies change route and duration, but not the need for source control

OVIVA: oral treatment can replace prolonged IV therapy

In 1,054 adults with complex orthopedic infection, definitive treatment failure occurred in 14.6% assigned to IV therapy and 13.2% assigned to oral therapy during the first 6 weeks. Oral therapy met noninferiority criteria. Catheter complications were more common with IV treatment, 9.4% versus 1.0%. This supports carefully selected oral regimens. It does not mean every oral drug is adequate or that source control can be omitted.

DATIPO: six weeks was not enough for all surgically managed PJI

The randomized PJI trial failed to show noninferiority of 6 weeks compared with 12 weeks. The signal was most concerning with retained-implant strategies such as DAIR. It supports avoiding a 6-week DAIR course, but it does not establish one universal duration for every joint or exchange strategy. The local treatment approach uses 3 months for hip/shoulder DAIR and 6 months for knee DAIR.

SOLARIO: very short systemic therapy after definitive surgery plus local antibiotics

In 475 eligible adults who underwent definitive curative surgery, received a local antibiotic carrier eluting for at least 4 weeks, and achieved primary closure, no more than 7 days of systemic therapy was noninferior to at least 4 weeks for definite failure at 12 months (11.1% versus 14.1%). Antibiotic-related symptoms were less frequent with the short course. DAIR and suppressive procedures were excluded, the cohort combined several orthopedic syndromes, and the PJI subgroups were not separately powered. Do not apply this result to retained prostheses or surgery without the same local-antibiotic and closure conditions.

Two versus four weeks after native-joint drainage: useful but anatomically narrow

The randomized trial enrolled 154 cases, 99 involving the hand or wrist. Two weeks after surgical drainage performed well in that population. It is weak justification for shortening treatment of large-joint, bacteremic, axial, or osteomyelitis-complicated infection.

Pressure-injury pelvic osteomyelitis: the evidence is mostly observational

Systematic and multidisciplinary reviews find no evidence that prolonged antibiotics improve outcomes when an open stage IV pressure injury cannot be debrided and closed. Outcomes are poor without flap reconstruction regardless of antibiotics in available cohorts. This is a source-control conclusion, not proof that pelvic osteomyelitis is harmless.

Where the evidence is weakest

Outside specific institutional pathways, optimal PJI duration after one-stage or two-stage exchange, exact rifampin timing, the value of an antibiotic holiday before reimplantation, treatment duration for Brodie abscess, retained fracture fixation, and the benefit/duration of chronic suppression remain incompletely defined.

Sources oviva, datipo, solario, 2v4, pressure-2019, pressure-2024

Cases: decide the source-control plan before the antibiotic clock

Case 1 · Crystals and a hot knee

A 67-year-old has fever and an acutely swollen native knee. Synovial fluid contains urate crystals and 72,000 WBC/µL with 92% neutrophils.

Discuss the decision

Crystals do not exclude septic arthritis. Send Gram stain/culture and blood cultures, start empiric therapy after aspiration if stable enough to sample first, and obtain urgent orthopedic input for drainage. Narrow to the organism.

Case 2 · Arthritis plus pustules

A young adult has migratory arthralgia, tenosynovitis, pustular lesions, and a swollen ankle.

Discuss the decision

Evaluate for disseminated gonococcal infection. Obtain NAAT from exposed mucosal sites, culture of purulent material when possible, and blood cultures when appropriate. Treat with ceftriaxone 1 g every 24 hours; treat chlamydia if not excluded. Total therapy is at least 7 days, with susceptibility-guided oral transition after substantial improvement.

Case 3 · Acute postoperative knee PJI

Three weeks after TKA, the patient develops 3 days of pain, effusion, and drainage. Components are well fixed. Aspiration grows MSSA.

Discuss the decision

This is a favorable timing pattern for DAIR if the soft tissue and implant are otherwise suitable. Exchange modular components when feasible, obtain multiple deep cultures, and use pathogen-directed therapy. In the local treatment approach, give at least 7–10 days IV, then active oral therapy to complete 6 months for a knee DAIR. Add rifampin only as combination therapy once MSSA is confirmed and the surgical bacterial burden has been reduced.

Case 4 · Chronic hip PJI considered for one-stage exchange

A patient has months of hip pain, a well-fixed prosthesis, good soft tissue, no sinus tract, and repeated deep aspiration cultures growing susceptible Cutibacterium acnes.

Discuss the decision

This is a potential one-stage exchange candidate because the organism is known and tractable and the soft-tissue envelope is favorable. Remove all infected components, perform radical debridement, obtain at least 5 deep specimens with separate instruments, and implant the new prosthesis during the same operation. The local pathway uses at least 7–10 days IV, followed by active oral therapy to complete 12 weeks. Routine indefinite suppression is not required after successful exchange.

Case 5 · Stable vertebral osteomyelitis with negative blood cultures

A stable patient has severe back pain and MRI findings compatible with L2–L3 discitis/osteomyelitis. Neurologic examination is normal and blood cultures are negative.

Discuss the decision

If clinically safe, do not rush into empiric six-week therapy before obtaining a microbiologic diagnosis. Arrange image-guided biopsy and assess alternative diagnoses. If bacterial NVO is confirmed, most patients need 6 weeks of parenteral or highly bioavailable oral therapy. Repeat MRI is not routine if the patient improves.

Case 6 · Months of tibial pain and a Brodie-type lesion

A 24-year-old has focal tibial pain for 3 months, no fever, normal WBC, and a well-circumscribed intramedullary lesion.

Discuss the decision

Subacute osteomyelitis is possible, but malignancy and other mimics matter. Orthopedic curettage/biopsy provides diagnosis, cultures, histology, and source control. Culture-directed therapy is often 4–6 weeks, but there is no high-quality trial defining the ideal course.

Case 7 · Stage IV sacral wound, exposed bone, MRI “osteomyelitis,” no flap option

A chronically immobile patient has an open sacral pressure injury with visible bone. There is no surrounding cellulitis, abscess, fever, bacteremia, or hemodynamic instability. Plastics determines durable flap closure is not feasible.

Discuss the decision

Do not prescribe six weeks, chronic suppression, or repeated antibiotic courses for the exposed bone/MRI alone. Focus on pressure redistribution, wound care, debridement when appropriate, moisture/incontinence management, nutrition, pain/odor control, and realistic goals of care. Treat future discrete cellulitis, abscess, bacteremia, or sepsis episodes when they occur.

Case 8 · The same wound, now septic with a gluteal abscess

The patient later develops fever, hypotension, spreading erythema, and CT evidence of a drainable gluteal collection.

Discuss the decision

Now antibiotics are indicated. Obtain blood cultures, drain the abscess, send a meaningful deep specimen, and start broad empiric therapy based on severity and prior resistance. Narrow when cultures return. The chronic open sacral bone does not automatically reset the plan to six weeks of osteomyelitis therapy.

Case 9 · Diabetic forefoot osteomyelitis after toe amputation

A patient undergoes minor amputation for infected toe osteomyelitis. The remaining bone-margin culture is positive.

Discuss the decision

IWGDF/IDSA suggests up to 3 weeks after minor amputation with a positive bone-margin culture. If infected bone had been completely resected with no residual soft-tissue infection, a prolonged osteomyelitis course would not be justified. Continue offloading, wound care, glycemic management, and vascular assessment as indicated.

Check the reasoning

1. Do urate crystals exclude septic arthritis?

Show explanation

No. Crystal disease and bacterial arthritis can coexist.

2. What is the highest-value action in a purulent native knee?

Show explanation

Prompt diagnostic sampling and effective drainage, with timely active antibiotics. Antibiotics do not substitute for drainage.

3. Does every acute PJI qualify for DAIR?

Show explanation

No. Implant stability, symptom duration, sinus tract, soft tissue, pathogen, host factors, and ability to exchange modular parts all affect the likelihood of cure.

4. Is rifampin a general add-on for any staphylococcal bone infection?

Show explanation

No. Its strongest clinical role is susceptible staphylococcal infection with retained prosthetic material in combination with another active agent. Never use it alone.

5. Does osteomyelitis require six weeks of IV therapy?

Show explanation

No. Six weeks is a common maximum for adequately source-controlled osteomyelitis without retained implant, and validated oral regimens are reasonable in selected patients. Complete resection may need no osteomyelitis therapy at all.

6. Does exposed sacral bone prove osteomyelitis?

Show explanation

No. Chronic exposed bone can show remodeling without histologic osteomyelitis, and MRI can overcall infection in this setting.

7. What should an asymptomatic open sacral wound with suspected pelvic osteomyelitis receive if there is no debridement/flap plan?

Show explanation

No systemic antibiotics for the osteomyelitis label alone. Treat acute soft-tissue or systemic infections when present and focus on wound/pressure management.

8. When is chronic suppression most defensible?

Show explanation

When a defined infection source such as retained prosthetic hardware cannot be removed, a known pathogen has a tolerable active oral option, and preventing relapse or further surgery is a meaningful patient goal.

9. Should worsening vertebral MRI at week 6 automatically extend antibiotics?

Show explanation

No. If pain, examination, and inflammatory markers are improving, imaging can lag behind clinical cure.

10. What is the central documentation sentence?

Show explanation

State the infected structure, source control completed or impossible, microbiologic target, treatment intent, and what event determines duration.

Bone and joint infections · One-page rounds sheet

September 20, 2026 · Teaching companion. Local orthopedic, wound-care, pharmacy, and institutional review pending.

Native septic arthritis

Aspirate before antibiotics if stable. Send culture, WBC/diff, crystals. Crystals do not exclude infection. Drain the joint. Empiric vancomycin + ceftriaxone is a common US starting point; use antipseudomonal coverage when risk warrants.

PJI diagnosis

Sinus tract to prosthesis = PJI. If stable, avoid premature antibiotics. Aspirate when useful. At surgery obtain multiple deep samples, ideally 5–6. Do not use superficial sinus/wound swab to choose long-term therapy.

PJI surgery

DAIR: acute, brief symptoms, fixed implant, no sinus, good tissue, exchange modular parts. One-stage: selected chronic PJI with known/tractable organism and good tissue. Two-stage: chronic/difficult infection, compromised tissue, or failed retention.

PJI antibiotics

Local treatment approach: DAIR IV 7–10 days then oral, total 3 months hip/shoulder or 6 months knee. One-stage: IV 7–10 days then oral to 12 weeks. Two-stage after explant: IV 7–10 days then oral to 6 weeks; local pathway uses ≥2 weeks off before reimplantation assessment. ≥2 positive reimplantation cultures: treat 12 weeks.

Osteomyelitis, no implant

Culture meaningful tissue, drain abscess, remove necrotic bone. With adequate source control, generally ≤6 weeks. Complete resection can require no osteomyelitis therapy or only 2–5 days if no residual soft-tissue infection.

Vertebral osteomyelitis

Stable neuro/hemodynamics: obtain microbiologic diagnosis before empiric therapy when possible. Most bacterial NVO = 6 weeks IV or highly bioavailable oral.

Diabetic foot

DFO without resection = 6 weeks. Minor amputation + positive bone margin = up to 3 weeks. Pair antibiotics with debridement, offloading, wound care, and vascular assessment when ischemic.

Stage IV sacral/pelvic wound

Exposed bone or MRI “OM” without acute infection and without debridement/flap plan = no systemic antibiotics. Treat discrete cellulitis, abscess, bacteremia, or sepsis. If debridement + flap occurs, ~6 weeks remains conventional.

Oral therapy

OVIVA supports oral treatment when stable, source controlled, absorbing reliably, and an active evidence-supported oral drug is available. Osteomyelitis does not automatically require a PICC.

Suppression

Noncurative. Best fit: defined pathogen + retained hardware/necrotic source that cannot be removed + tolerable active oral drug + meaningful goal. Do not routinely suppress an open sacral pressure wound.
