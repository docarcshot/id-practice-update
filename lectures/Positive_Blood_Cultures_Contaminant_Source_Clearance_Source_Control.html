Positive Blood Cultures

The lab calls with a positive bottle. What needs to happen now, what can wait, and what information actually changes the plan?

What you should be able to do

A positive blood culture is a piece of data, not yet a diagnosis. When the lab calls, I first want to know how sick the patient is, what grew, how the cultures were collected, and whether there is hardware or a source that needs attention. The antibiotic choice matters, but it is rarely the only decision.

  1. Decide whether a positive culture is likely to represent true bloodstream infection, contamination, or an unresolved result.
  2. Separate the likely portal of entry from possible endovascular or metastatic infection.
  3. Choose when follow-up blood cultures are useful and when they are probably unnecessary.
  4. Identify needed source control and determine which date should anchor treatment duration.
  5. Give the primary team a clear recommendation, including what could change it.
A limitation up front. This is not an antimicrobial order set. Drug and dose still depend on the syndrome, susceptibility results, renal and hepatic function, allergy history, weight, local antibiogram, formulary, pharmacy guidance, and current labeling.

How I approach the initial call

The order varies a little depending on the patient. An unstable patient gets active therapy while the rest of the evaluation is happening. In a stable patient, there is usually time to look at the collection details before adding another drug.

How sick?Is current therapy active, and can anything wait?
Is it real?What grew, from how many sets, and from which sites?
Where from?What is the likely source, and could another site have been seeded?
Repeat?Will another culture establish clearance or change the workup?
What needs fixing?Is there a line, device, obstruction, or collection that antibiotics will not solve?

For residents

  • Look at the patient before treating the microbiology report.
  • Count independently collected sets and review where they were drawn. Bottle count alone is not enough.
  • A plausible source may explain entry into the blood, but it does not always explain the full extent of infection.
  • Repeat cultures for a reason, not simply because the first ones were positive.
  • Your plan should address source control and duration, not only antibiotic selection.

Questions for fellows

  • Which uncertainty matters enough to change what you do today?
  • Are you applying an organism-specific approach or reflexively using the same workup for every bacteremia?
  • Is a prediction score helping with risk assessment, or are you asking it to exclude disease that it cannot reliably exclude?
  • What does the local rapid panel detect, and what can it miss?
  • If cultures remain positive, is the presumed source wrong, incompletely controlled, or not the whole story?

The organism changes the conversation

Some results should be treated as clinically significant while the workup continues. Others need context before committing the patient to treatment. This is where the species, number of independently drawn sets, and presence of prosthetic material matter much more than a simple count of positive bottles.

The shortcut that causes trouble. “One bottle is a contaminant” is not a reliable rule. One bottle can represent true infection. The probability depends on the organism, independently collected sets, blood volume, collection site, time to positivity, host, devices, and clinical course. The 2024 ASM laboratory report uses a common commensal isolated from a single set within a culture series in its contamination definition, but the bedside decision remains clinical.3
How much weight should we give time to positivity?

Rapid growth may support a higher organism burden, but there is no universal time cutoff that separates pathogen from contaminant across organisms, platforms, blood volumes, and prior antibiotics. I would not stop or continue treatment on time to positivity alone.

When catheter-related bloodstream infection is suspected and paired peripheral and catheter cultures are drawn simultaneously with comparable volumes, catheter growth at least 2 hours earlier than the peripheral culture supports a catheter source. It does not substitute for the full clinical assessment.5 Apply the local laboratory’s collection and reporting method.

What can we safely do with a rapid panel result?

The rapid panel can move care forward, but only if you know what the local assay detects, what it misses, and how it handles polymicrobial cultures. A resistance gene is not the same thing as a complete susceptibility profile. Conventional identification and phenotypic susceptibility testing still matter. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, rapid diagnostics were associated with lower mortality when paired with active stewardship support, but not when used alone.8

Cases for discussion

For each case, stop before opening the answer. Decide what you think the result means, what you need to do today, and what additional finding would change your mind.

Case 1: One set, one bottle, coagulase-negative staphylococcus

A stable patient admitted with a heart-failure exacerbation had one blood-culture set collected for a temperature of 37.9°C. One bottle grows coagulase-negative staphylococcus at 28 hours. There is no central line, prosthetic valve, cardiac device, vascular graft, or orthopedic hardware. Nothing else suggests an infection.

Before moving on

  • Would you call this bacteremia?
  • Would you continue vancomycin?
  • Do you need another culture?
How I would handle it

This is most likely contamination. I would reassess the patient and make sure there is not a device or clinical finding missing from the initial story. If the probability remains low, I would stop or avoid vancomycin and document why the isolate is not being treated.

I would not repeat cultures automatically. I would repeat properly collected peripheral sets if the history is incomplete, a prosthetic device comes to light, or the clinical course makes true infection plausible. When safe, obtain those cultures before giving another antibiotic dose.

The error here is allowing the word “positive” to settle the diagnosis. Contaminated cultures lead to unnecessary antibiotics, additional testing, adverse events, and longer hospitalization.34

Case 2: One bottle with S. aureus and new back pain

A patient with no obvious source has one bottle positive for S. aureus. They are afebrile after antibiotics but mention new focal lumbar pain when you examine them.

What matters now?

  • Does one positive bottle change how seriously you take the result?
  • What studies need to be ordered today?
  • Does becoming afebrile lower the concern?
How I would handle it

I would treat this as S. aureus bacteremia. One positive bottle does not make S. aureus a contaminant. The immediate work includes active therapy, at least two follow-up culture sets at 48 hours after the index sample, TTE, review of all intravascular and prosthetic material, and a careful search for focal symptoms.

The back pain changes the case. It needs directed evaluation for vertebral or epidural infection. Defervescence after antibiotics does not answer that question. If cultures remain positive at or beyond 48 hours, the patient moves into an increased-risk pathway and the source and extent of infection need to be reconsidered.1

For fellows. A negative TTE does not end the echocardiographic evaluation in every patient. The 2026 consensus supports TEE when risk remains higher because of an intracardiac device, predisposing valve disease, cultures positive at least 48 hours, embolic events, or more than one noncontiguous focus. Community onset and injection drug use also increase concern.1

Case 3: E. coli bacteremia with an obstructed ureter

A patient with pyelonephritis and E. coli bacteremia undergoes ureteral decompression. They are hemodynamically stable and improving on active therapy. There is no prosthetic valve, endovascular graft, severe immunocompromise, or evidence of a deep focus.

Decisions for rounds

  • Do you need daily cultures until negative?
  • What made the biggest difference, the antibiotic or decompression?
  • When could oral treatment be reasonable?
How I would handle it

The major intervention was decompression. A longer antibiotic course would not have corrected the obstruction. If the patient continues to improve and source control is adequate, I would not routinely repeat blood cultures.

Seven days of effective therapy is supported for eligible patients. Oral transition is reasonable when the source is established, the patient is improving, the isolate is susceptible to an oral agent with adequate systemic exposure, and the gastrointestinal tract is functioning.67

For fellows. Do not stretch the BALANCE result beyond its population. The trial excluded severe immunosuppression, prosthetic heart valves or endovascular grafts, deep foci requiring prolonged therapy, S. aureus, S. lugdunensis, possible contaminants, unusual pathogens requiring prolonged therapy, and fungemia.7

Case 4: Yeast in blood after abdominal surgery

A patient with recent bowel surgery, an anastomotic leak, and a central venous catheter has yeast reported from one blood-culture bottle.

What would you do tonight?

  • Could this be dismissed as contamination?
  • What has to happen besides starting an antifungal?
  • When does the treatment clock begin?
How I would handle it

I would manage this as candidemia while identification is pending. Start active antifungal therapy using current guidance and local pharmacy input. Obtain blood cultures every day or every other day until clearance. The abdomen needs another look for drainage or repair, and the catheter should be addressed early when it is the presumed source and can be removed safely.

For candidemia without metastatic complications, the IDSA guideline recommends treatment for 2 weeks after documented clearance and resolution of attributable symptoms. The clock does not start with the first positive bottle.2

When should the cultures be repeated?

This is organism- and situation-specific. S. aureus bacteremia and candidemia require documented clearance. An improving patient with a controlled gram-negative source usually does not need daily cultures. The useful question is not “Were the cultures positive?” It is “What will another set tell me or change?”

Put the reason in the plan. “Repeat cultures to document S. aureus clearance” is useful. So is “repeat because the catheter remains in place.” For an improving patient with uncomplicated gram-negative bacteremia, it may be appropriate to document why cultures are not being repeated.

What are the antibiotics not going to fix?

On rounds, this is often the most important part of the discussion. If the source is obstructed, walled off, necrotic, leaking, or attached to foreign material, changing antibiotics may accomplish very little.

Remove

Is a catheter, cardiac device, vascular graft, or other hardware involved? If removal may be needed, identify who will make that decision and when.

Drain

Is there an abscess, empyema, infected collection, septic joint, or another closed-space infection? If someone says it is not drainable, clarify whether that conclusion came from the procedural service.

Debride

Is there necrotic tissue, an infected wound, devitalized bone, or an uncontrolled surgical focus? Antibiotics cannot restore perfusion or remove dead tissue.

Decompress or repair

Is there urinary or biliary obstruction, a leak, fistula, perforation, or another continuing anatomic problem?

If cultures stay positive, stop and reconsider the case. For S. aureus, a positive follow-up culture at least 48 hours after the first positive culture is a major increased-risk feature. Check whether therapy is active and reaching the patient, but also reconsider the source, every line and device, and occult endovascular or metastatic infection. Broader therapy alone is usually not the answer.1
When the team thinks the line is the source
  • Were peripheral and catheter cultures collected before antibiotics, at the same time, with comparable volumes?
  • Is the line still necessary, and can it be removed now?
  • Is there tunnel, pocket, exit-site, or septic-thrombus disease?
  • Is the organism one for which catheter retention carries a high failure risk?
  • If salvage is attempted, what is the failure threshold and when will cultures be repeated?

The 2009 IDSA catheter guideline remains an older source and should be reconciled with local vascular-access and infection-prevention policy.5

Answer the question the team actually has

A technically correct consult is not very useful if the primary team cannot tell what to do next. The assessment should state whether the culture is significant, where the infection probably came from, what remains unproven, and what needs to happen today.

A practical note structure

Positive blood cultures Blood cultures: [dates, number of sets, collection sites, identification, susceptibilities] Assessment: This is [true BSI / likely contamination / still uncertain]. The likely source is [ ] because [ ]. We remain concerned about [endovascular, prosthetic, or metastatic site] because [ ]. Plan today - Active therapy: [ ] - Repeat cultures: [what, when, and why] - Source control: [action, responsible service, expected timing] - Additional evaluation: [only tests likely to change management] Duration and follow-through - Duration anchor and anticipated stop date: [ ] - Pending results and who will review them: [ ] - Criteria for narrowing, oral transition, discharge, or reopening the workup: [ ]

What I would say to the primary team

“I think this is true S. aureus bacteremia even though only one bottle is positive. We need two follow-up sets tomorrow at the 48-hour mark and a TTE. His focal back pain also needs directed spine imaging. If the cultures remain positive, we need to assume that the source is not controlled or that there is another endovascular or metastatic focus.”

Before the patient leaves the hospital

  • “Continue antibiotics per ID” is not a discharge plan. State the drug, dose, route, indication, duration anchor, stop date, and monitoring.
  • List all pending blood cultures and susceptibilities, who will review them, and what result changes the plan.
  • Record source-control completion and any retained material.
  • Specify necessary imaging, specialty follow-up, vascular-access plan, laboratory monitoring, and symptoms requiring urgent reassessment.
  • Use oral therapy when evidence, susceptibility, absorption, adherence, and syndrome permit. Bacteremia alone is not an indication for a PICC.

Where these cases usually go off track

Calling it from the bottle count

“One bottle means contamination” and “two bottles mean true infection” are unreliable shortcuts. Start with the organism, independently collected sets, collection sites, host, devices, and syndrome.

Stopping once a plausible source is found

A positive urine culture may explain how bacteremia started. It does not make new back pain, an intracardiac device, persistent cultures, or embolic findings irrelevant.

Repeating cultures by habit

Follow-up cultures are essential in some infections and low yield in others. Know what question the next set is supposed to answer.

Changing antibiotics instead of controlling the source

A broader regimen does not drain an abscess, remove an infected line, decompress an obstruction, or debride necrotic tissue.

Overreading the rapid panel

An organism or resistance gene detected on a panel is not a complete phenotype. Know the local assay and wait for conventional results when they matter.

Choosing duration from memory

There is no single bacteremia duration. The answer depends on the organism, source, extent, source control, host, response, and the population actually studied.

Knowledge check

1. One of four bottles grows S. aureus. The patient looks well. Contaminant?

Answer

No. Treat as clinically significant and begin the S. aureus bacteremia evaluation. Bottle count does not make S. aureus a contaminant.

2. When should adult S. aureus follow-up cultures be obtained?

Answer

The 2026 IDSA/ESCMID consensus suggests at least two sets at 48 hours after sampling of the first positive culture, then one or two sets every 24 to 48 hours until negative. Intensify for ongoing illness, deep or endovascular infection, or persistence.

3. Does every adult with S. aureus bacteremia need TTE?

Answer

The 2026 consensus suggests routine TTE for all adults with S. aureus bacteremia. TEE is risk-based after TTE, not automatically omitted because TTE is negative.

4. An improving patient has E. coli bacteremia from pyelonephritis after ureteral decompression. Repeat daily cultures?

Answer

Usually no, if this meets uncomplicated gram-negative BSI criteria. Repeat if response is inadequate, an endovascular focus is possible, or source control is limited.

5. One set grows Corynebacterium in a patient with a prosthetic valve. Ignore it?

Answer

No. It may still be contamination, but prosthetic material raises the consequence and probability of true infection. Reassess the syndrome and obtain properly collected repeat sets before dismissing it.

6. What does a catheter culture turning positive 2.5 hours before a simultaneous peripheral culture suggest?

Answer

If collected simultaneously with comparable volumes, a differential time to positivity of at least 2 hours supports a catheter source. It does not prove it and must be interpreted with the organism and clinical context.

7. Can the BALANCE trial justify 7 days for candidemia or S. aureus bacteremia?

Answer

No. The trial excluded fungemia, S. aureus, S. lugdunensis, deep foci requiring prolonged therapy, and other important high-risk groups.

8. What is the most useful one-sentence bacteremia recommendation?

Answer

One that states significance, source, next action, and contingency. Example: “This is true bacteremia from an obstructed urinary source; decompression is complete, repeat cultures are unnecessary if improvement continues, and we will narrow to an active oral option when susceptibilities return.”

Positive blood cultures: one-page rounds sheet

1. Validate

Organism. Independent sets and sites. Blood volume and collection quality. Host and devices. Compatible syndrome. Trajectory.

2. Stabilize

Is current therapy active and timely? Do not wait for final susceptibility in an unstable patient. Use local empiric guidance.

3. Localize

Portal: urine, biliary/abdomen, lung, skin/wound, catheter. Extent: valve/device, thrombus, spine, joint, bone, abscess, embolic sites.

4. Clear selectively

S. aureus: 2 sets at 48 h, then q24–48 h until negative. Candida: daily or every other day. Uncomplicated GN BSI: usually no repeat.

5. Control

Remove line/device when indicated. Drain. Debride. Decompress. Repair leak or fistula. Name owner and timing.

6. Set the clock

Document the duration anchor and stop date. S. aureus and candidemia use documented clearance; later source control may reset the anchor in selected cases.

7. Communicate

Significance + source + action + contingency. State what result or clinical change reopens the workup.

8. Handoff safely

Drug/dose/route, stop date, pending results and owner, source control, retained material, monitoring, follow-up, return triggers.

Do not do these: call S. aureus or yeast a contaminant; use bottle count alone; repeat every culture daily; assume antibiotics replace source control; generalize 7-day data to excluded pathogens or deep foci.
