Penicillin allergy

Does this history prevent us from using a beta-lactam?

A label raises two separate questions: what caused the original reaction, and whether the antibiotic needed today is likely to cause the same reaction. Sometimes the second question can be answered before the first.

Resident core / 01

The reaction history determines the next step

A 52-year-old with a susceptible bacterial infection has an amoxicillin allergy label. At age 8, a rash appeared several days into treatment. There were no mouth sores, blisters, breathing symptoms, or hospitalization. The patient has avoided penicillins since then.

The record says “rash.” What can the patient recall about its appearance, its timing after a dose, and how it was treated? Is there a reliable record of taking the culprit or another beta-lactam since?

About 10% of U.S. patients report a penicillin allergy, but most do not have allergy confirmed after evaluation. Confirmation rates vary with the setting and patient selection. Inaccurate labels, infection-associated rashes, and loss of sensitization over time contribute to the discrepancy. The history still matters. A label of "rash" is not enough information. [1] [2]

An inaccurate label often leads to broader or less effective therapy. That is associated with more adverse drug effects, C. difficile infection, resistant organisms, longer hospitalization, and higher cost. An accurate history can preserve access to an effective antibiotic while keeping a serious past reaction visible in the record. [2]

Resident core / 03

PEN-FAST estimates test positivity; phenotype determines whether the score applies

First consider blistering or mucosal injury, systemic illness with rash, or drug-induced organ or blood-cell injury. Those histories fall outside routine direct challenge, even if the arithmetic gives a low score. A point estimate is not an estimate of this patient’s risk of anaphylaxis. [1] [5]

Among Melbourne patients scoring below 3, 96.3% had negative allergy testing. That proportion ranged from 84.9% to 98.4% in the external cohorts. A low score therefore helps identify adults for further assessment; it neither removes a label nor establishes that a challenge is safe. A remote severe cutaneous reaction can score below 3. Isolated drug fever, serum sickness, interstitial nephritis, and drug-induced liver injury were excluded from the study. Open the PEN-FAST calculator. [5]

Resident core / 04

Testing, challenge, and desensitization answer different questions

Skin testing looks for sensitization. A challenge tests tolerance of a particular drug. Desensitization makes a necessary course possible despite suspected or established allergy.
ApproachPurposeClinical considerations
Immediate-reading penicillin skin testingValidated reagents and controls assess immediate IgE sensitization. In a compatible history, a negative skin test generally leads to an observed amoxicillin challenge to confirm tolerance. A negative skin test by itself is not the same as tolerating the drug.Most useful after anaphylaxis or a recent suspected IgE-mediated reaction. A negative immediate-reading test does not establish safety after SJS/TEN, DRESS, AGEP, interstitial nephritis, serum sickness, hemolytic anemia, or other serious non-IgE reactions. Specialist delayed skin or patch testing is a different evaluation; it does not authorize routine culprit challenge after a severe reaction. [2] [4] [12]
Observed drug challengeTests current tolerance of the selected drug. “Direct” means preceding skin testing is omitted in an appropriately selected patient. A graded challenge uses more than one step; neither approach induces temporary tolerance.For a low-risk history, observation by trained staff and immediately available rescue treatment are part of a standardized challenge. A severe delayed reaction or drug-induced organ or blood-cell injury excludes routine direct challenge. [1]
DesensitizationCreates temporary tolerance when a particular drug is necessary and immediate allergy is established or strongly suspected. Tolerating the course under desensitization does not demonstrate that the original label was wrong.Requires a supervised protocol and uninterrupted dosing; it does not remove the allergy label. SJS/TEN, DRESS, AGEP, immune cytopenias, and serum sickness are contraindications to the usual immediate-allergy desensitization pathway. Serious drug-induced organ injury also requires avoidance and specialist assessment. [4] [9]

Consider Allergy/Immunology when the label is forcing inferior therapy, when an immunocompromised patient is likely to need repeated antibiotics, or when multiple antimicrobial allergy labels substantially limit treatment. [2] [4]

Resident core / 05

A penicillin allergy does not automatically exclude cefazolin

Related teaching on beta-lactam spectrum and agent selection.

There is no single cross-reactivity rate for every penicillin/cephalosporin pair. Risk depends on confirmed versus unverified allergy and R1 similarity. Cefazolin has very low cross-reactivity. With confirmed aminopenicillin allergy, assess any aminocephalosporin with a shared R1 side chain separately. [4]

With an unverified, nonanaphylactic penicillin history and no severe delayed features, cephalosporins can generally be given without preceding testing or extra allergy precautions. After penicillin anaphylaxis, the 2022 parameter conditionally supports a cephalosporin with a dissimilar R1 side chain, such as cefazolin, without preceding testing or extra precautions. Neither situation clears an independent cefazolin allergy. For a carbapenem, a history of immediate penicillin or cephalosporin allergy, including anaphylaxis, likewise does not itself require preceding testing or extra precautions. Severe delayed skin reactions or drug-induced organ injury are a separate problem; these recommendations do not establish safety in those patients. [4]

The opening patient’s remote, uncomplicated rash makes a supervised direct amoxicillin challenge a reasonable option if the full history remains low risk. If cefazolin is the appropriate antibiotic now, selecting it need not wait for an amoxicillin challenge. Tolerating cefazolin would document cefazolin tolerance; it would not resolve the original amoxicillin label. [4] [6]

Read the important pairings

Amoxicillin shares R1 with cefadroxil and cefprozil; ampicillin shares R1 with cephalexin and cefaclor. These two amino side-chain groups are related, which is why amoxicillin–cephalexin is marked S rather than I. Ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, cefpodoxime, and cefepime share R1; cefuroxime has related methoxyimino features. Ceftazidime and aztreonam share R1. Their comparisons with the five methoxyimino cephalosporins are marked S for partial structural resemblance, not an identical side chain or a uniform clinical risk. The DGAKI structural discussion describes occasional ceftazidime cross-reactivity within this group. Aztreonam's S cells extrapolate the structural comparison from its identical R1 with ceftazidime; they do not establish clinical cross-reactivity for each pair. Published charts disagree about some related pairs. [4] [8] [11]

Cefazolin and carbapenems

Cefazolin has no shared R1 side chain with the other drugs shown. A penicillin allergy alone does not establish cefazolin allergy. For meropenem (†), the 2022 parameter permits a carbapenem in patients with penicillin or cephalosporin allergy, including prior anaphylaxis, without routine preceding testing or additional precautions. That recommendation does not establish tolerance in someone with a carbapenem allergy or a severe delayed reaction. The aztreonam–meropenem cell is outside that specific recommendation. For immediate penicillin or cephalosporin allergy, the same parameter permits aztreonam without preceding testing except when the history involves ceftazidime allergy. The S cells do not override that clinical recommendation or clear a prior aztreonam allergy or severe delayed reaction. [4]

Anaphylaxis to a cephalosporin differs from anaphylaxis to penicillin. After cephalosporin anaphylaxis, the 2022 practice parameter conditionally suggests confirming a negative cephalosporin skin test before giving a parenteral cephalosporin with a nonidentical R1 side chain. A negative test alone does not prove tolerance. Do not use this chart to justify an unsupervised challenge after anaphylaxis or a routine culprit challenge after severe cutaneous or organ injury. Resident core / 07

Document what was tested and what the result means

See how a cleared amoxicillin allergy label affects H. pylori regimen selection.

When the completed evaluation rules out the suspected allergy, remove that label from the allergy list and explain the result. Tolerance of a different beta-lactam is recorded separately. Useful documentation includes the original reaction, the drug and dose administered during evaluation, the date, the observation period, and the outcome. The patient and the next clinician need to know which drug was tolerated and which label changed. Absence of immediate symptoms does not rule out a later rash. [2] [4]

If the patient instead recalls blistering and oral ulcers, the history now raises concern for a severe delayed reaction. The time elapsed does not make a routine test dose appropriate. Avoid the suspected culprit and seek specialist assessment; a low PEN-FAST score cannot clear this history. [1] [5]

Apply this on the next consult

In the original case, a remote, uncomplicated childhood rash may support a supervised amoxicillin challenge after assessment. In the blistering and oral-ulcer variant, routine challenge is inappropriate; the suspected culprit should be avoided pending specialist assessment. If an appropriate evaluation clears amoxicillin, remove that label and communicate exactly what was tested. Tolerance of cefazolin alone does not clear amoxicillin.

For syphilis in pregnancy, penicillin G is the only proven treatment for fetal infection and prevention of congenital syphilis. A reported penicillin allergy warrants prompt allergy assessment. Give stage-appropriate penicillin G once allergy is excluded, or after supervised desensitization when indicated. Severe delayed reactions require specialist assessment outside routine testing or challenge. Allergy guidance · Syphilis guidance

Fellow detail / Optional

Know what the validation studies did and did not establish

PEN-FAST estimates positive allergy testing, not an individual's risk of anaphylaxis. In the 622-person Melbourne derivation/internal-validation cohort, 17 of 460 people with scores below 3 had positive testing, giving a negative predictive value of 96.3% (95% CI, 94.1–97.8%). The three external cohorts included 945 people; their negative predictive values ranged from 84.9% to 98.4%. The score bands above are derivation estimates, not fixed risks in every referral population. [5]

A later Singapore referral-clinic validation, published online in 2025 and in a 2026 issue, found positive testing in 16 of 215 patients with PEN-FAST below 3: negative predictive value 92.6% (95% CI, 89.2–94.9%). Those reactions included positive skin testing, urticaria, and mild delayed rashes. This retrospective study reinforces the importance of phenotype and setting; it does not establish a new universal cutoff. [10]

PALACE randomized 382 adults at six specialist outpatient centers and analyzed 377: 187 assigned to direct oral challenge and 190 to skin testing followed by challenge. Physician-verified immune-mediated reactions within one hour occurred in 1/187 versus 1/190. The risk difference was 0.0084 percentage points (90% CI, −1.22 to 1.24), meeting the prespecified 5-percentage-point noninferiority margin. The trial used a 60-minute observation and day-5 follow-up to measure immediate and delayed outcomes. These were study procedures, not a universal observation or follow-up schedule. Delayed rash or urticaria occurred in 6/187 versus 3/190; no serious adverse events were reported. The sample does not exclude rare severe reactions. [6]

PALACE enrolled adults with PEN-FAST below 3, but more than 94% in each arm scored 0 or 1. Key exclusions included a history of anaphylaxis with any drug, severe delayed skin or organ reactions, chronic spontaneous urticaria, mast cell disease, and pregnancy. These are limits of this trial, not a claim that every excluded group can never undergo specialist allergy evaluation. The results support a supervised pathway in carefully selected low-risk adults; they do not support bypassing phenotype review or giving an unmonitored test dose. [6]

Side-chain charts are structural aids, not outcome calculators. Published charts disagree about some partial similarities. Useful recognized pairings include amoxicillin with cefadroxil, ampicillin with cephalexin or cefaclor, and ceftazidime with aztreonam. Cefazolin has no shared R1 side chain with the other drugs shown. The reaction phenotype remains essential even when a cell is unmarked. [4] [8]

When the preferred drug is being withheld because of an allergy label, the consultant should state whether the concern is immediate allergy, severe delayed hypersensitivity, side-chain cross-reactivity, or simple uncertainty. That distinction determines whether the next step is routine administration, supervised challenge, skin testing, desensitization, or avoidance.
