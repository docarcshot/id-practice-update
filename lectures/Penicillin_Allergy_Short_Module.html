Resident core / 01

The reaction history determines the next step

A 52-year-old with a susceptible bacterial infection has an amoxicillin allergy label. At age 8, a rash appeared several days into treatment. There were no mouth sores, blisters, breathing symptoms, or hospitalization. The patient has avoided penicillins since then.

What was the drug, when did the reaction start, what actually happened, was treatment required, and which beta-lactams have been tolerated since?

About 10% of U.S. patients report a penicillin allergy, but most do not have allergy confirmed after evaluation. Confirmation rates vary with the setting and patient selection. Inaccurate labels, infection-associated rashes, and loss of sensitization over time contribute to the discrepancy. The history still matters. A label of "rash" is not enough information. [1] [2]

An inaccurate label often leads to broader or less effective therapy. That is associated with more adverse drug effects, C. difficile infection, resistant organisms, longer hospitalization, and higher cost. The goal is not to make the label disappear. The goal is to give the best antibiotic safely. [2]

The classic Gell-Coombs categories help organize the phenotype, but real reactions do not always fit neatly into one box. Ask specifically about mucosal injury, skin peeling, facial edema, eosinophilia, kidney or liver injury, cytopenias, treatment required, and subsequent beta-lactam tolerance. [1] [4]

Resident core / 03

Use PEN-FAST only after excluding a severe delayed reaction

PEN-FAST estimates the probability of a positive penicillin-allergy test in adults.
CriterionPoints

ScoreDerivation-cohort estimate of positive testing
0Very low, less than 1%

A score below 3 identified a lower-risk group in the original Melbourne derivation/internal-validation cohort, with a negative predictive value of 96.3%. External-cohort values ranged from 84.9% to 98.4%; predictive values depend on the population tested. The score does not remove the allergy label or independently authorize a challenge. A remote severe cutaneous reaction may still produce a low score. Isolated drug fever, serum sickness, interstitial nephritis, and drug-induced liver injury were excluded from the original study; do not use a low score to clear these histories. Resident core / 04

Testing, challenge, and desensitization answer different questions

These procedures are not interchangeable. Follow the applicable institutional protocol.
ApproachPurposeClinical considerations
Immediate-reading penicillin skin testingAssesses immediate IgE sensitization with validated reagents and controls, commonly including major determinant reagent and penicillin G. Use the Allergy service's protocol for the full reagent panel. In an appropriate phenotype, a negative test is generally followed by an observed amoxicillin challenge.Most useful after anaphylaxis or a recent suspected IgE-mediated reaction. A negative immediate-reading test does not establish safety after SJS/TEN, DRESS, AGEP, interstitial nephritis, serum sickness, hemolytic anemia, or other serious non-IgE reactions. Specialist delayed skin or patch testing is a different evaluation; it does not authorize routine culprit challenge after a severe reaction. [2] [4] [12]
Graded or test-dose challengeTests whether a selected low-risk patient tolerates the drug. It does not induce temporary tolerance.Use a standardized protocol for an appropriately selected low-risk patient, with direct observation, trained staff and rescue treatment immediately available. Severe delayed reactions or drug-induced organ/blood-cell injury exclude routine direct challenge. [1]
DesensitizationCreates temporary tolerance when the preferred drug is necessary and immediate allergy is established or strongly suspected.Requires a supervised protocol and uninterrupted dosing; it does not remove the allergy label. SJS/TEN, DRESS, AGEP, immune cytopenias, and serum sickness are contraindications to the usual immediate-allergy desensitization pathway. Serious drug-induced organ injury also requires avoidance and specialist assessment. [4] [9]

Consider Allergy/Immunology when the label is forcing inferior therapy, when an immunocompromised patient is likely to need repeated antibiotics, or when multiple antimicrobial allergy labels substantially limit treatment. [2] [4]

Resident core / 05

A penicillin allergy does not automatically exclude cefazolin

Beta-lactam spectrum and agent selection

There is no single cross-reactivity rate for every penicillin/cephalosporin pair. Risk depends on confirmed versus unverified allergy and R1 similarity. Cefazolin has very low cross-reactivity; aminocephalosporins sharing an aminopenicillin side chain need separate assessment. [4]

For an unverified, nonanaphylactic penicillin allergy without a severe delayed phenotype, the 2022 practice parameter supports routine cephalosporin administration without preceding testing or added precautions. It also supports a structurally dissimilar cephalosporin such as cefazolin after penicillin anaphylaxis. This does not clear a cefazolin allergy or make cefazolin appropriate after a severe delayed beta-lactam reaction. Carbapenem cross-reactivity is very low; the same parameter supports carbapenem administration after penicillin or cephalosporin allergy, including anaphylaxis, without routine preceding testing or added precautions. This immediate-allergy recommendation does not authorize carbapenem use after a severe delayed beta-lactam reaction or serious drug-induced organ injury; obtain specialist assessment for those histories. [4]

For the opening case, a remote benign rash may qualify for supervised direct amoxicillin challenge under the current pathway. If a cephalosporin is the appropriate treatment, the penicillin label alone need not delay its use. Choosing cefazolin and removing a penicillin label are separate decisions.

Read the important pairings

Amoxicillin shares R1 with cefadroxil and cefprozil; ampicillin shares R1 with cephalexin and cefaclor. These two amino side-chain groups are related, which is why amoxicillin–cephalexin is marked S rather than I. Ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, cefpodoxime, and cefepime share R1; cefuroxime has related methoxyimino features. Ceftazidime and aztreonam share R1. Their comparisons with the five methoxyimino cephalosporins are marked S for partial structural resemblance, not an identical side chain or a uniform clinical risk. The DGAKI structural discussion describes occasional ceftazidime cross-reactivity within this group. Aztreonam's S cells extrapolate the structural comparison from its identical R1 with ceftazidime; they do not establish clinical cross-reactivity for each pair. Published charts disagree about some related pairs. [4] [8] [11]

Cefazolin and carbapenems

Cefazolin has no shared R1 side chain with the other drugs shown. A penicillin allergy alone does not establish cefazolin allergy. For meropenem (†), the 2022 parameter permits a carbapenem in patients with penicillin or cephalosporin allergy, including prior anaphylaxis, without routine preceding testing or additional precautions. That recommendation does not establish tolerance in someone with a carbapenem allergy or a severe delayed reaction. The aztreonam–meropenem cell is outside that specific recommendation. For immediate penicillin or cephalosporin allergy, the same parameter permits aztreonam without preceding testing except when the history involves ceftazidime allergy. The S cells do not override that clinical recommendation or clear a prior aztreonam allergy or severe delayed reaction. [4]

Do not use this chart to select an unsupervised challenge after anaphylaxis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis, DRESS, or serious immune-mediated organ injury. A history of cephalosporin anaphylaxis requires a separate evaluation even when the proposed cephalosporin has a different R1 side chain. [4]

This is a teaching matrix, not a complete allergy clearance tool. It does not map R2 side chains, beta-lactamase inhibitor allergy, or other penicillins such as penicillin G/V, oxacillin, and piperacillin. Within-penicillin cross-reactivity can involve shared penicillin determinants. This matrix highlights selected R1 relationships, not every molecular resemblance. Published charts disagree about the clinical importance of partial similarities, including aztreonam–ceftriaxone. Neither S nor an unmarked cell supplies a patient-specific probability: use the phenotype and the clinical recommendations above. [4] [8]

Resident core / 07

Document what was tested and what the result means

H. pylori regimen selection when amoxicillin can be used

After a successful evaluation, update the allergy list as well as the note. Record the culprit history, drug and dose administered, date, observation period, and outcome. Document beta-lactams that were tolerated. Explain the result to the patient and communicate it to the next clinician. An uneventful immediate observation does not guarantee that no delayed rash will occur. [2] [4]

If the same patient instead recalls blistering and oral ulcers, stop. A remote severe reaction is still a severe reaction. Avoid the culprit and obtain specialist guidance rather than using a low PEN-FAST score or a routine test dose.

Fellow detail / Optional

Know what the validation studies did and did not establish

PEN-FAST estimates positive allergy testing, not an individual's risk of anaphylaxis. In the 622-person Melbourne derivation/internal-validation cohort, 17 of 460 people with scores below 3 had positive testing, giving a negative predictive value of 96.3% (95% CI, 94.1–97.8%). The three external cohorts included 945 people; their negative predictive values ranged from 84.9% to 98.4%. The score bands above are derivation estimates, not fixed risks in every referral population. [5]

A later Singapore referral-clinic validation, published online in 2025 and in a 2026 issue, found positive testing in 16 of 215 patients with PEN-FAST below 3: negative predictive value 92.6% (95% CI, 89.2–94.9%). Those reactions included positive skin testing, urticaria, and mild delayed rashes. This retrospective study reinforces the importance of phenotype and setting; it does not establish a new universal cutoff. [10]

PALACE randomized 382 adults at six specialist outpatient centers and analyzed 377: 187 assigned to direct oral challenge and 190 to skin testing followed by challenge. Physician-verified immune-mediated reactions within one hour occurred in 1/187 versus 1/190. The risk difference was 0.0084 percentage points (90% CI, −1.22 to 1.24), meeting the prespecified 5-percentage-point noninferiority margin. Participants were observed for 60 minutes and contacted on day 5. Delayed rash or urticaria occurred in 6/187 versus 3/190; no serious adverse events were reported. The sample does not exclude rare severe reactions. [6]

PALACE enrolled adults with PEN-FAST below 3, but more than 94% in each arm scored 0 or 1. It excluded drug-associated anaphylaxis, severe delayed skin or organ reactions, chronic spontaneous urticaria, mast cell disease, and pregnancy. These are limits of this trial, not a claim that every excluded group can never undergo specialist allergy evaluation. The results support a supervised pathway in carefully selected low-risk adults; they do not support bypassing phenotype review or giving an unmonitored test dose. [6]

Side-chain charts are structural aids, not outcome calculators. Published charts disagree about some partial similarities. Useful recognized pairings include amoxicillin with cefadroxil, ampicillin with cephalexin or cefaclor, and ceftazidime with aztreonam. Cefazolin has no shared R1 side chain with the other drugs shown. The reaction phenotype remains essential even when a cell is unmarked. [4] [8]

When the preferred drug is being withheld because of an allergy label, the consultant should state whether the concern is immediate allergy, severe delayed hypersensitivity, side-chain cross-reactivity, or simple uncertainty. That distinction determines whether the next step is routine administration, supervised challenge, skin testing, desensitization, or avoidance.
