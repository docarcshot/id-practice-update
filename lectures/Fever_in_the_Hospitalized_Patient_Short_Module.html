Fever in the hospitalized patient

What should change when a hospitalized patient develops fever?

Assess urgency, choose a focused workup, and reconsider both infectious and noninfectious causes.

Resident core / 01

Assess the patient before expanding the antibiotic regimen

A 67-year-old develops a temperature of 38.6 C on hospital day 6 while receiving antibiotics for an abdominal infection. Blood pressure and oxygen requirements are unchanged. A drain and urinary catheter remain in place.

Discuss before reading on. Which findings would require immediate treatment? What would you review before changing antibiotics?

Confirm the temperature and measurement method. Assess blood pressure, oxygenation, mental status, perfusion, and urine output. Reconstruct the timeline of symptoms, procedures, devices, medications, and prior cultures. This module addresses new or persistent inpatient fever, not the full evaluation of prolonged FUO. [1]

Resident core / 02

The urgency depends on sepsis probability and organ dysfunction

Antibiotic timing follows the 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidance.
Clinical assessmentNext action
Possible septic shock or probable/definite sepsisBegin appropriate antimicrobials immediately, ideally within 1 hour of recognition, while resuscitating and evaluating the source.
Possible sepsis without shockConduct a rapid, time-limited evaluation. If concern for infection persists, administer antimicrobials within 3 hours of first suspecting sepsis.
Low likelihood of infection and no shockClose observation with reassessment can be appropriate. A temperature elevation alone does not require broader antibiotics.

Collect indicated cultures before antibiotics if this will not materially delay treatment. Do not wait for a procalcitonin result to treat probable sepsis. [2]

Neutropenic fever requires its own urgent empiric treatment pathway. Profound immunosuppression, suspected meningitis, and other time-sensitive syndromes need immediate senior assessment; routine ward observation rules do not apply. [3]

Resident core / 03

Use the history and examination to choose tests

Avoid a standing order to culture every body site with each temperature spike.
Finding or concernFocused evaluation
Bacteremia or a central-line source is plausibleObtain blood cultures; use paired peripheral and line samples when evaluating a catheter source. Document collection sites.
New cough, hypoxemia, or respiratory findingsChest imaging; respiratory testing guided by the syndrome and exposures. ICU guidance recommends a chest radiograph for new fever.
Urinary findings or unexplained sepsisEvaluate for UTI in context. A catheter or pyuria alone does not establish a urinary source.
New unexplained diarrheaConsider C. difficile testing when there are at least 3 unformed stools in 24 hours and no better explanation, such as laxatives.
Abdominal pain, ileus, abnormal drainage, or recent surgeryExamine the abdomen and wound, review source control, and discuss targeted imaging with the procedural team.
[1] [5]

For suspected intra-abdominal abscess in a nonpregnant adult, CT is the preferred initial modality in the 2024 IDSA update. Contrast use depends on the clinical question and patient factors. [4]

Testing formed stool or repeating a C. difficile test to document cure is not appropriate. [5]

Resident core / 04

Persistent fever can reflect a new source or a noninfectious process

A differential should lead to a specific reassessment, not an indiscriminate panel.
PossibilityQuestion to ask at the bedside
Uncontrolled or newly acquired infectionIs a collection undrained, a device infected, or a new syndrome developing?
Medication reactionDid fever begin after a new drug? Is there rash, eosinophilia, or organ injury? Their absence does not exclude drug fever.
Thrombosis or pulmonary embolismAre there compatible symptoms or signs that justify directed testing?
Postoperative inflammation or tissue injuryDoes the course fit the procedure, and is the patient improving? Do not use postoperative timing alone to exclude infection.
Transfusion reaction or other inflammatory diseaseDoes the timing or a new examination finding point away from infection?
[1] [6]

Review the entire medication administration record, including new non-antibiotics. Drug fever is an exclusion diagnosis; rash with organ injury needs evaluation for a serious drug reaction. [1] [6]

Resident core / 05

Reassessment should produce a specific plan

For the opening case, review the original diagnosis, cultures, antibiotic activity and dosing, drain output, and most recent imaging. If an abdominal source remains plausible, reassess drainage with surgery or interventional radiology. A broader drug does not replace drainage of an infected collection. [2] [4]

If the patient deteriorates or a new syndrome emerges, treat accordingly. If improving and no new evidence supports infection, avoid broadening solely for persistent fever; reassess whether current antibiotics remain indicated. [2]

Example handoff. "New fever without current hemodynamic or respiratory deterioration. Reviewing the abdominal source and drain with the procedural team. Reassess immediately for hypotension, increased oxygen need, or altered mentation."

Resident core / Close

Apply this on the next consult

Assess stability and immune status, identify the likely source, and choose tests that can change management. State what you will do now and which clinical changes will trigger escalation.

Fellow discussion / Optional

Know which recommendations come from ICU evidence

The 2023 SCCM/IDSA guideline concerns new fever in adult ICU patients without severe immunocompromise. Many recommendations are conditional or best-practice statements. The ward approach here is a clinical adaptation; routine ICU testing should not become a universal ward order set. [1]

When fever remains unexplained, repeat the history and examination before pursuing advanced imaging or broad serologies. The guideline conditionally suggests FDG-PET/CT in selected critically ill patients after unrevealing investigations when transport is acceptable. This is not a first-line test for an isolated ward fever. [1]

Discussion variation. The temperature is unchanged, but the patient now has hypotension and confusion. Explain how the urgency changes even before the source is known. [2]
